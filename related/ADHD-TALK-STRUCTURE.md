## MYTH 1 · 1:30–4:30 · "Meditation is watching your breath and not getting distracted."

> That's the version most of us got. Sit still, watch the breath, when you drift, come back. Every day.
>
> What's actually going on: that's the beginner's drill from one tradition, and the tradition was nothing like the drill. Here's what Zen training actually looked like. The master walks the hall with a bamboo stick. You're sitting, you drift, you slump, and you get hit. First you're angry, and you can't do anything about it. Then you start to fear the stick. Every corner, every doorway: is he there? Then he leaves you alone for a month. You relax. That's when you get hit again. He's been doing this for twenty years. You cannot outguess him.
>
> Eventually something gives. You notice he isn't hitting you all day. It's a bamboo switch. It stings and it passes. And you stop bracing for it. You take it the way you take a mosquito. That was the lesson, and it took years: stop bracing against what's coming, because you never controlled it anyway. Acceptance, taught by a man with a stick who knew exactly what he was doing.
>
> Try putting that in an app. By today's standards it's assault. So the tradition got cleaned up until it fit on a phone, and what was left is the part that's easy to teach and easy to keep subscribed to. The breath, on its own. It's like learning a language and being handed only the alphabet. You can drill the alphabet for ten years. You'll say it beautifully. You still can't order a coffee. A piece of a method, without the method, stops being the method. A copy of a copy, and it suits the brain that's fine doing one thing over and over. Most brains are. That's why it spread.
>
> So we were handed one exercise and told it was the whole thing. And when it didn't work, we were told the problem was us.

**Anchor (FOUND, Alex 2026-09-22), in your words:** you also tried learning meditation online. It had some effect, cool effects even. But it never felt like "that's it." Say it flat, after "the problem was us." No app, no brand: you never used one.

## MYTH 2 · 4:30–6:30 · "If it bores you, your mind is weak. Keep going and it'll improve."

> I believed this one for years. Push through, and one day it clicks.
>
> What's actually going on: different brains need different practices at the start. Not forever. Over time a brain learns to work differently, and once it has, it stops caring what the practice is. Observe this, observe that: everything gets more and more interesting.
>
> So boredom at the beginning isn't a sign that it's the wrong practice. It's a sign that it's the wrong practice right now. Boredom is what we feel when we don't understand what we're doing, or why. That's a guidance problem, not a mind problem.
>
> Without guidance, here's the loop. You watch your breath, you get bored, you decide it's stupid, and it stops working. And the moment you call it boredom, the practice gets worse. It feeds itself.
>
> Which is why you can't force this, any more than you can force yourself to fall asleep. You set up conditions: guidance, and a practice that fits your brain today. We'll come back to that.
>
> And for the record: I still get bored for the first few minutes, every time. That's not the wrong practice. That's the warm-up.

Landing line on the slide: **Wrong practice. Right now.**

## MYTH 3 · 6:30–9:30 · "ADHD means you can't focus."

> This is the one the whole diagnosis is built on. The name says it: attention deficit. Something missing.
>
> What's actually going on: two things are true about that brain. One, it runs on novelty. New thing, full attention. Same thing again, attention gone. That's the reward wiring, not a character flaw. Two, it's called distractible. A door opens, a bird goes past, a chair creaks, and your attention goes with it. And that's where the name is wrong. Look at what's actually happening. Most brains never even receive the door and the bird. A filter drops them before they arrive. Yours receives everything. That's not a failure to focus. That's a brain that picks up more. Highly perceptive, and nobody handed it the controls. A wider aperture with no steering wheel.
>
> And everyone has this brain some of the time. After coffee. In a new city. When you're in love. You know exactly what I'm describing.
>
> One theory why it exists, and I like it. Go back far enough and we were all out looking for food. The brain that noticed the broken twig, the footprint, the sound behind you, kept everyone alive. Notice everything, love what's new: that was the best brain in the group. Then we built a world of nine-to-fives and textbooks and doing the same thing every day, and the same brain got a diagnosis. And here's the part nobody says out loud: as the world moved that way, meditation moved with it. It became one more sit-still-and-repeat task.
>
> Now put that brain in front of the breath exercise. Task one: repeat. Task two: ignore everything else. You've just asked it to do the two things it's worst at. Of course it left the room.
>
> Today is about the aperture. The steering wheel is a different talk. The day after tomorrow, in fact.

## MYTH 4 · 9:30–11:30 · "A good meditator has a still, empty mind."

> The picture in everyone's head: a monk, motionless, thinking nothing, for thirty years.
>
> What's actually going on: nobody could watch their breath for thirty years. What long-term practitioners do is closer to the anthill. They explore. Take a feeling, ask where it comes from, find something under it, ask again. There's always another layer, and nobody's found the bottom.
>
> That kind of practice needs exactly two things. A hunger for what's new, because every layer is new. And perception fine enough to notice what's under the last thing you noticed. Those are the two "problems" from myth three. In the boring version they disqualify you. In the real version they're the engine. The brain that receives everything gets to the next layer faster than the brain that filters. In this kind of practice, you're ahead.
>
> And the conditions from myth two: a task that fits the brain, no clock, and other people doing it next to you, because attention is contagious. Hold that. Two more myths. The one I hear most, then the biggest one.

## MYTH 5 · 11:30–13:30 · "I have too many thoughts. I'm doing it wrong."

> I hear this one most. And I said it most.
>
> What's actually going on: in a proper practice, thinking isn't the failure. It's the process. When the body and the mind start to let go, the tension comes out as thoughts. Plans, memories, arguments you never had. That's mental tension leaving. It's supposed to happen.
>
> The only job you have is this: when you notice you're thinking, you come back to the practice. That's it. Not fight the thought. Not analyse it. Not feel bad about it. Notice, return.
>
> And how often you have to come back, how many thoughts, how loud they are: none of it measures progress. Some days it's ten times a minute. Some days twice. Both are the practice working. The only real mistake is deciding it's a mistake, because then you're back in myth two: the story makes it worse.

Landing line on the slide: **Thoughts are the tension leaving. Notice, return.**

## MYTH 6 · 13:30–16:30 · "Meditation is the point. Meditate more and it comes."

> This is the one I believed longest. If I just sat more, it would come.
>
> What's actually going on: meditation is not what any spiritual tradition is about. Neither is yoga. Take yoga. What we got is a stretching class. In the original system the postures are one part of eight. The other seven are about how you live, how you breathe, and where your attention goes. And that's just one branch of it. There's a yoga of action, a yoga of devotion, a yoga of knowledge, a yoga of the mind. The postures were the warm-up for sitting still. We took the warm-up, built an industry on it, and called it yoga.
>
> Meditation went the same way. It's a tool inside something much bigger: a whole way of developing a human being. And each of those wholes was built for its time and its place. A monastery in Japan. A village in India two thousand years ago. Its language, its food, its daily life. The depth is real. But for someone in this room, in 2026, with a laptop and a flight next week, getting full access to it is close to impossible, and most of it wouldn't be relevant anyway.
>
> So what reaches us is the tool without the whole. And here's what happens to a tool without guidance, nearly every time: it turns into something that makes you feel better. That's all. A healthier way to get through the day. Some people have a drink after work. The more mindful ones sit for twenty minutes. Same job. I did that for years, and I'm not knocking it. It's better than the drink.
>
> But it never touches the root. It doesn't teach you to actually take charge of your attention, and it doesn't deal with what's going on inside you. It manages the pressure. It doesn't solve where the pressure comes from.
>
> Guidance means someone who has walked it, telling you the next step, and checking. That isn't a talk. That's the Saturday workshop.
>
> The goal was never meditation. The goal is development: a person who sees more, reacts less, and can do more. Meditation is one tool for that. A good one. Not the main thing, and not something you do and then wait.
>
> That's why Anna and I chose the method we work in. Twenty years, two centres. Our teacher took a whole method of human development and rebuilt it for people like us: step by step, in plain language, practical, something you can do inside a normal life. That's the part I'll go into on Friday.
>
> Here's what changes when a person chooses development, even a little, so you know what to look for. In a hard situation you see more options. A reaction that used to last days lasts hours, then minutes. You have the conversation you've been avoiding. And people start asking what happened to your face. From the outside: calmer, but warm. People say you're steadying to be around.

Landing line on the slide: **Meditation is a tool. Development is the goal.**

## PRACTICE · 16:30–21:30 · listening, then listening with the whole body

The evidence. Four myths busted with words is still just words. This is where the room finds out. The practice is built on the exact trait from myth three: every sound that was a distraction in the old exercise is the material here.

Frame it in one breath:

> So here's one tool. Not the thing. One that fits this brain. And if you find yourself thinking in it: notice, return. That's the practice, not the failure. Everything that would have pulled you off the breath, the door, the chair, someone's stomach, is the practice now. The goal is not to finish. The goal is to find one thing you hadn't noticed before. If you get bored, you've slipped back into the old version. Go smaller.
>
> This is the whole of what we call stillness: attention on what's actually there, not on a task.

PLANT: the big talk says "the thing you did two days ago, slowed down to an hour."

**Part one · 2 min · listening**

> Eyes closed, or soften your gaze to the floor. Don't pick a sound. Let them come to you. How many different sounds can you find? Now the farthest one. Now the quietest one. Now the one underneath the one you just found.

Say each cue once, then leave 20–30 seconds of silence after it.

**Part two · 2–3 min · listening with the whole body**

> Now stop listening with your ears. Listen with the whole body. Where does each sound land? The low ones, the sharp ones. Notice the body reacting before you've named the sound. Find the sound you can feel but can't hear.

This is "one layer down." Say it once as the switch, then stop talking.

**After · 30 sec** silence. Then:

> What did you find?

Two or three people, one sentence each. Don't comment on the answers. Just take them.

> Then: Notice who found the most just now. That's the superpower. It was never missing.

**your slot** — how you open your eyes and come back. Yours, not mine.

## What kills it

- Saying "you have a gift". Traits, not gifts. The mechanism does the flattering.
- Busting a myth without saying who believed it. Every myth gets an "I believed this too" or it reads as preaching.
- Explaining the practice longer than doing it.
- Skipping "What did you find?". Without it you have a lecture.
- The school's vocabulary. Mechanism, not words.
- If asked what the steering wheel is: "the part of you that decides where the aperture points. Friday."
