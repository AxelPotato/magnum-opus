Why letting go is a high-agency skill — talk structure (Network School)

Title chosen 2026-09-25. Working name: Rational Spirituality. Event image: event_which.png (the four rooms, “Which one are you?”).

Rebuilt 2026-09-25 evening from the live recording (live-transcript.md, 58 min with questions). The order below is the order the talk actually took. The planned METHOD section and the child-in-the-dark story were not said; they sit in Appendix A for a future version. Beats and key lines, not a script. [YOUR SLOT] = the aside is yours. Every slide switch is marked ▶ SLIDE.

The claim of the whole talk in one sentence: a person develops along two independent axes, letting go and taking action, and almost everyone works on only one of them. Where the two meet is a high-agency life.

Vocabulary, fixed: the x-axis is action (things happen to you → you make things happen). The y-axis is letting go (braced → released; you said it live: not relaxed, released, free from your internal limitations). Agency is not an axis; it's what you get when both are high. Two kinds: outer agency (doing things in the physical world) and inner agency (admit you were wrong, say sorry for real, take the first step in a conflict). Outer agency lives on the x-axis. Inner agency is how you climb the y-axis on purpose.

Live length: 58 minutes including questions. Sections below carry the live clock. If you want 45, the cuts are at the end.

PART 1 · 0:00–4:10 · Letting go (the y-axis)

▶ SLIDE 00 · title · up as people sit down · one line: "this is a new way for me to explain what we do, so feedback welcome"
▶ SLIDE 01 · the y-axis · on screen while you explain it
  • Two axes of development. Start with the one less spoken about. Letting go is the ability to be at ease with yourself. To show your inner world to other people. To know how much you believe your own fears, how much the ideas in your head run you, how much other people's opinions move you. Your internal resilience. And the caring, supportive part of you, because that only comes out when you're not braced.
  • The ideas in our head are a source of tension, and the tension lives in the body. It affects health. And it affects how effective you are, not just how nice. Tension is a tax on your ability to see solutions. With enough fears and doubts you never even look at the unusual solution; you take the default one already in your head.
  • So: the more we let go, the more of life we see, feel and express.
  • Credit Jonny Miller, "How to Unclench". He talks for forty minutes; you're doing the general version. Then the line that landed: "the entire talk is one big graph, drawn on this board. Congratulations. This is the most rational approach to spirituality I've ever tried."
  • Clear up the confusion before the rooms. Progress on this axis gets associated with meditation. Partly true. But mostly it's the thing nobody wants to do: admit you were wrong in a situation and take the first step to reconcile it. Say sorry for real. Have the hard conversation with the person you don't want to hurt. Feel the "I don't want to" and step anyway. That's how this axis actually moves. Meditation matters; this is the part that's usually missing. (This is inner agency; it gets its name in a minute.)
  • Optional, not said live: the fist demo. Ten seconds, everyone clenches, tries to feel glad. Use it if the room is cold.
  • "Any questions on this one?" Then move.

PART 2 · 4:10–5:40 · Action (the x-axis)

▶ SLIDE 02 · the x-axis · on screen while you explain it
  • Action is simple. How much you hold the steering wheel of your life. How much you make things happen versus how much things happen to you. How much of an initiator you are; how willing to put effort in and get stuff done.
  • The easiest test: how well am I able to follow up on my words? Talk versus walk. It doesn't need a loud personality; a very quiet person can do things that are very hard for others. It doesn't need a world-changing project. It's the direction you live in: you get stuff done.
  • Optional, not said live: Wilbur Wright, and the steering-wheel callback to Wednesday.

THE QUADRANT INTRO · 5:40–7:40 · two questions, then the word agency

▶ SLIDE 03 · both axes · empty rooms
  • The two together make a quadrant, and we'll live in it for the rest of the hour.
▶ SLIDE 03a · where are you? · two questions
  • Two questions locate you. One: how many things did I say I'd do, and then didn't? Two: how often am I the first person to take the first step in a conflict? Let them answer silently. That's the whole test; no belief required.
▶ SLIDE 03b · two kinds of agency
  • "Does everybody know the word agency?" Agency, in its purest form: the ability to express your being in this world. To live your potential. The top-right room is named after it, so define it now.
  • Two kinds. Outer agency, the familiar one: I do things in the physical world. Inner agency, the one people don't talk about: the sorry, the first step, the admission. Both axes are a way of truly expressing yourself, and effectively. That's the word.

Top-right · "High Agency" · released and active · 23:20–30:30

▶ SLIDE 07 · HIGH AGENCY
  • Both high, finally. You've let go of some of your limitations and you're actually doing things. The path of a person's evolution, the conscious version. Where you start truly expressing yourself. And it's turbulent.
  • The jail-cell test (Mack, credit him once): third-world country, a jail cell, you don't speak the language. Who do you call? That's the highest-agency person you know: capable, and cares enough to act.
  • Why it's turbulent: walking on two legs. In Driven you hop on the action leg. In Drifting you hop on the letting-go leg. Here every step swivels from one to the other until you find the centre: active and released at the same time. Not relaxed. Released. Free from your internal limitations.

The method (planned ten minutes; not said live)

This is the section the talk exists for. Stay rational: mechanism, not vocabulary. No school terms in English.

  • Name it plainly. A method of stillness. Twenty years, tens of thousands through it, two centres, 120 people living it full time. One breath on the facts, no more.
  • What stillness means here. Not relaxing. Attention turned inside the body, to the exact place that's braced, and staying there until it lets go on its own. The fist demo from minute one, slowed down to an hour. Callback: "Not repetition. Exploration. Myth four, if you were there on Wednesday. The listening you did is this, slowed down to an hour." That is the up-leg, trained on purpose, daily. And the practice doesn't stop on the cushion: what you find braced in stillness is usually a person, and the next step is inner agency: the call, the apology, the honest sentence. Stillness shows you where; inner agency does the moving.
▶ SLIDE 12 · two legs on the path
  • Why it isn't the island. In the school the practice is welded to activity. Morning stillness. Then real work with real people: kitchen, building, teaching, whatever the place needs. Then stillness again. Every day, both legs. The work shows you where you're still braced (the sting is the map), the stillness releases it, and the freed energy goes back into the work. That alternation is the shrinking swing. You never get to run to a corner for two years, because tomorrow morning is the other leg. Show slide 12.
  • What actually damps the swing, named:
    • A reference point. Once your body has fully felt released, even once, you feel the distance from it. You catch the overshoot at two out of ten instead of nine, not because you're disciplined but because you can feel it. This is the whole argument for the retreat; set it up here, cash it in below.
    • People further along. Calm is contagious like a yawn. You can't stay braced for long in a room of people who've let go. Alone, your resistance is all you've got.
    • Discipline and a guided method. A schedule that doesn't ask how you feel, and someone who has walked it telling you what the next step is. At first it's effort. Later it's ease.
  • Honest limit. At home, alone, twenty minutes a day, this is slow, and you can fool yourself for years. That's not a sales line; it's why the formats exist.
▶ SLIDE 13 · the reference point
  • The retreat, as a rational object. You can't aim at a state you've never felt. And be precise about where it takes you: not to the top of the y-axis. Into the top-right room. Days of stillness AND work, in a clean space, with a group, both legs at once, deeper into that room than most people ever go in a lifetime. You live there for several days. When you come back, you're changed. Not permanently; you don't get to keep the depth. But you've shifted in the right direction, and for the first time you know exactly what you're aiming at. The swing finally has something to shrink toward. Show slide 13.
  • The workshop, on the same scale. Four hours is one step into that room, not a stay. Enough to feel it move you, and to come back a little different. Say that honestly; nobody in this room trusts a four-hour transformation. What they'll trust is: you'll feel the direction, and you'll know whether you want the days.
  • Back to friend A. No slide change; slide 13 stays up. Don't retell it. One line: his retreat was exactly this move, out of the far corner toward the middle for a few days, and the climb afterwards kept the agency. Point at the red line into the ring and the green line out. This is what the retreat is for.
  • And it's not only him. (Site testimonial, anonymous; the pattern, not a person.) The shape we see most often from this corner: mental fog, anxiety, workaholism, a life on autopilot. After: inner quiet, a clear sense of direction, joy in small things, flow. Notice it's the same list as the next slide. That's not a coincidence; the "after" is what the y-axis looks like when the x-axis is kept.
▶ SLIDE 14 · what changes
  • "You will see the difference," made concrete. Show slide 14. Callback: "I gave you this list on Wednesday. Tonight: how." Four things, all observable: how many options you see in the next hard situation; how long a reaction lasts (days, then hours, then minutes); the conversation you finally have; people asking what happened to your face. Give them the two closing questions as the measure: answer today, answer again two weeks after.

The child in the dark (planned for MY GOAL; not said live)

  • The child in the dark. No slide; leave slide 14 up or go black. A child lies in a dark room and sees monsters. Not "imagines": sees. The fear is real, the heartbeat is real, the shapes are as solid as the furniture. Then someone turns on the light. Nothing there. The room was empty the whole time. Driven · Jim Carrey and Michael Phelps

  • Carrey, the line you already use, paraphrased: he wishes everyone could get rich and famous and get everything they ever dreamed of, so they'd see it isn't the answer.
  • Phelps. The most decorated Olympian in history: 28 medals, 23 gold, over four Games (2004–2016). And after every Games, by his own account, a crash. He has described it as post-Olympic depression that hit each time the goal disappeared. 2004: a drink-driving arrest at 19. 2008: eight golds in Beijing, then the tabloid photo months later. 2012: London, announced his retirement, and the deepest fall: a second drink-driving arrest in September 2014, then days alone in his bedroom, not eating, barely sleeping, and, in his words, not wanting to be alive. In October 2014 he checked into a treatment centre in Arizona for about six weeks. What he says he learned there is the exact content of this talk: he had thought of himself as a swimmer, not as a person; he had never talked about what he felt; he learned it was okay to not be okay, and to say so. Then the part that matters for the chart: he came back. Rio 2016, five golds and a silver, and he calls those his happiest Games, because for the first time the swimming wasn't holding up his whole life. And then, honestly: he did not stay happy. He retired for good, married, four sons, and the depression didn't leave. He's described a scary spell a couple of years after Rio, meltdowns behind closed doors while smiling in public. Since then: the Michael Phelps Foundation, the documentary "The Weight of Gold" (2020), where he estimates most Olympians go through post-Games depression and nobody talked about it, and years of advocacy. Use: he is Jim Carrey's line lived out, and then exactly the school's claim: not a cure, a smaller swing, caught earlier, with help. High Agency · Gandhi and Marcus Aurelius

  • Gandhi, as before: led a country to independence and kept a weekly day of silence while doing it.
  • Marcus Aurelius. Emperor of Rome for nineteen years (161–180), which is about as much outer agency as a human has ever held. Plague across the empire, a long war on the Danube, a general who declared himself emperor behind his back, and most of his children dead before him. At night, in a tent on campaign, he wrote notes to himself in Greek that he never meant anyone to read; we call them the Meditations. They are not philosophy from an armchair. They're a man with the largest to-do list on earth training himself to stay present, do the task in front of him, not be run by other people's opinions, and not add his own reaction to what happened. When the usurper was killed, the record has him grieving that he'd lost the chance to pardon the man: inner agency, at the top of the world. One line of his, in a common translation: "What stands in the way becomes the way." (Meditations 5.20.) Use: presence and action in the same person, two thousand years before anyone called it flow.
