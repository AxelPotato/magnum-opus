# Three levels of belief

Context material for future work. It is not part of the essay, and no essay part was changed for it. The idea is Alex's, told in his own words on 2026-10-14 and formalized here with his examples and his meaning kept. In each section his own examples come first. The further ones were added at his request the same day, as illustrations in the same spirit.

People hold beliefs on three levels. The levels differ in how easily a belief can be looked at, questioned and changed.

## Regular beliefs

The first level is the ideas we all carry around and think are right. An average healthy adult can look at these ideas critically and change them when the circumstances are right, and this is even more true of someone who has done some inner work. Which car brand is the best is a good example. So are Apple against Android and strong preferences in many other fields. Such opinions can sit very close to the heart, and still most people are willing to change them when they meet a good enough reason.

More examples of the same kind:

- Tea or coffee, and the best way to make either one. A friend shows a method that tastes better and the old one is gone by the next morning.
- Mac or Windows, or the phone someone has used for ten years. A new job or a better device is often enough to move a person across.
- Mornings or evenings for a workout, and the right way to load a dishwasher. People will argue with energy and then update after one good counterexample.
- The best film of all time, the best city to live in, the best season of the year. These are strong feelings that stay open to a friend saying "have you tried this?"
- Which route is the fastest to work. One day the map suggests another road, it works, and the habit changes without any drama.

The common thread is that the person can hold the belief, defend it in good humor, and still be curious about the other side. When someone challenges it, the response is interest or a friendly argument.

## Core beliefs

The second level is the beliefs a person has built their identity around. The same belief can sit on either level, depending on the person. Someone who defines themselves as a Porsche person, or as a lifelong supporter of a soccer club, will defend that to the end, while for someone else the same idea is a preference. Statements about oneself belong here too, such as "I'm the kind of person who gets angry."

The same goes for beliefs about the world. Racism can be a core belief for people who grew up in places where it is an integral part of the culture. Even a hint that a core belief might be untrue or irrelevant is met with strong negativity, because considering it would mean considering not being who they are. That is the whole idea of a core belief. In life or death situations people can change some of them. This usually means a huge overhaul of the person's life. It does happen, and it is rare, and it almost always takes a great event or a series of events.

More examples of the same kind:

- Diet as identity. One person eats a plant based diet because it suits their health and would happily try something else if the doctor suggested it. Another person is a vegan, or a carnivore, or someone who never eats sugar, and the diet is a statement of who they are. Same food, different level.
- A profession or a role. "I'm a doctor," "I'm a founder," "I'm the one who holds the family together." When the job ends or the children grow up, the person can lose their footing, which shows how much of the self was resting on the belief.
- A nation, a city or a family name. "I'm a New Yorker," "we are a family of engineers." A joke about it from outside can sting in a way a joke about their taste in music never would.
- A political or religious tribe. The same question asked at a dinner table either gets a thoughtful answer or a tight jaw, depending on whether the belief is an opinion or part of the person.
- Self descriptions that sound like facts about a personality. "I'm shy," "I'm not good with money," "I'm not a morning person," "I always have to be the strong one," "I never ask for help." People repeat them for decades, and a friend who says "that doesn't look true to me" is usually met with a defense of the sentence.
- A rival loyalty. A fan of one team who cannot sit in a room wearing the other team's colors.

A useful way to tell the first level from the second is to notice what happens in the body when the belief is questioned. A regular belief gets a smile or a shrug. A core belief gets a rising heat, a defense that arrives before the thinking does, or a quick change of topic.

## Beliefs held as facts

The third level is made of things a person does not experience as beliefs at all. They hold these as how things are, and they cannot picture them being discussed even in theory. Good examples are hard to find, because most of the ones that matter are easy to argue with. Gravity works well. When I let go of something, it falls to the floor, and nobody puts that up for debate. The sun rises in the morning is another. Most people agree about these, which is part of what makes them feel like facts.

Even here the line moves from person to person. That the earth is round is a fact for most people, and then it comes up for discussion, because some people claim it is flat. The ideas people take as facts are the ones they find extraordinarily difficult to let go of.

More examples of the same kind:

- The floor holds me up, and the chair I sit on is solid. Nobody thinks to check, and the thought that a table is mostly empty space at the smallest scale sounds odd even to people who learned it at school.
- Time moves forward in one direction at one speed. A day has the same length for everyone.
- The things I see are really there, in the colors I see them, whether or not anybody is looking.
- Objects stay where they are when I leave the room. Nobody has to decide this each morning.
- My body ends at my skin, and I am the same person who woke up yesterday.
- A heavy stone falls faster than a light one. For a long time this was simply how things were understood to work, and it took careful experiments and trusted voices to look at it again.
- The sun goes around the earth. Our language still says sunrise and sunset, and it still feels true when we stand in the morning light.

What marks this level is how the mind reacts. A third level idea that is challenged does not produce anger. It produces surprise, laughter, a blank look or a polite change of subject, because the person has no place in their head where the question could land.

## Why this matters for awakening

The process of awakening and remembering who we are is the process of letting go of beliefs on all three levels. The levels also explain why it is so difficult.

With core beliefs we feel a lot of resistance to changing them, and we need help from somebody we trust.

With the third level there is no conscious resistance to point at. We cannot even make a case against the change, because we never treated it as something that could change. For this we usually need a guide we trust more than we trust ourselves, someone who can tell us that this is different from what we think. Our first reaction will be to ask how they can claim that. What makes the difference is that we believe them anyway, and make the effort to see a new reality beyond those ideas. In the case of gravity, the idea to try on would be that levitation exists.
