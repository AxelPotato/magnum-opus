# Questions and answers

A few questions about my own life and the school around it. They sit here, after the book, because they put the focus on the form our school has, and that isn't what the book is about.

## Is this a cult?

My friends and my family joked about it a lot at the beginning, because none of them were super surprised by it. I'd already been talking to them about spirituality and things like that for about eight years before I even met Genadiy, so to them it was a natural next step. I think almost every student in our school has heard some version of that joke from a relative or a friend: you moved to God knows where for some meditation stuff, so it's probably a cult. To be fair, I did move, and there is a lot of meditation. We live together and meditate three times a day, so I can see how it looks. If it is a cult, it's a badly run one. I've just written a whole book without inviting you to join it.

That last part is serious. I'm not inviting anybody to become a student of Genadiy. There are two different things here, Giving School and Paradanta, and the difference matters.

## What is Giving School?

It's the way we, Genadiy's students, share our experience, his knowledge, the practices and the method with anyone who wants them. Some of it is online and some of it is retreats, offline, and you take part as much as you want. From what we can tell, about 50,000 people have gone through its different courses and events. I'm one of its instructors, and I've dedicated my time to making it more available in English.

## What is Paradanta?

It's the project of Genadiy's students themselves, and we are its members, fewer than 150 of us. This is where the neo-monastic way of life comes in. Put plainly, a neo-monastery is a place where the whole day is built around one clear goal: to awaken.

It all starts with a very clear decision: yes, in this lifetime I want to answer the questions of who I am and why I'm here. The people who make that decision are willing to make a serious effort for it. We live together, in centers, and a stay can last anything from a few months to years, depending on what the person wishes.

## Who lives in the centers, and why?

Genadiy lives there, and so do we, his students. There are two centers, one in Sochi, where Genadiy lives, and one in Riga. Our day is shaped by three meditative practices and the daily work we do.

We live together because it's a school, and its students are learning to live in a very different way. Letting go of the ideas we have about ourselves is a very big challenge. A lot of it comes with pain, as old hurts come up and you let them go, and that doesn't always look nice while it's coming out of you. It takes a place with people who honestly understand what you are going through and won't judge you for it, and that's what our centers are for.

Once someone has been through it, they don't need to be in the center as much, and they can spend more of their time traveling and sharing it with the world. I've been traveling a lot for more than a year now, and the center is still my home, my base of operations.

## What happens when someone leaves?

Nothing special. We're sad when someone goes. Usually they've simply decided this isn't the lifestyle they want, or not right now, and most of them have very good friends here, so they stay in touch. Some former students live close to our center and we see them now and then. Some go and do something else, maybe a project of their own, and we stay friends. Some stay in our online circles, because that path suits them better for now, and that's fine. Some come back. Very rarely someone leaves with bad feelings. I don't think it has happened in the last few years, but I do remember cases from before.

## What does "modern monk" mean for you?

If you've ever seen a monk, Buddhist or Christian, someone who took the serious spiritual path in one of the religions, and thought to yourself, I want that too, minus the religion: that's pretty much what I thought and felt when I wanted to become a student of Genadiy.

In practice the most important part is the inner one: I've dedicated my life to the spiritual path, and it's my focus every day. That's why I do the three meditations a day whether I'm at the center or not, just the same, and why I can't just stop taking part in something because I don't feel like it that day. It's a way of life, and discipline is a big part of it.

## What is your opinion on substance use?

We, Genadiy's students, have a strict policy: alcohol, drugs and cigarettes aren't allowed for us. Mainly because they interfere with letting go of the many inner programs and processes that run in us, which is delicate enough already, and because they have a big negative effect on our health.

On a more personal note, I never did drugs, and I never smoked, apart from a few cigarettes to try it. I've had a strong position on both since early childhood, because my father smoked a lot and I didn't like it. I did drink a nice amount, though, until I was 21, and after that it went down so much that it became almost irrelevant to my life. I'm a firm believer in freedom, and to me being dependent on a substance goes against that on a basic level, so I never liked the idea. Once my spiritual journey began, it was clear to me that plants of power and other substances weren't my path.

At the same time, I have to give them credit. A huge number of people would never have opened up to the idea of spirituality at all if not for an experience with drugs, or an ayahuasca retreat, for example. So they definitely have their place, and I don't close them off, especially with the recent studies on using psychoactive substances in dealing with depression and the like.
