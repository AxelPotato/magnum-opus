## 1. The short answer

Every comparer in the files answers "why do the religions differ if they say the same thing", and every answer is the comparer's claim and not the tradition's own (P01, P13) [M]. They fall into ten families:

1. One teaching, then decay: Plethon, Steuco, Blavatsky, Guénon in part [C, M].
2. Same idea, other words: Pico, Dara Shikoh, Rumi's grapes, Subba Row [C, M].
3. One faith, many rites: Cusa, Vivekananda [C].
4. Stages of education, or a teacher adapted to an age: Lessing, Leibniz, Besant, Baha'i [C, M].
5. Outer form and inner teaching, with the diversity willed: Schuon, Guénon, Besant [C, M].
6. Levels of Reality: Schuon, Smith [M].
7. Temperament and path: Huxley, Ramakrishna, the Gita, Hodson [M].
8. Partial standpoints: the Udana, Jain many-sidedness, Rumi's elephant [M].
9. One Real through different filters: Hick [M].
10. The differences are real and should be argued, not explained away: Griffiths, Zaehner, Katz, Prothero, Ferrer [M].

Alex has a different kind of answer to a different question. P01 lists three old explanations for the agreement itself (common revelation, common transmission, common experience) and says his is a fourth, common logic: the ideas recur because they follow from one axiom [M]. That explains why the same ideas keep turning up. It says nothing about why the words differ, and section 4 uses that gap.

## 2. Each answer, and what scholars say about it

### 2.1 Schuon: the difference is willed

As recalled, Schuon (The Transcendent Unity of Religions, French 1948, English 1953) gives three reasons (P04) [M, gist]. The Infinite holds every possibility, so it shows in many forms and no one form uses it up. People and ages differ in what they can receive, so a message is shaped to its receiver. A form that stressed its own relativity would stop saving anyone. The contradictions between religions are real at the outer level and are no error, since a form that called itself one of several would lose its force. The outer layer has to present itself as the one truth, because that holds a whole society together, and the inner layer reads the outer as a symbol [M]. P04's summary image, a window that admitted all the other windows would stop working as one, is the file's and not Schuon's. Whether Schuon cites Quran 5:48 and 49:13, as Muslim pluralists do, is [U].

He puts the personal God of the Abrahamic religions and the impersonal Absolute of Advaita and Buddhism at two degrees of one Reality (Beyond-Being, Being, Existence), and compares only within one degree and one layer: Eckhart beside Shankara and Ibn Arabi, never a catechism beside the Gita [M; which pairs, in which book, is U] (P05). He holds that the unity is seen from inside one form, forbids mixing, and rejects a common-factor arithmetic [M].

Scholars say the grid decides what counts as inner, so a disagreeing text is filed as outer and the thesis cannot fail; that the grid is Vedanta and Sufi theory laid over the rest; and that history is set aside [M, standard criticism; who said what U] (P04). Theravada scholars deny that nirvana is the Absolute, and Scholem and Idel read Kabbalah without the grid [M] (P06). Katz aimed at the common-core claim about experience, and whether he touches Schuon is [U]; the school rests on doctrine and intellectual intuition (P12) [M]. Serious allegations have been reported about Schuon's circle, so cite the books and not the man [M] (P04, P09).

### 2.2 Exoteric and esoteric: Guénon on decline and orthodoxy

Guénon (Introduction to the Study of the Hindu Doctrines, 1921) holds that each tradition has an outer face, religious and open to all, and an inner face, initiatic and metaphysical, and that only the inner carries the doctrine [C] (P02). Forms are adapted to a people and an age, and a tradition can lose its inner side; he thought the Christian West had [C]. He reads history as decline through the Hindu ages, and his six thousand years for the present Kali Yuga is his own count, not the Puranic one [C]. Orthodoxy means regular transmission from a lineage. In 1921 that made Buddhism heterodox for him, because it rejects the Veda; Coomaraswamy, Pallis and Schuon pressed him and he authorised amendments [C]. Schuon repudiated his view that the sacraments had sunk to the exoteric level, part of their split of 1948 to 1950 [C]. So one move gave two verdicts on Christianity inside one school.

The Theosophists use it too. Besant's Esoteric Christianity (1901) reads the creed as the outer face of the lesser mysteries [M] (T08).

Scholars say the rule cannot lose, since a disagreeing text can always be assigned to the outer layer (P07, P09, P12) [M]. Sedgwick calls Guénon's approach theological and not historical, and Legenhausen finds the comparisons tenuous and the selection prejudiced [C] (P02). The strict school would refuse Alex's method of many witnesses with no lineage [C] (P02).

### 2.3 Huxley: temperament, idolatry, the knower, and a common factor

The Foreword names the perennial philosophy as the "Highest Common Factor in all preceding and subsequent theologies" [M, recalled, not cleared] (P08; P07 first had "of the higher religions" and P08 corrected it). A common factor is an intersection, a method of subtraction. Alex's method derives from an axiom and then shows examples, and the files say to keep those apart (P08) [M].

His explanations of difference (P08) [M unless marked]. Temperament: the chapter "Religion and Temperament", the Gita's three yogas of knowledge, devotion and works, and W. H. Sheldon's body types [Sheldon link U; the typology is discredited as science, so cite the idea and not the authority]. Idolatry, meaning mistaking a symbol or doctrine for the Reality [chapter title U]. And the condition in the knower: knowledge is a function of being, so direct knowledge needs a changed knower, loving, pure in heart and poor in spirit [ideas M, wording U; do not call them "the Beatitudes"]. The files' own objection, labelled theirs and not a scholar's, is that this last move shields itself, since a critic who does not see it can be told he lacks the conditions (P08).

Scholars say Zaehner argued that nature, monistic and theistic mysticism differ and that Huxley folded them into a monism; Stace gave the common-core claim a better argument; Katz says no experience is unmediated [M] (P08). Huxley read English translations, and for Eckhart the usual source behind such anthologies is Pfeiffer's 1857 collection, which mixes authentic and doubtful texts [M] (P07). No file has a sourced Traditionalist statement about Huxley [U] (P08).

### 2.4 Huston Smith: levels, and two books

Smith (Forgotten Truth, 1976; The World's Religions, 1991) borrows Schuon's tools (P09). A quarrel between a theistic and a non-theistic tradition is often a quarrel between two levels, the celestial Person-God and the impersonal Infinite [M]. Outer forms differ and the inner teaching agrees [M]. He insists the differences are real and valuable and should not be flattened [U]. The two books are the split in practice: one presents each tradition on its own terms, the other gives the shared map, and he meant them read together [M]. Scholars say the levels can reassign any disagreement upward, and the grid puts both Brahman and emptiness at the Infinite while Buddhist specialists keep them apart [M].

### 2.5 Blavatsky and her circle: loss, symbol, veil, and the "blinds"

Her explanation of agreement is descent from one archaic Wisdom-Religion kept by initiates, with proofs said to lie in the crypts of libraries of the Occult Brotherhood [M, wording about 80 percent, not for printing] (T02). Her explanation of difference is loss, in five parts (T02) [M, pattern only; locators unknown]. An outer form for the many and an inner teaching for the few, so disagreement sits in the outer form. Symbols read literally, so allegory became history. Keys lost, the Mysteries closed, priestcraft kept the husk. Adaptation, each age getting a teacher who restates the one teaching (Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster, Jesus). Cycles of decline, the Kali Yuga.

The "blinds" appear in one line in the files: T07 says she also explains differences as deliberate "blinds", veils set by the initiates [M, single mention]. No other file says more. Find her own use in The Secret Doctrine first; meaning and place are [U].

Her circle adds more. Besant's Four Great Religions (1897) has each Great Teacher give the same truth in the form suited to a people and an age [M, chapter content U] (T08). Hodson ties differences in religious feeling to seven types [title M, content U]. Subba Row argues that counts of a person's principles differ because each scheme cuts the same thing at different joints, and he still disagreed with Blavatsky and Sinnett on the count [title and claim M; grouping and specific points U] (T09). Olcott's fourteen propositions of 1891 are the one case where leaders of different Buddhist schools were asked to accept a short common list [M; signatories U].

Scholars say the method cannot fail, since agreement confirms it and disagreement counts as corruption; that it reads Indian and Buddhist terms through a Western occult frame; and that Coleman traced much of her material to nineteenth century books [M] (T02, T03). Max Müller wrote in 1893 that there is no esoteric Buddhism, and the standard reply is the Buddha's "closed fist of a teacher" (Digha Nikaya 16, numbering differs by translator) [M] (T06, T09). Historians reject the claim that a Church council banned rebirth [M] (T04, T08). Her treatment of Jehovah as a lower power breaks Alex's rule never to say the Abrahamic religions fit badly [M] (T02).

### 2.6 Scripture, parables and saints

Rigveda 1.164.46, "ekam sat viprah bahudha vadanti" [M, Sanskrit and locator], says the inspired speak of what is one in many ways, and the verse names gods (Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, Yama, Matarishvan, Garutman) [M]. "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names" is a loose slogan, often credited to Gandhi or Vivekananda, who quoted it (P13) [M].

Gita 4.11 (as remembered, however people approach me I meet them so), 7.21 (I steady the faith of a worshipper in whatever form) and 9.23 (devotees of other gods worship me too, by the wrong rule) are inclusivist: other worship reaches Krishna, imperfectly [M] (P13). Hacker's thesis is that Hindu tolerance mostly absorbs the other and ranks it lower [M; publication details U].

Quran 5:48 says that to each community God gave a law and a way, that had He willed He would have made one community, that people should compete in good works, and that all return to God, who will tell them what they differed about. 2:285 has the believers make no distinction among the messengers (P13) [M]. Classical commentators read these inside the Abrahamic frame [M].

The elephant. In the Udana (6.4) the Buddha tells it of quarrelling sectarians who cling to views; P13 reads the point as against clinging, with the Buddha as the one who sees, and no claim that religions are parts of one truth [M]. Jain philosophy has a doctrine of many-sidedness, anekantavada: each standpoint (naya) is partial and true from where it stands; Shankara attacks it, and Jains hold that only their path liberates and that souls are many [M]. P13 could not name a Jain text that tells the elephant [U]; P15 recalls the Jains telling it, from an unchecked file [M]. Rumi's elephant in a dark house (Masnavi III, near 1259 [U]) concerns the limits of sense perception more than religions [M]. Newbigin noted the story needs a teller who sees the whole elephant [M].

Rumi's grapes (Masnavi II, near 3681 [U]) is the cleanest parable about words. Four men share one coin and quarrel because each names a grape in his own language, angur for the Persian, inab for the Arab, uzum for the Turk and istafil for the Greek, and a man who knew all four languages would settle it [M]. Quote Nicholson, which is public domain; whether the "neither Christian nor Jew" poem is Rumi's is [U] (P13).

Ramakrishna pictured one lake with many landing steps, where Hindus say jal, Muslims pani and Christians water [M]. His slogan "yato mat tato path" and his practice of other religions rest on disciples' accounts, and part-2-notes could not confirm the slogan [U] (P13). Vivekananda at Chicago quoted the streams that mingle in one sea and said each faith should take in the spirit of the others and grow by its own law [C, search-summary wording] (P01); he ranked Advaita highest, so he is an inclusivist [M] (P13). Coomaraswamy's mountain has several gateways at the foot and one summit, and the paths cannot be seen to meet until the top; he also wrote that we should approach another believer as one who is already "one of His" [C, check the book] (P03).

### 2.7 Hick, and the writers who say the differences are real

Hick (An Interpretation of Religion, 1989) says the Real an sich lies beyond every human concept, and the traditions are different human responses, filtered in a Kantian way and met as persons or as impersonal absolutes. The test is whether a tradition moves people from self-centredness to Reality-centredness [M] (P12). He accepts that concepts shape experience and defends "one Real, many responses", not a common core of experience [M] (P08).

Scholars say: Plantinga, that pluralism is itself an exclusive claim; D'Costa, that it is not neutral and does not match what the traditions say; Buddhists, that nirvana is no manifestation of a ground; and if nothing can be said of the Real, "it is one" is out of bounds [M] (P12). The price is calling the traditions partly mistaken about themselves.

The realists (P12) [M]. Griffiths holds that traditions teach contradictory doctrines and the respectful course is to say so and argue. Zaehner holds that loving union with a personal God differs from union by identity, the objection a Christian or Muslim reader will raise against the second view (P07). Katz writes, as recalled, "There are NO pure (i.e. unmediated) experiences", essay p. 26 [M, not cleared; the parenthesis matters]. Prothero says religions ask different questions and offer different cures. Loy says "not two" does not mean "one". J. Z. Smith says the comparer makes the similarity and must state the respect and include difference [page U]. Alex's claim concerns texts and ideas, and Katz's concerns experience (P11).

### 2.8 The "different words, same thing" move

It is old. Pico thought Plato and Aristotle used different words for the same ideas [C]. Dara Shikoh (Majma al-Bahrayn, c. 1655) set Sufi and Vedantic terms side by side, held there was no essential difference apart from the manner of exposition, and called the Upanishads the Quran's hidden book [C]. Cusa (1453) wrote of one religion in a variety of rites [C; word order U]. Lessing's ring parable says the true ring cannot be shown and each son should prove his by love, which is Alex's own stance of examples without proof [C]. Leibniz (1714) wrote of drawing gold from dross [C].

The risk is that the comparer picks the match. Dara's Vedanta came out in Sufi vocabulary, and the old comparers mostly had a home team: Ficino and Steuco put Christianity on top, and Cusa on one reading [C] (P01). Translators carry equations too (P07) [M].

The careful defenders share rules (P12, section 4) [M, a summary of the field and not a count]: state the respect in which you compare, use the tradition's own term, report difference as well as match, quote inner or scholarly sources and not the popular version, and do not let a similar word hide a different meaning. Panikkar's term for the honest version is homeomorphic equivalents, terms that do a similar job in different systems without being identical [M]. Wittgenstein's family resemblance, applied to religion by Alston and Saler, allows a cluster of themes that recur without a shared core [M], which fits the essay's admission that rebirth is not unanimous.

## 3. Side by side

What they share (P01, P13) [M]. Every answer is the comparer's. Nearly every one keeps the difference on the surface and the agreement underneath; the realists in 2.7 are the exception. Every one has a top: Dara reads Vedanta in Sufi words, Vivekananda ranks Advaita highest, Kukai puts Shingon highest, the Baha'i scheme puts the latest revelation last, and Schuon's grid is Vedanta and Sufi theory. Every one deals with a counterexample by reassigning it, to the outer layer, a lower level, corruption, an earlier stage or a filter.

Where they fight. Guénon attacked Theosophy (1921, 1923) while sharing the idea of a primordial teaching, and split with Schuon over Christianity [C]. Subba Row disagreed with Blavatsky on the count [U]. Zaehner argued against Huxley, and Katz against Stace [M]. On lives the movement splits: Guénon rejects Western reincarnation, Coomaraswamy has one transmigrant, and Blavatsky taught rebirth by the 1880s [C, M] (P02, P03, P16). P01's fair summary of the scholarship is that the agreement is partly real, partly made, and the makers had a stake [M].

What each costs. Descent from one source failed as history: Casaubon redated the Hermetica in 1614, and Yates and Walker called the lineage mistaken history [C]. Outer and inner cannot lose. Levels are one school's grid. Temperament and stages carry the Sheldon and progress problems [M]. Standpoint parables need a teller who sees the whole. Hick's filter is the cleanest philosophically and is attacked from both sides.

Rigour and permission trade. The strict authors (Guénon, Schuon, Coomaraswamy) demand one form and a lineage and refuse mixing. The loose ones (Huxley, Perry, Wilson) mix freely and explain less. Alex's witness method is nearer the loose camp and wants the strict camp's care [my reading from P04, P07]. On Race's map of exclusivist, inclusivist and pluralist [M], an axiom on top with the traditions as witnesses sits with inclusivism, and Hick's pluralism is the one that puts no frame on top [my reading]. Say so before a reader does.

## 4. Four ways to say it

The drafts are for Alex to put in his own voice, and the quotation marks in them mark my draft sentences.

A. Name the match and keep it small. Anchors: J. Z. Smith (a comparison has two things and a respect), Panikkar, Loy, and Dara's lesson that the comparer picks the match. It costs nothing in truth and lets the reader check. Draft: "When I put two lines side by side, I'm the one doing the matching, so I try to say exactly what I'm matching. For example, both of these say the sense of a separate self is made and can end." The example is the file's own (P12) [M]. Each pair then gets a named "both say X about Y", which tightens the draft's habit of "the same things".

B. Outer face and inner face, credited and limited. Anchors: Schuon (the difference is on purpose and the outer layer must claim to be the one truth), Guénon, and the Theosophist version. The draft already does this at line 59. It is a theory with a name and a record, so credit it and say what it costs: it can file any disagreeing text under the outer face. Draft: "Several of the people I quote say a religion has a face for everybody and a face for people who go looking, and that the two can differ without either being false. Frithjof Schuon built a system on it. I use it in a smaller way. When a tradition's everyday teaching skips one of these ideas, I look in its mystics or its scripture read closely, and I tell you which layer each quote comes from. You're allowed to catch me filing the awkward texts under the outer face." It would be honest to add that the strict school would not accept this method, since I have no lineage and take witnesses from many traditions [C, M] (P02, P04).

C. One thing seen from different places, with the teller problem. Anchors: Rumi's grapes for words, the Udana and Jain many-sidedness for standpoints, Coomaraswamy's mountain, Ramakrishna's lake. Grapes fits best because it is about words. Draft: "Rumi has a story of four men with one coin who quarrel because the Persian says angur, the Arab inab, the Turk uzum and the Greek istafil for the same grape. A man who knew all four languages could settle it. I only have a phrasebook, so take my reading as a tourist's." It links to the computer picture, where windows on one machine each show it from their own program; that link is mine and no author's. It meets Newbigin's point by having Alex say he is not the teller who sees the whole.

D. Decline to say why, and show your top. Anchors: the ten families in section 1, each the comparer's claim; the fact that the axiom explains recurrence and not difference; and P13's note that every reconciler has a top. Draft: "People who have compared religions give many reasons for the different vocabularies: one old teaching worn down in the retelling, an outer face and an inner face, different temperaments, different stages of a long education, one Reality seen through different filters. Each is the comparer's story and none is the tradition's own. My axiom says why the same few ideas should keep turning up. It has nothing to say about why the words differ, so I'm leaving that open. And every comparer ends up with a top, a view from which the others look like rungs. Dara read the Upanishads in Sufi words and Vivekananda put Advaita highest. Mine is the axiom, and I'd rather say so now." This is the most candid and the cheapest to defend.

A and D fit together best. C is the warmest and the most quotable. B is in the draft already and should be named.

## 5. What not to say

- That the traditions or their texts say all religions are the same. Prothero, Griffiths and Zaehner are the standard replies, and the Rigveda, the Gita and the Udana were not written for pluralism (P13) [M].
- That the Gita says all religions are equal. 4.11, 7.21 and 9.23 are inclusivist and call other worship imperfect [M].
- That Quran 5:48 or 2:285 say the religions are one. 5:48 says each community has its own law and way and is to compete in good works [M].
- That the Udana's elephant is a pluralist story, or that Rumi's is about religions (P13) [M].
- That Jain thinkers retold the elephant. The draft says this now, and P13 could not name a Jain text that does [U]. Safe wording keeps the doctrine of many-sidedness and drops the retelling until a source turns up.
- That "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names" is the Rigveda's wording, or any Rumi line from a quotation site, such as "you are not a drop in the ocean". Quote Nicholson with a Masnavi line, or nothing (P06, P13) [U].
- That the Traditionalists are allies of the Theosophists, that Schuon, Smith or Nasr hold Blavatsky's views, or that Schuon, Coomaraswamy or Guénon teach the return of the same individual. Guénon wrote two books against her movement [C].
- That priests or Church councils suppressed the sameness. Historians reject the council claims, including the Council of 553 as a ban on reincarnation (T04, T08) [M], the story cannot be tested, and the notes forbid a named enemy.
- That the Highest Common Factor is Alex's method. His derives; the factor subtracts. Nor Leibniz as coiner of "perennial philosophy": Steuco's De perenni philosophia (1540) is earlier [C].
- "Heresy of separateness" or "There is no religion higher than truth" as Blavatsky's (the motto is the Benares family's, adopted by the Society around 1880), or the nosegay line as hers (it is Montaigne's, which she quotes) (T01, T03) [M, U].
- That emptiness is the One. "Not two" does not mean "one", and maya and mithya mean "dependent" more than "illusion" (P12, part-2-notes) [M].
- Sheldon's types as science, or "the Beatitudes" for Huxley's three conditions (P08) [M].
- Any Gandhi one-liner from the web, or a line passed as "Huxley said", which is often someone he was quoting (P13) [M].
