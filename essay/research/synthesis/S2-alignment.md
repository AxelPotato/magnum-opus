## 1. (a) The axiom and the chain, in three vocabularies

Plain words. There is one whole and everything is in it. That whole looks at itself from a very large number of positions, and each position is a life with a point of view. From inside one position the positions feel like separate things. That feeling is an accurate report of a local fact (my memory is mine and yours is yours) read as a fact about the whole. The looking from many positions is a phase, and it ends when the whole knows itself as one. Alex offers this as an assumption and asks whether the main religious ideas follow, which tests coherence and leaves truth alone. The chain: the separation is an illusion in the sense of a wrong reading of something real; it feels solid because the wall is real inside the system; one life is too short to finish, so the run is longer and something continues; what I do to another position I do to the whole, which is karma and the golden rule; a person can become a very good separate program (the small square) or stop assuming the separation (the big square); the stages of that move form a scale from minus four to plus four, pictured as speed; and letting go is the method, because it lowers the wall.

Perennial words. The nearest formulation is Schuon's, with Guénon's logic underneath. Reality is the Absolute, which is also the Infinite. Nothing can lie outside the Infinite, so it includes the possibility of something that looks like not the Absolute. That possibility, realised, is Maya, real in its own order and illusory only when taken as separate. Atma, the Self, is the Absolute seen from inside a person, and the ego is a ray that takes itself for a source. The fall is forgetting and deliverance is recognition, which is identity and not arrival [M; Schuon's own wording unchecked, P04, P05]. Guénon's version is Universal Possibility, with the human state as one of indefinitely many states of a being [M; titles C]. Coomaraswamy adds Self and self, and the claim that the only transmigrant is the one Self [C]. Smith makes it readable as four levels of reality and four of selfhood [M]. Note the kind of answer. Schuon and Guénon give a logic with no motive: manifestation follows from Infinity. Alex's second clause gives a motive: a whole that wants to know itself needs a position to know from. The oldest motive text is Brihadaranyaka 1.4.1 to 4, where the Self alone is afraid, takes no delight, wishes for a second and divides in two [M; check in Olivelle].

Theosophical words. Blavatsky's Proem to The Secret Doctrine (1888) has three propositions: one boundless Principle beyond thought; universes that appear and vanish in cycles; the identity of all souls with a universal Over-Soul, and an obligatory pilgrimage for every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law [M for structure, U for wording]. Alex's clauses match the first and third. The second has no counterpart in his axiom and should not be imported (T08 finding 1). Her third is one step softer than his first: her souls are identical with an Over-Soul that is itself an aspect of the Unknown Root [M, T07 C17]. Her reason for separation: unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity only by passing through experience, so the spark wins individuality by its own effort, with no privileges [M for the Proem gloss, T02 finding 10; U that the Key repeats it]. In the Rounds scheme (Sinnett 1883) the life-wave descends into denser matter, bottoms out on Earth in the middle of the fourth round and climbs back carrying the self consciousness it gained [M, T09 finding 3]. Her chain words: personality is the mask, individuality the lasting Ego; karma is a law of harmony; devachan is the rest between lives; brotherhood is a fact in nature [M].

## 3. (c) Where his order of derivation differs, and what he can safely claim

Lives. Alex reaches lives by a dilemma: if most people do not finish in one life, either the whole is a bad designer or the run is longer. No Traditionalist derives lives that way, and most do not teach lives in the Western sense. Guénon: no state is repeated, the human state is one of indefinitely many, transmigration means change of state [C quotes, M reasoning]. Schuon followed in outline [U]. Nasr, Lings and Burckhardt hold the Islamic one life plus barzakh [M/U]. Coomaraswamy keeps transmigration and moves the subject to the one Self [C]. Theosophy takes the pilgrimage as received law and then argues for it by reason in Alex's own style (unequal starts, prodigies, one life too short, karma carrying over) [M]. The academic ancestors of his dilemma are Lessing and Hick. So on lives he sits with Theosophy, Lessing and Hick and he should not cite "the perennial philosophy" for it.

A tension inside the draft. The dilemma treats the individual as the unit that has to finish. The axiom says the individual is a position of the whole, and then the one running every life is the whole (P16 section 1; Gaudapada 2.32). That is why Coomaraswamy fits the axiom best and the Theosophical Ego fits the saved state best. The draft holds both pictures. He can keep both if he labels them as answers at two levels.

Karma. He derives it from clause one. In the traditions karma comes first as a law of consequence (BU 4.4.5, "as one acts so one becomes"; AN 6.63, "it is intention I call kamma" [M]) and oneness, where held, grounds the ethic as a separate step. In Advaita karma belongs to appearance. Blavatsky is a partial exception: karma as a law of harmony with no judge, tied to unity by collective karma [M]. The clean precedents for deriving it from oneness are Schopenhauer, Emerson's Compensation [M] and Wang Yangming's one body [M]. Do not cite Guénon for karma: he says it "never had the sense of causality" [C, one result, book unplaced], which overstates, since consequence is classical.

Third clause. For Advaita, Schuon and Guénon the Self never left and deliverance is recognition. Alex's "returns" fits Plotinus, Proclus and Theosophy. His "ends" fits Schuon. He should say which, or let both stand (P04 finding 16).

Stages. The traditions' staged maps are ladders of practice (Underhill, Teresa, the Voice), worlds (Dante, the Mi'raj) or degrees of reality (Schuon, Smith). Growth of a person through time is Theosophy, Aurobindo, Wilber, Bucke, Teilhard and Underhill. The strict Traditionalists reject evolutionary pictures (P05 finding 17). So his natural comparers on the arrow are the first group, and the Traditionalists should be cited for "different forms, same inner teaching" and not for stages (T05 section 9).

Safe claims.
1. The nearest older statement of my axiom is Blavatsky's 1888 Proem, once the wording is checked. She gave it as received teaching and I give it as an assumption, with no lost original and no Masters.
2. Schuon and Guénon give a logic for why the many follows from the One. I give a motive. Both kinds of answer exist.
3. The traditions disagree about lives, and the two movements I lean on split on it. The axiom itself does not settle it.
4. On who carries over there are two answers, Theosophy's lasting individuality and Coomaraswamy's one Self.
5. Karma is taken as given in most traditions. Deriving it from unity has precedents in Schopenhauer and Emerson.
6. Stage maps run from four steps to a hundred. I know of none numbered symmetrically about zero [M, not searched; P18 C18].
Unsafe: "all traditions say"; "the perennialists teach reincarnation"; "Theosophy calls it birth"; "Jainism or Theravada say one whole"; "Huxley is the best".

## 4. (d) The skeleton, and a better framing

Assessment. The draft names four questions: what is real, who are we, what happens to us, how do we live. Huxley's definition covers the first, second and last, and omits the third, which is exactly where the movement splits. The draft's method is also derivation from one principle, which is closer to Schuon and Guénon than to Huxley's anthology, whose Foreword reaches "the Highest Common Factor in all preceding and subsequent theologies" by subtraction [M, P08]. Schuon rejects that arithmetic because the unity lies above the forms and cannot be found by averaging [M, P04 finding 13]. So "that's the skeleton of this part, and I'll follow it" misdescribes both the structure and the method.

Who is best for which job (Alex's word is "best", so read this as "most useful"). What is real: Schuon for the argument, Guénon for the logic of Universal Possibility, Smith for the readable version, Huxley's chapters "That Art Thou" and "The Nature of the Ground" as a door [M]. Who are we: Coomaraswamy for the two selves and the one transmigrant [C], Smith's four levels of selfhood [M], Theosophy's individuality and personality [M], with James, Underhill and Stace as testimony [M]. What happens to us: no single perennial answer, and the draft should say so. How to live: Schopenhauer and Vivekananda for deriving the ethic from oneness [M], Schuon for virtue and humility [M], Huxley's Charity chapter for the same move [M]. The way: Schuon's three parts, Underhill's five stages, the Voice's portals [M]. Objections if wanted: Zaehner, Katz, Forman, Sedgwick, Hanegraaff, Ferrer [M]. What Huxley is best for: a readable door, the Foreword's idea that knowledge depends on the knower's being [M, recalled], which backs the draft's "the evidence is the experience of letting go" and carries the objection that it shields itself, and The Doors of Perception's reducing valve [M], a cousin of the one machine picture, with the drug context in view (P08 lead 10).

Improved framing in six moves. A family of books with names by job. Each gives an outline and they differ. The skeleton is the three clauses, with Huxley's three parts as a sticky note and the fourth question added. Two honesty warnings (the strict school objects to the method and disliked Theosophy; every comparer has a home team). Theosophy with the third proposition. Older voices with the caveat that none was written for pluralism. Do not restore the Katz paragraph, which Alex cut. One clause is enough: the claim is about ideas as written.

"There's also a fair question that the axiom has to face: if there is only one mind, who is it that carries over? The Advaita answer is that individuality belongs to the level of appearance, which in the machine is the process, since the processor never had an individuality to begin with. Ananda Coomaraswamy made the point in 1944 in a paper called On the One and Only Transmigrant, which opens with a line of Shankara's that there is no transmigrant but the Lord. On that reading what travels from life to life is the whole, wearing each of us in turn, and nothing individual carries over at all, which is bad news for the saved state and good news for the axiom. Gaudapada, an early Advaitin, goes one step further and says that from the highest standpoint nobody is bound and nobody is freed, which I find hard to argue with and impossible to put on a poster. So I have two pictures, the saved state working at the level of the process and the one transmigrant at the level of the processor, and I'd call it an open question."
