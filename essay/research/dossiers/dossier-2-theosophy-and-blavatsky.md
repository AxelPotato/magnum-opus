## 1. The picture in brief

The Theosophical Society (TS) was founded in New York on 17 November 1875 by Helena Petrovna Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge and a few others, out of the Spiritualist scene [M]. Its first stated aim was research into the hidden laws of nature. Over about twenty years the order of the aims flipped: brotherhood first, the study of religions, philosophies and sciences second, the investigation of unexplained laws and latent powers third. The move to India in 1879 gave the comparing an Eastern direction [M].

Blavatsky's big books are comparative encyclopedias. Isis Unveiled (1877) is mostly Western in weight (Hermetic, Kabbalistic, Neoplatonic, Gnostic). The Secret Doctrine (1888) is mostly Indian and Buddhist in vocabulary. The Key to Theosophy (1889) is a plain question-and-answer handbook. The Voice of the Silence (1889) is a short devotional text on the inner path. The Theosophical Glossary (1892, posthumous) is a cross-religion dictionary [M].

Her explanation of why the religions agree is historical. There was one archaic Wisdom-Religion, kept by initiates, from which the outer religions descend and have decayed. Her explanation of difference is loss: priestly corruption, symbols read literally, lost keys, teaching adapted to each age. Her authority rested on hidden Masters and on a text (the Book of Dzyan) that no outsider has seen. That is the weak point [M]. The Hodgson report (1885) called her phenomena fraud, Vernon Harrison's 1986 paper attacked the report, and historians now mostly set the question aside and study what the movement did [M].

Her frame is one boundless Principle with no personal face. Individual souls are rays or sparks of it. They make a long pilgrimage through matter, through many lives, under a law of harmony called karma, and return having become self-conscious by their own effort. The separate self is the passing personality, a mask, and the illusion is taking the mask for the whole. Brotherhood is a fact of nature, so ethics follows from unity [M].

Three things matter most for Alex.
- The Secret Doctrine opens with three propositions (section 3.2). They have the shape of his axiom and its consequences: one source beyond thought, a universe that appears and vanishes in cycles, and every soul identical with a universal Over-Soul and bound to a pilgrimage through lives under karma. She gives them as received teaching, not as logic [M]. Her Over-Soul is "itself an aspect of the Unknown Root", so her third step is slightly softer than "the whole and the parts are one" [M].
- Theosophy has no speed, no galaxy rung of its own and no minus four to plus four. The mapping on to run, bike, car, fly, orbit and escape velocity is Alex's, and the essay should say so [M]. Theosophy puts the Earth's Logos above the Master, where his arrow leaves Earth at plus one [M].
- He can say plainly how he differs. She explains agreement by a lost original and hidden teachers. He explains it by logic from an axiom and invites the reader to check the examples [M].

Cautions that run through the whole dossier.
- Blavatsky's own teaching changed. In Isis Unveiled (1877) she rejected the general doctrine of reincarnation. By 1888 she taught it as the rule. "Blavatsky taught reincarnation" needs a date [M].
- Keep her own work (1875 to 1891) apart from the later layers of Besant, Leadbeater and Bailey, and apart from the Society as a body [M].
- The two movements Alex leans on were rivals. The Traditionalist school of the perennial philosophy (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) called Theosophy a counterfeit and rejected its reincarnation, while sharing the idea of one primordial teaching [M]. One clause saying so protects him from a reader who knows the field.
- Scholars find her equations of terms strained in many places (anatta, alaya, devachan, the sevenfold person), and the root-race scheme is the part hardest to defend (section 9).

## 2. Blavatsky and the Society: who she was and what the Society set out to do

### 2.1 Her life before 1873

- Born Helena von Hahn on 12 August 1831 (new style) at Ekaterinoslav, Russian Empire (now Dnipro) [M]. Father an army officer of German descent. Mother the novelist Helena Fadeyeva, who died young [M]. Princess Dolgorukova was her grandmother and Sergei Witte a cousin [M].
- At seventeen she married the much older Nikifor Blavatsky and left within months [M]. The year is 1848 in her own and Sinnett's early account and 1849 in Meade and in the usual modern date [U]. Say "at seventeen" or leave the year out.
- She later claimed about a decade of travel from the early 1850s, including Tibet and study under Masters. Almost none of it is documented [M]. The first meeting with the Master Morya in London in 1851 is her own account [M].
- A safe sentence: "A Russian-born writer and medium who said she was taught by hidden teachers." It is accurate and does not endorse the teachers [M].
- Her first Russian publication was the travel series From the Caves and Jungles of Hindostan in Katkov's Russkii Vestnik (from 1879, signed Radda-Bai) [M]. A Russian Theosophical Society appeared in 1908 [U]. Ouspensky's Tertium Organum (1912), Scriabin and Kandinsky belong to the Russian reception, as do the Roerichs (Nicholas 1874 to 1947, Helena 1879 to 1955), whose Agni Yoga or Living Ethics (1920 to 1937) was received from a Teacher whom Theosophy knows as Master M.; Helena worked on a Russian Secret Doctrine [M, publication U]. Scholars call the Roerich teaching a Theosophical offshoot, and Roerich is disputed in Russia [M].

### 2.2 America and Spiritualism, 1873 to 1875

- She reached New York in July 1873 [M]. In 1874, at the Eddy farmhouse in Chittenden, Vermont, she met Olcott (1832 to 1907), a lawyer, Civil War investigator and journalist, who was writing up the Eddy seances (People from the Other World, 1875) [M].
- She presented herself as a medium but said the visible spirits were not the dead. She later told correspondents that the Masters had sent her to prove real phenomena and to correct Spiritualist doctrine [M]. The 1875 Corson letter (recalled as 11 February 1875) as the source of the "sent to America" claim is [U].
- Lavoie's point is worth one clause: the TS began inside Spiritualism and defined itself against it [M].
- Sources for this period: Olcott, Old Diary Leaves vol. 1 (1895); Lavoie (2012); Meade [M].

### 2.3 The founding, September to November 1875

- On 7 September 1875 George H. Felt lectured on the lost canon of proportion of the Egyptians in Blavatsky's rooms, and Olcott proposed a society for such study [M]. The name Theosophical Society was chosen at a meeting in the days after: 8 or 13 September [U], with the credit for the name going to Charles Sotheran [U] (one file mentions a competing dictionary-browsing story [U]). Leave the name's origin out of the essay.
- Officers were elected on 30 October. The inaugural meeting and Olcott's presidential address on 17 November 1875 is the conventional founding date. Olcott was President and Blavatsky Corresponding Secretary [M].
- Do not name any other officer [U]. The claim "Judge, counsel" was removed: the verifier recalls Charles Carleton Massey as "Counsel to the Society" and Judge as a founding member and a lawyer, not an elected officer [U].
- Safe: "Founded in New York in 1875", "17 November", and "founded by Blavatsky, Olcott, Judge and others" [M].

### 2.4 The first aim, and how the Objects changed

- The 1875 Preamble had one aim: to collect and diffuse knowledge of the laws that govern the universe [M]. The recalled By-laws sentence is a handle only [M]. The Preamble explained the name as seeking knowledge of the supreme power and higher spirits [M, about 70 percent]. It hoped that, by going deeper into ancient esoteric philosophies than modern science had, members could find proof of an "unseen universe" (the title of an 1875 science book by Balfour Stewart and P. G. Tait, first published anonymously) [M]. No brotherhood and no comparing of religions appear in it [M].
- So the TS did not start as a comparative society. Alex's note that the comparative study came into the wording later is right [M]. The repository notes, written after an earlier session with working search, say the same: one aim in 1875, the comparative wording current since 1896 [C].
- The stages as recalled [M for (a), (b) and the 1896 reform; U for exact years and for later edits].
  - (a) 1875: one aim, knowledge of the laws governing the universe.
  - (b) About 1878 to 1882, in Bombay and Madras: a list of three. Form the nucleus of a universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, colour or creed. A study aim naming Eastern literatures, religions and sciences (the form "Aryan and other Eastern" was in use in the 1880s). Investigate unexplained laws of nature and the psychical powers latent in man. Brotherhood moves to first place, which fits the Mahatma Letters (section 6).
  - (c) 1896: the second Object is reworded to the comparative form: encourage the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science. The first by then names race, creed, sex, caste or colour.
  - (d) Minor later edits (recalled as 1949 and 1975 [U]) leave the present form. The verifier recalls that today's Adyar text reads "a nucleus of the Universal Brotherhood of Humanity" and the older text "the nucleus of a Universal Brotherhood of Humanity" [U, about 75 percent]. Copy the wording from the Society's own page if it is ever quoted.
- Do not quote the Objects from memory. The Key's own printed wording of the second Object is [U]. Do not quote "comparative study of religions, philosophies and sciences" as the Society's text: the present wording is "comparative religion, philosophy and science". The essay's own phrase that the aims "came to include the comparative study of religion, philosophy and science" is accurate; do not turn it into a quotation [M].

### 2.5 Motto, creed and freedom of thought

- The motto "There is no religion higher than truth" translates the Sanskrit satyat nasti paro dharmah, the family motto of the Maharajas of Benares, adopted by the TS around 1880 and set on its seal [M]. The earlier-search repository notes agree with "around 1880" [C]. That it stands on the title page of the 1888 Secret Doctrine is [U], and that it was on the seal from the start is [U]. Dharma is wider than "religion" (law, duty, right order), so a specialist could say the English narrows it [M].
- It is the Society's motto, not a Blavatsky saying. Do not present it as "a Blavatsky quote" [M]. Say "around 1880", not a closer date.
- The Society has no creed. Members may hold any belief or none. What it has is three Objects (whose wording changed) and a motto [M]. Membership requires agreement only with the first Object [M]. A freedom-of-thought statement says no teacher or writer, Blavatsky included, can impose views on members (year recalled as 1924 [U]).
- The "Three Truths" (the soul is immortal; the principle of life dwells in us and is undying; each person is their own lawgiver) are a passage from Mabel Collins's Idyll of the White Lotus (1884), which Theosophical publications print as a summary. They are not Blavatsky's and not the Society's creed [M]. Their words are [U].
- The inner circle (section 2.9) qualifies "creedless": the open Society had no creed, the Esoteric Section had a pledge and a teacher [M].

### 2.6 India and the Eastern turn, 1878 to 1882

- Blavatsky and Olcott left New York in December 1878 and reached Bombay in February 1879 [M]. (One file said "moved to India in 1878"; that is wrong as written.) They allied with Dayananda Saraswati's Arya Samaj and broke with it in 1882 [M; dates U]. In October 1879 she began the journal The Theosophist [M]. Blavatsky became an American citizen in 1878 [M].
- On 25 May 1880 in Galle, Ceylon, she and Olcott took the Buddhist five precepts in public [M]. Olcott wrote A Buddhist Catechism (1881), approved by the monk Hikkaduwe Sumangala, and worked for Sinhalese Buddhist schools [M]. Headquarters moved to Adyar, Madras, in December 1882 [M].
- This explains why the later vocabulary (karma, mahatma, devachan) is Sanskrit and why Isis Unveiled (1877) is more Hermetic than the later books. The softer reading is better: Isis already treats Brahmanism and Buddhism at length. What grew after 1879 was the load of Sanskrit terms and the Vedanta frame [M]. Bevir's article "The West Turns Eastward" (Journal of the American Academy of Religion 62, 1994, pp. 747 to 767 [U on pages]) argues she moved from a Western occult frame to Eastern categories and changed the occult tradition by doing so [M].
- In Godwin's account the early Masters were Egyptian (the "Brotherhood of Luxor", names such as Tuitit Bey, 1875 to 1878) and only later Tibetan and Indian [U on the chronology]. The Hermetic Brotherhood of Luxor was a separate order that came into public view from about the mid-1880s. It is a neighbour of the Society and not an earlier source of it. Whether it is the same body as the "Luxor" adepts of her letters of 1875 to 1878 is disputed [M on separateness; U on dates].

### 2.7 The Coulomb affair, the Hodgson report and Harrison

- In 1884, while Blavatsky and Olcott were in Europe, Emma and Alexis Coulomb, her housekeeper and carpenter at Adyar, accused her of faking the Masters' letters through a hidden panel behind the shrine. The Madras Christian College Magazine printed the charge [M].
- The Society for Psychical Research (SPR, founded 1882) sent Richard Hodgson to India (late 1884 to early 1885). His report, SPR Proceedings vol. 3 (December 1885), said the letters were hers and the phenomena fraud [M]. The committee's summary called her, as recalled, one of the most accomplished, ingenious and interesting impostors in history. That sentence is the committee's, not Hodgson's alone, and it is a handle only: copy it from the Proceedings before quoting [M]. In its fuller form it says the committee regards her neither as the mouthpiece of hidden seers nor as a mere vulgar adventuress [M].
- She left India in March 1885 and did not sue [M].
- In 1986 Vernon Harrison, a document examiner (and, recalled, a former president of the Royal Photographic Society [U]), published "J'accuse: An Examination of the Hodgson Report of 1885", Journal of the SPR 53 (1986), pp. 286 to 310 [M; April issue U]. He argued that Hodgson's handwriting evidence and method were flawed. A longer book followed: H. P. Blavatsky and the SPR (Theosophical University Press, 1997) [M]. The SPR did not retract the report [M]. The SPR's own later statement is known mainly through Theosophical sources: do not use it [M].
- The repository notes record the Harrison reference (JSPR 53, pp. 286 to 310) from an earlier search session [C].
- The Kiddle incident (1883) turned on a K.H. passage that closely paralleled a Spiritualist lecture by Henry Kiddle. Theosophists blamed a hasty copying disciple. Critics saw human authorship [M].
- Safe line: the SPR judged her phenomena fraudulent in 1885, and a later paper in its own journal challenged that [M].

### 2.8 The Masters in her story

- From 1880 to 1884 or 1885, letters said to come from two Masters, Morya and Koot Hoomi (K.H.), reached A. P. Sinnett, editor of The Pioneer at Allahabad, and A. O. Hume (later a founder of the Indian National Congress), by non-ordinary delivery at Adyar and Simla [M]. The date range varies across files ("1880 to 1884", "1880 to 1885"); print no range until a catalogue entry is read [U]. They were published in 1923 (A. T. Barker) [M]. Details in section 6.
- Do not cite the Letters as evidence. They are Theosophy's own account of why brotherhood is Object one [M].

### 2.9 London, 1887 to 1891, the Esoteric Section and her death

- She left India ill in 1885, lived in Wurzburg and Ostend, and settled in London in 1887 [M]. She founded the magazine Lucifer and the Blavatsky Lodge and published The Secret Doctrine (1888, subtitled The Synthesis of Science, Religion and Philosophy), The Key to Theosophy (1889) and The Voice of the Silence (1889) [M]. The Theosophical Glossary came after her death (1892) [M].
- Annie Besant reviewed the Secret Doctrine, met her in 1889 and joined [M].
- In October 1888 she opened an inner circle, first the Esoteric School of Theosophy and by about 1890 the Esoteric Section, bound by a pledge and under her as Head, to give students teaching beyond the open Society [M].
- After her death the section was led jointly by Annie Besant and William Q. Judge (Judge for America). Their quarrel over the charges against Judge ended in the split of 1895. Besant alone led the part that stayed with Adyar [M for the joint leadership; U for exact names and the date of the change]. ("After 1891 Besant led it" is wrong or one-sided.)
- The Golden Stairs, a list of qualities for the chela from the Esoteric Section papers of 1888 to 1889 [U], has eleven clauses as recalled [U for count, wording and year]: a clean life, an open mind, a pure heart, an eager intellect, an unveiled spiritual perception, a brotherliness for all, readiness to give and receive advice, courageous endurance of personal injustice, brave declaration of principles when unjustly attacked, valiant defence of those unjustly attacked, and a constant eye to the ideal of human progression and perfection [U]. An earlier list of seven left out four of these.
- She died of influenza in London on 8 May 1891. Theosophists keep 8 May as White Lotus Day [M].

### 2.10 The word Theosophy and the Ammonius story

- Theosophia is Greek for divine wisdom, wisdom about the divine [M]. In the Key she reads it as the wisdom the gods have, not "wisdom of God" in the monotheist sense [M].
- Her story: the word goes back to the third century and the Alexandrian school of Ammonius Saccas, whose followers were called lovers of truth (Philalethians; the Key may spell it "Philaletheians" [U]), Analogeticists (some editions "Analogists" [U]) and Eclectic Theosophists [M for the gist; U for the section number and spellings].
- Ammonius (died about 242, Alexandria, nicknamed Saccas) taught Plotinus for about eleven years from 232 [M]. He wrote nothing. Porphyry reports that Plotinus, Herennius and an Origen (the pagan Platonist, not the Christian) agreed not to divulge his teachings, which later readers took for a secret doctrine [M]. Hierocles of Alexandria, through Photius, calls him "God-taught" and says he reconciled Plato and Aristotle [M].
- The "Eclectic Theosophical School" label is an eighteenth-century church historians' label (Brucker and Mosheim are the names associated with it [U]). Blavatsky's picture of an eclectic reconciler of all religions comes from them and from her friend Alexander Wilder, who edited Isis Unveiled [M]. Historians say little is known of Ammonius's teaching and that "Neoplatonism" is a modern label [M]. The story that Ammonius founded a Theosophical school has no ancient support [M]. Treat the lineage as her story.
- She also says Diogenes Laertius traces a theosophic system to an Egyptian priest called Pot-Amun [U]. What Diogenes Laertius (Lives, Prologue 21) does say is that Potamo of Alexandria introduced an eclectic school a short time ago; scholars read Potamo as a Greek name, so the Egyptian etymology is hers [M]. Do not repeat Pot-Amun.
- "Theosophy" also names older things (section 8.1). In the essay write "the Theosophical Society" or "Blavatsky's Theosophy" in full [M].

## 3. Her books as comparative-religion works

### 3.1 Isis Unveiled (1877), the first comparing

- Two volumes, J. W. Bouton, New York, 1877. Full title A Master-Key to the Mysteries of Ancient and Modern Science and Theology. Volume 1, Science, attacks materialist science, argues that the ancients knew nature and force better than moderns allow, and treats magic and Spiritualist phenomena. Volume 2, Theology, attacks Church dogma (Rome and the Jesuits above all) and argues that Christian doctrine and ritual were borrowed from older systems, with Gnostics, Kabbalists and Neoplatonists as the true heirs [M]. Written in New York with Olcott's help [M]. Chapter titles, and a Montaigne motto on the title page ("Cecy est un livre de bonne foy"), are [U].
- Her aim in the Preface, in gist: a plea for recognising the Hermetic philosophy, the anciently universal Wisdom-Religion, as the key to the Absolute in science and theology [M for the gist; U for "only possible" and the capital A]. Handle: "a plea for the recognition of the Hermetic philosophy" [handle, M]. "Wisdom-Religion" is already used in this Preface (the claim that it first appears here is a priority claim nobody can support). Later near-synonyms: Esoteric Philosophy, Secret Doctrine, Archaic Wisdom, Archaic Doctrine, Theosophia, Gupta Vidya (Sanskrit, secret knowledge), Brahma Vidya (an Upanishadic term, also used by Besant), and Bodhism or Bodhi Dharma [M for the names; U for exact sources; "Isis Unveiled onward" and "Bodhism in Isis" are U]. The Traditionalists later say primordial tradition [M].
- Examples are mostly Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, Gnostic and Christian, with Brahmanism and Buddhism as older versions of Christian doctrines. Parallels she cites: Hermes and Egypt, Pythagoras, Plato and the Neoplatonists, Plotinus, Kabbalah (the Zohar, a "Chaldean Book of Numbers"), Gnostics, Brahmanism, Buddhism [M].
- The veil. The title takes up the old image of Isis whose veil no mortal lifts. Plutarch (On Isis and Osiris, about section 9, 354C) says the inscription was on the statue at Sais of Athena, whom the Egyptians identify with Isis, and his word is "robe" (peplos), not "veil" [M]. "Veil" is later: Kant quotes the inscription in a footnote inside section 49 of the Critique of Judgment (Academy edition about 5:316, mentioning Segner's vignette) [M, about 85 percent], and Proclus on the Timaeus gives a longer form ending "and the fruit I bore was the sun" [M]. Do not tell the reader Plutarch wrote "veil". The veil of Isis is a Western precedent for Alex's veil, older than the Vedanta words [M].
- Rebirth in 1877. She rejected the Spiritist (Kardec) doctrine of reincarnation and allowed a return to earth only in exceptional cases. She read Pythagorean and Platonic metempsychosis as the soul's passage through states, not a return to animal bodies, and spoke of a "cycle of necessity" (the phrase is hers; see section 3.2.6). By 1888 she taught reincarnation of the individuality as the rule [M]. This is the finding of Julie Chajes, Recycled Lives (Oxford University Press, 2019) and of the Theosophy Forward article "Madame Blavatsky's Two Doctrines of Reincarnation" [C]. See section 4.4.
- Consequence for the draft: the Part two sentence that Isis Unveiled (1877) and The Secret Doctrine (1888) were "one of the main routes" by which English readers met karma and reincarnation is too wide for Isis. Better: Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism (1883), the Key (1889), the Secret Doctrine, and the Theosophical writers after them. It was not the only route: Edwin Arnold's The Light of Asia (1879), Olcott's Buddhist Catechism (1881), Max Muller's Sacred Books of the East (from 1879) and Kardec's French Spiritism (1857) were others. An English version of Kardec by Anna Blackwell is recalled at about 1875 [U] [M for the rest].
- Coleman (section 9.2) traced a large share of Isis to nineteenth-century books. His figures (roughly two thousand passages from about a hundred books; about 1,400 books cited and about 100 actually used) are [U] and not for printing. Sources he stressed for Isis: Eliphas Levi, Hargrave Jennings, Des Mousseaux, Mackenzie's Royal Masonic Cyclopaedia, Godfrey Higgins, Edward Moor, Max Muller [M].

### 3.2 The Secret Doctrine (1888)

3.2.1 Structure

- Two volumes, The Theosophical Publishing Company, London, late 1888 (volume 1 about October, volume 2 about December [U]). Subtitle The Synthesis of Science, Religion and Philosophy. The subtitle is her own name for her method: synthesis [M].
- Volume 1, Cosmogenesis: Preface, Introductory, the Proem, Part I Cosmic Evolution (seven Stanzas from the Book of Dzyan, each with commentary), Part II The Evolution of Symbolism (the mundane egg, the days and nights of Brahma, lotus, tree, serpent, the creative gods), and a part setting the teaching against science [M]. How the science part divides between the volumes is [U].
- Volume 2, Anthropogenesis: Part I, twelve Stanzas on the origin of man (root races, Lemuria, Atlantis); Part II, The Archaic Symbolism of the World-Religions (Genesis, the Fall, Adam Kadmon, the Kabbalistic scheme); addenda [M].
- A third volume was made from her papers after her death under Annie Besant (1897 [U]). Some Theosophical bodies (the United Lodge of Theosophists and the Point Loma lineage, as recalled) do not accept it as her own arrangement [M]. A revised third edition of 1893 (Besant and Mead) and a six-volume reissue of 1897 also exist [U].
- Structure of the argument: the One, manifestation, the pilgrimage of the many, return [M].

3.2.2 The Preface: aim and stance

- She states the aim: to show that Nature is no chance concurrence of atoms, to place man in the scheme of the universe, to rescue from degradation the archaic truths that are the basis of all religions, and to uncover, to some extent, the fundamental unity from which they all spring. Handle: "the fundamental unity from which they all spring" [handle, about 85 percent].
- She disclaims the role of revealer. What she gives lies scattered through thousands of volumes of Asiatic and early European scripture, hidden under glyph and symbol, and she tries to gather "the oldest tenets" into "one harmonious and unbroken whole" [handles]. The book is a partial statement of what she was taught by more advanced students, with a few details of her own [M].
- The nosegay. She calls the book a nosegay of culled flowers with only the string her own. The line is Montaigne's (Essais III.12, "Of Physiognomy"; French "n'y ayant fourny du mien que le filet a les lier"); she quotes it [M]. So the essay may say "Blavatsky quotes Montaigne's nosegay" and must not give the line to her. Her place for it is the Preface of the Secret Doctrine volume 1, about p. vii [U]. Florio's English Montaigne says "thread"; whether her printing says "string" or "thread" is [U]. The "string" in her case is the Masters' system [M].
- A sentence recalled from the same Preface, "the most useful single sentence of hers for Alex" if it checks out: the teachings belong neither to the Hindu, the Zoroastrian, the Chaldean nor the Egyptian religion, neither to Buddhism, Islam, Judaism nor Christianity exclusively, and the Secret Doctrine is the essence of all of these [handle: "belong neither to the Hindu, the Zoroastrian, the Chaldean"; U, locator unknown]. Check it first.

3.2.3 The Introductory: one religion behind all, and an archive

- The Introductory says the Secret Doctrine was the "universally diffused religion of the ancient and prehistoric world" [handle, about 80 percent; page unknown]. 3.2.6 Her glosses on the propositions

- On proposition 1 she uses Vedantic terms [M for terms and roles; U for exact wording]. The Absolute is Parabrahm, the field of Absolute Consciousness, out of all relation to conditioned existence. Once thought leaves it, duality enters: Spirit (Consciousness) and Matter, subject and object, which are not independent realities but two aspects of the Absolute. The Great Breath is precosmic ideation, source of force and individual consciousness. Mulaprakriti, precosmic root-substance, underlies all objective planes and is presented as the veil of Parabrahm (the word "veil" is U). Maya is her word for the manifested universe: all is temporary in it, from a firefly's life to the Sun's [gist M].
- Two honesty points. Her Absolute has two aspects, Spirit and Matter, so it is not plain Advaita; her adviser T. Subba Row, a Madras Advaitin, is said to have disagreed with parts of her scheme [U]. And her Maya means "temporary and changing", which agrees with the repository notes that maya and mithya mean "dependent" more than "illusion" [M].
- On proposition 2 she names the absolute universality of the law of periodicity: flux and reflux, day and night, waking and sleeping, life and death, which she says physical science already records. For the cosmos the periods are Manvantara (manifestation) and Pralaya (rest), the days and nights of Brahma, with Puranic figures drawn from Wilson's Vishnu Purana [M]. Handle: "the law of periodicity". Numbers are in section 4.8.
- On proposition 3, why the one becomes many. The spark of the Over-Soul, the Monad (Atma joined to Buddhi), cannot have conscious independent existence until it has passed through every elemental form of the phenomenal world in that Manvantara and gained individuality, first by natural impulse and then by its own efforts, checked by its karma. It rises through every degree of intelligence from mineral and plant to the highest angelic rank [M]. (She writes Dhyani-Buddha for the top rank; Dhyan-Chohan is her usual word for the rank, and Dhyani-Buddha is a Tibetan Buddhist term for the five transcendent Buddhas, so the equation is hers [U].) The pivotal doctrine, she says, admits no privileges or special gifts in man, save those won by his own Ego through personal effort and merit across a long series of metempsychoses and reincarnations. Handle: "no privileges or special gifts" [about 85 percent]. In short: the One becomes many so that each spark can become self-conscious by its own work, then the many return [M].
- This answers what Alex's notes call the unargued premise, why a whole would experience separation at all. He can offer it as one tradition's answer without adopting it. "No privileges, only what effort wins" fits Part one's agency theme [M].
- The idea that "unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity" is in the spirit of this passage but the phrase itself is unplaced; it may be a later writer's paraphrase. Do not quote it; quote the "no privileges" passage instead [U].
- The phrase "Cycle of Necessity" is hers. Herodotus 2.123 (Egyptians say the soul passes through land, sea and air creatures and into a human body again after 3,000 years) does not contain it. For the Greek side the Orphic "wheel of birth" and Empedocles' "decree of Necessity" are candidates, and whether she means either is [U]. Do not give the phrase a Greek or Egyptian source.

3.2.7 The Stanzas of Dzyan

- Volume 1 is built on seven Stanzas, volume 2 on twelve, said to be translated from the Book of Dzyan, written in Senzar, a secret priestly tongue, and called the first volume of commentaries on the Seven Secret Folios of Kiu-te (read as Tibetan rgyud, tantra). Stanza I, as recalled, has the Eternal Parent slumbering for seven eternities; Stanza III has the lotus bud [M].

Critics read the Stanzas as her own composition from nineteenth-century sources (Coleman); Theosophists hold them translated [M]. 3.2.8 Symbol as the shared language, and her equations

- Both big books treat symbols as the common language of the religions: egg, circle and point, triangle, cross, lotus, tree, serpent, the number seven. Her guide is analogy, "as above, so below" [M].
- Equations, with gist [M] and each particular [U]:

  - The Unmanifest: Parabrahm, the Kabbalistic En Soph, and the Rigveda hymn of creation, which she cites in English translation.

3.2.9 Why the religions differ today (her five parts)

1. Every religion has an outer form for the many and an inner teaching for the few; disagreement lies in the outer form (the master idea). 2. Symbols were read literally: allegory became history. 3. The keys were lost: the Mysteries closed, initiates withdrew, priestcraft kept the husk. 4. Adaptation: in each age a teacher restates the one teaching in terms that age can take (Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster and Jesus are read this way). 5. Cycles of decline: in the dark age (Kali Yuga) the inner teaching shrinks [M for the pattern; locators unknown]. Her examples attack church Christianity and the Hebrew God as lesser beings. ## 4. Her teaching: the one, the veil, lives, karma, after death, stages and cosmic scale

This section gives her teaching and the core Theosophical system built on it. Where a point belongs to Sinnett, Besant, Leadbeater or Bailey and not to Blavatsky, it says so. Their own books are in section 5.

### 4.1 The one and the many

- One boundless Principle stands behind everything, with no personal face. Her Eastern name is Parabrahm; she sets it beside the Kabbalistic En Soph (the Boundless) and the Rigveda hymn of creation. These are different concepts and she equates them [M]. The Absolute is beyond definition. From within the One the differentiated universe appears. Spirit and Matter are aspects of the One, not independent realities [M].
- Individual souls are rays or sparks of the Universal Soul (the Over-Soul). The Monad (Atma joined to Buddhi) descends into matter, individuates through a long chain of personalities and returns [M]. Atma is no personal possession; it is the one universal Spirit, with Buddhi its vehicle [M]. So separateness belongs to the vehicles [M].
- The Key states this in plain form [M]; the Secret Doctrine's third proposition states it in short (section 3.2.5). Both are received teaching, not derived. Alex derives it and can say so [M].
- Why the one becomes many: unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity only through experience, by passing through separation under its own effort (the "no privileges" passage, section 3.2.6). Whether the "experience makes it self-conscious" argument is in the Key itself or only in the Secret Doctrine is [U].
- The Letters' Cosmological Notes teach that spirit and matter are two states of one element, that the differentiated universe appears from within the One, and that every being is a ray of the One Life, not a separate being. They distinguish the Absolute from the manifested first Logos and from the Dhyan Chohans [M]. An 1880s English form of Alex's axiom, not a proof [M].
- The letter on God (section 6.3) puts the denial of a personal God on Buddhist grounds [M].

### 4.2 The veil, illusion, and the mask

- Maya, in her Glossary, is illusion in a particular sense: only what is changeless and eternal is real, and everything that changes, and so has a beginning and an end, is maya [M for the gist]. This makes "illusion" mean "impermanent", closer to the Advaita mithya than to "unreal". For Alex: the wall is real inside the system, and it is not final [M].
- Mulaprakriti, root-substance, is presented as the veil of Parabrahm. In Subba Row's Gita lectures a Logos arises through it like a reflection on a surface. A veil made of the One itself: the best early example, like walls made of the machine in Alex's computer picture. Advaita would call the root-matter maya or avidya, not a principle in its own right [M].
- The personality is a mask (persona) and the illusion is taking the mask for the whole [M]. The Voice has the disciple "at one" with the Soul-Thought of Nature (alaya), invincible; "in separation" he becomes the playground of samvriti, origin of delusions [gist M; Fragment I U]. Specialists: alaya-vijnana in Yogacara is an individual stream carrying karmic seeds, not a universal soul; the comparer equates what specialists keep apart [M]. Samvriti is the Buddhist conventional truth [M].
- "Mind is the great Slayer of the Real" (section 3.4) is the Voice's line for why separation feels real [M].
- The veil of Isis is the Western precedent (section 3.1).
- Besant treats consciousness as one Self limited by its vehicles, called sheaths (a Vedanta word); the sense of being separate comes from the sheath, not the Self. A Study in Consciousness (1904) and The Self and Its Sheaths (1895 [U]) [M for titles; U for what the first says on separateness]. The plainest Adyar statement of one consciousness experiencing separation through form [M].
- Bailey names three veils over the mind (maya, glamour, illusion), linked to the physical, emotional and mental levels [U]. Structure only; the dictated source is unverifiable.

### 4.3 Separateness as the error

- Light on the Path by Mabel Collins (London, Redway, 1885), Part I: "Kill out all sense of separateness" [about 85 percent for the words; rule number U]. As recalled, the following sentences say that you cannot stand aside from the bad or the foolish man, since they are yourself in a lesser degree than your friend or your master, and that holding the idea of separateness from a vile or silly person creates karma which binds you and that person together for many incarnations [U, about 70 percent, handle only]. If verified, this is the best nineteenth-century English line for Alex's fingers. It is Collins, not Blavatsky, and the book was said to be dictated by a Master [M].
- The phrase "the heresy of separateness" is common in Theosophical writing and credited to Blavatsky on many sites, with no locator. Four research files could not place it in her. It may be later Theosophical usage [U]. Possible leads, all [U]: Collins's line; the Voice's separation lines; and the Buddhist term sakkaya-ditthi, which older English rendered "heresy of individuality" (Childers 1875 and Rhys Davids, recalled at about 65 percent). If it is found in the Voice or the Key it is the best single phrase for what Alex's axiom says is false. Until then say "Theosophical writers" and no more.
- Brotherhood and duty in the Key: brotherhood is a fact in nature and not a sentiment; duty is what is owed to humanity, especially the poorer and more helpless; an unpaid debt leaves us spiritually insolvent in the next life; Theosophy is the quintessence of duty [recalled near-verbatim, M]. The root evil is separateness [gist M; the phrase for it U].
- Karma and unity: karma works for families, nations and humanity as well as individuals; humanity is one, so harm to one is harm to all; "he is paying for past sins" is no excuse for refusing help, and the one who refuses makes his own karma [M for collective karma; U for the help passage and for 1 Corinthians 12:26].

### 4.4 Lives

Her doctrine and its date.
- In Isis Unveiled (1877) she rejected Kardec's Spiritist reincarnation of the personality and allowed rebirth only in exceptional cases. By the Secret Doctrine (1888) and the Key (1889) the Ego (individuality) reincarnates over many lives and the personality does not, with kama-loka and devachan between lives [M; reported by Chajes, section 9 [C]].
- Chajes argues the rule was built up in the early 1880s (about 1880 to 1882 [U]) in dialogue with Spiritualist and Spiritist progressive reincarnationism, Kingsford and Maitland (The Perfect Way, 1882), Sinnett (Esoteric Buddhism, 1883) and Arnold, and not as an import from India [M for the thesis; details U]. Later Theosophical claims that Isis was misread and always meant the Eastern doctrine exist; how Chajes weighs them is [U].
- Say Theosophists adopted reincarnation and argued for it by reason; do not say they brought it from the East [M]. Her Eastern sources are real, but Indian rebirth is mostly a bond to be ended, and the Theosophical version is a ladder of growth. Not purely: Bhagavad Gita 6.41 to 45 has the yogi who falls short reborn to resume ("perfected through many births", 6.45), and Buddhism has the stream-enterer with at most seven rebirths to go. Cumulative progress across lives is in the Indian texts too; what is nineteenth-century and Western is the evolutionary frame [M; open the verses before quoting].
- Alex's arrow has the ladder shape that resembles the Spiritist and Theosophical progressive rebirth; do not claim Theosophy supports his scale [M].

What carries over.
- A person has seven principles (section 4.7). The lower four dissolve. The upper three (Atma, Buddhi, Higher Manas) continue. What continues is the individuality, the Ego. What does not is the personality, the mask of one life, with its name and memories [M].
- The Ego assimilates the spiritual essence of each life. A life with no spiritual aspiration leaves almost nothing to assimilate. The Ego sends a ray (lower manas) into each new personality [M]. A rare wholly material life may sever the lower mind from the Ego, so the individuality survives but that personality is lost [U]. This is the Theosophical form of "the account survives, the session closes", and it answers Alex's worry that the whole rigs the run: only a life fails, not the individuality [M].
- Terminology traps. Her Ego is the lasting self; Alex's ego is her personality. Do not tie "a self that is built and can end" to her [M]. Prefer "rebirth" and "transmigration" to "reincarnation" in the essay; do not use "Ego" in her sense without defining it [M].

Why rebirth (her reasons, by reason).
- Unequal starts need a cause; heredity cannot account for prodigies or sibling differences; one life is too short; karma carries over, so it is just [M]. These do not depend on any text and are the arguments Alex already uses. They were already Kardec's, collected by E. D. Walker (1888) [M].

Why we do not remember.
- Memory belongs to the brain of the personality, and the new brain is new. We do not remember most of this life either (early childhood, a given day). Experience persists as faculty and character; the Ego keeps the harvest, not the autobiography. In ordinary life the Ego's voice shows as conscience, intuition, talent and character; full recall comes with high development [M]. Alex can use the childhood comparison and credit her [M]. Whether she cites Lethe or Virgil is [U].

No return into animals.
- A human Ego does not return in an animal body. Pythagorean and Platonic metempsychosis is read as a long passage through states, or allegory; Hindu and Buddhist animal-rebirth stories are read as popular distortion, in part the transmigration of life-atoms [M; an 1886 Theosophist article U]. Scholars: the Pali canon lists the animal realm among destinies and Chandogya 5.10.7 gives the womb of a dog or a pig for foul conduct, so mainstream scholars say the texts mean it; Kardec also rejected animal rebirth; the rejection is a nineteenth-century progressive taste [M].

The interval.
- Devachan is recalled at ten to fifteen centuries on average [U]. Do not state it as doctrine. If used, call it Theosophy's. The number of Egos in a cycle is said to be fixed, so population growth means shorter intervals [U].

The skandhas and no-self.
- The Key and Glossary present the five skandhas (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) as the attributes of a personality that become the karmic germs of the next life. She reads the Buddha's no-self as a denial of the personal self only, with a higher individuality behind it [M]. Most Buddhologists read the khandhas as an analysis of a person and the Buddha as denying any lasting self. The Milindapanha compares continuity to a flame passed from lamp to lamp. The "higher Ego bearing the skandhas" is hers [M]. Say "she reads", never "Buddhism teaches" [M].

The thread of lives.
- Her circle's image is the sutratman (thread-soul) on which lives are strung like beads [M]. In Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7 (the Antaryamin passage) the thread (sutra) on which this world, the other world and all beings are strung is the wind (vayu): it links all beings at one time, not one being's successive lives [M]. The word sutratman is later Vedanta, and the Theosophical use of it as a thread through lives is a reassignment. Who brought it into Theosophy (Subba Row or her Glossary) is [U]. Gita 7.7, all this strung on me like rows of gems on a thread (sutre manigana iva), is the nearer image for Alex [M]. If Alex's figure uses "the thread", say it is a Theosophical image with a Vedanta word [M].

Witnesses the Theosophists cite for rebirth, and the counter-evidence the files note (Walker 1888; Head and Cranston 1961 and 1977; Besant; Judge). All [M] unless marked.
- Greek and Roman: Pythagoras and the beaten dog (Xenophanes, DK 21 B7); Empedocles (DK 31 B117); Plato's Meno 81, Phaedo (cycles of opposites), Phaedrus 246 to 249 (periods of a thousand years between lives; return after ten thousand years, or three thousand for the lover of wisdom, 248e to 249b), Republic 10 (the Myth of Er), Timaeus 41 to 42, Laws 903 to 905 (the whole caring for the whole); Plotinus Enneads 3.2; Virgil Aeneid 6. The Myth of Er is myth; the comparers read it as doctrine. Whether Greek rebirth came from India is disputed; many say it arose independently.
- Egyptian and Druid: Herodotus 2.123 (cited as the Egyptian "cycle of necessity"; Egyptologists find no Egyptian text with this cycle); Caesar, Gallic War 6.14, Lucan 1.454 to 458, Diodorus 5.28 (outsiders' reports who may have read Pythagoras into what they saw). Cite Caesar and Lucan as "Greek and Roman writers report"; skip Egypt or flag Herodotus.
- Jewish: Josephus, War 2.163 and Antiquities 18.14 describe the Pharisees' belief, not a general Jewish doctrine; War 2.154 to 158 gives the Essenes a Platonic pre-existence, not return; War 3.374 to 375 is a speech Josephus puts in his own mouth. Kabbalah: gilgul in the Bahir (12th century), the Zohar, Isaac Luria and Hayyim Vital (souls return to repair, are rooted in the limbs of Adam; ibbur, dibbuk). Saadia Gaon (933) rejected transmigration. Scholem treats gilgul as a medieval development. Luria's limbs of Adam are the fingers in Jewish form. Philo's locators are U.
- Christian and Gnostic: Matthew 11:14 and 17:10 to 13 (Elijah and John), paired with the counter-text John 1:21 and Luke 1:17; John 9:2; John 3:3; Galatians 6:7 (sowing and reaping, an agricultural proverb about moral consequence); Revelation 3:12 and James 3:6 [M]; Hebrews 9:27 is the opposing text. Origen taught pre-existence and final restoration, and as recalled rejected transmigration (Commentary on Matthew 13.1), so Theosophists cite him for a reading he did not hold [M]. Do not use Jerome (Letter 130): as recalled the passage condemns the doctrine and the anthologists print hostile testimony as approval [U]; do not use even the anthologists' Jerome line as "Jerome says". Gnostics: Carpocratians (Irenaeus 1.25.4), Pistis Sophia (Mead 1896), Apocryphon of John; Manichaeans and Cathars [M].
- Islamic: Rumi's lines on dying as mineral, becoming plant, animal, man (Masnavi III, about 3901 to 3906 in Nicholson's numbering; scholars read them as ascent); the Druze teach taqammus. Mainstream theology rejects tanasukh. These circulate in loose English versions: quote none without a named translator and a numbered verse [M; that Theosophists cite Rumi is U].
- Hindu and Buddhist: Gita 2.13 and 2.22; Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 5 (the caterpillar; "as you act so you become"); Chandogya 5.10; Katha 1.1.6; the Tibetan Bardo Thodol, whose English title "The Tibetan Book of the Dead" is Evans-Wentz's (1927), modelled on the Egyptian one; the Tibetan title is roughly "Liberation through Hearing in the Intermediate State". Evans-Wentz was himself linked to Theosophy (joined the Society about 1901). Early schools disagreed on an interim state (Sarvastivada yes, Theravada no) [M]. The Gita 2.22 as "the standard reincarnation text" for Judge and Besant is [U].
- The councils device. Judge, Besant and later Theosophists say the Church condemned rebirth at the Council of 553 and "abolished the spirit" at the Council of 869. Historians find no council condemning rebirth by name. The 553 anathemas concern Origen's pre-existence and final restoration (the fifteen Origenist anathemas are usually placed in a preliminary session or the 543 synod [U]); the 869 canon (Constantinople IV, canon 11) rejected the view that a man has two souls [M]. Do not repeat the claim. "The sixth-century condemnations of Origenism" is the safe phrase.

### 4.5 Karma

- For Blavatsky karma is a law of harmony, not of punishment [M]. No judge administers it. We reward and punish ourselves [M].
- The Secret Doctrine passage. Volume 1, near the end, perhaps p. 643 of the 1888 edition [U]; which Part or Section is unknown. In gist: the one decree of Karma is absolute harmony in the world of matter and in the world of spirit; so it is not Karma that rewards or punishes but we who reward or punish ourselves, according as we work with Nature or break her laws of harmony [wording M, page U]. Handles: "absolute Harmony in the world of matter", "we who reward or punish ourselves". She names the law Karma-Nemesis, ties it to Nemesis and Adrasteia, and names Providence, Fatalism and Chance as the three ways people misread it [M]. A related sentence, as recalled: the ways of Karma would not be inscrutable "were men to work in union and harmony, instead of disunion and strife" [gist only; wording unverified]. If verified, this is the nearest nineteenth-century line to "something I did to another part of myself", with no judge. Public domain, so it can be quoted in full once the printed page is open.
- Page conflict. An earlier brief gave p. 634 for the "ultimate Law" wording; three files recall the harmony passage at p. 643. 634 and 643 may be two different sentences in two books and not a transposition. Open both [U].
- The bough sentence. A recalled sentence runs: Karma creates nothing nor does it design; it is man who plans and creates causes, and Karmic law adjusts the effects, which adjustment is not an act but universal harmony, tending ever to resume its original position, like a bough which, bent down too forcibly, rebounds. One file places it in the Key's karma section; another places it in the Secret Doctrine passage above [U, about 70 percent]. Two memories disagree. Do not use it.
- The Key's karma section [M for gist; wording U]. Karma is the unerring law which adjusts effect to cause on the physical, mental and spiritual planes of being (handle; "spiritual" not "moral" [U]). It is no person and does not reward or punish. Man plans and makes the causes and the law adjusts the effects. We weave our destiny thread by thread like a spider's web. It is not fate. She calls it the ultimate law of the universe; handles "the ultimate Law of the Universe, the source, origin and fount of all other laws" [U for which book carries it] and "the one Universal Law" guiding "unerringly, and, so to say, blindly" all other laws [U]. Public domain, quote in full once checked.
- Glossary, "Karma": see section 3.5.
- Her Greek link, Nemesis, is a stretch: Nemesis is a goddess of retribution for hubris, a personal agent. Better Greek parallels for equilibrium are Anaximander (things pay penalty to one another, DK 12 B1) and Heraclitus B94 [M, added by a checker].
- Lords of Karma. In later Adyar writing the Lords of Karma appear as beings who allot a person's lot. The contrast "her karma is impersonal, the Lords are later" is too neat: she already writes of the Lipika as recorders of karma, and later Theosophical glossaries equate them with Lords of Karma. The safe part is "impersonal law of harmony" [M; the origin of the phrase "Lords of Karma" as mostly Besant, Leadbeater, then Bailey and the "I AM" family is U]. Do not put a judge or a board into Alex's karma and do not cite her for one.
- Besant, Karma (1895), probably uses the Hindu three-way division (stored, ripening, being made now) and a "parallelogram of forces" image [U].
- Karma works by the ethical test: the Voice judges the two paths by whether they leave the world to suffer (section 4.9) [M].

### 4.6 The states after death

The system, in the Key and Sinnett, built from the Letters (and not a Hindu or Buddhist doctrine in this form) [M].
- The body dissolves; prana returns to the common store; the astral double fades. The desire body goes to kama-loka (the place of desire), a semi-material plane, and disintegrates (the "second death"). The remnants are "shells", which she says mediums meet at seances. This was the Masters' reason for saying that spirit communications are not the dead. Suicides and the violently killed linger [U].
- The Ego then rests in devachan, a dreamlike subjective state in which the best of the last life is fulfilled, with those it loved present in the dream. It compensates for unmerited suffering. Its length follows the spiritual energy of the life. It is not a place and not eternal [M]. Thirst for life draws the Ego back; the karmic tendencies (the skandhas) ripen into a new personality fitted to them [M]. The undeveloped return sooner [M].
- The word. "Devachan" is her reading of Tibetan bde-ba-can as deva plus chan, "dwelling of the gods". Tibetan bde-ba-can is the Tibetan rendering of Sanskrit sukhavati, not "the word behind" it. Her spelling is a Sanskritizing misreading [M for direction; U for exact Tibetan spellings (bde-ba-can against bde-ba-chen)]. A Pure Land is a place of practice on the way to awakening, reached by the Buddha's vow, from which one does not fall back; it is no rest before the next birth. The equation is a comparer's move [M]. Devachan resembles the Hindu svarga, a heaven of merit with a return [M].
- Better witnesses for "rest, then return": Gita 9.21 (those who enjoyed the wide heaven return to the world of mortals when merit is spent), and the Hindu two paths (Chandogya 5.10, Brihadaranyaka 6.2; the parallel passage is Chandogya 5.3 to 10, with the two paths at 5.10) [M, added by checkers]. Use "devachan" only as Theosophy's name for it.
- Theravada rejected an intermediate state between death and rebirth (Kathavatthu, recalled as VIII.2 [U]); the Sarvastivada accepted one (antarabhava) [M].
- She compares kama-loka to Catholic purgatory [U that she does], the Tibetan bardo, and seances (rejected). Leadbeater later wrote detailed reports (The Astral Plane, The Devachanic Plane, The Other Side of Death) from claimed clairvoyance; not evidence, only witness to the equation habit [M].
- Use: "the soul continues between lives" has a detailed Theosophical form, but it is a dream-rest, and seance "spirits" are shells in her system. Keep the claim light [M].

### 4.7 The seven principles

- Man is sevenfold, in her list: sthula sharira (physical body), prana (life), linga sharira (astral double), kama (desire), manas (mind, higher and lower), buddhi (spiritual soul), atma (spirit) [M]. Sinnett's English names in Esoteric Buddhism (1883): body, vitality, astral body, animal soul, human soul, spiritual soul, spirit. Sinnett's Sanskrit for the body may be rupa, with sthula sarira as the later usage [U].
- The scheme reached print in Sinnett's "Fragments of Occult Truth" in The Theosophist (from October 1881, signed "a Lay Chela", attributed to Hume or Sinnett [U]) before the book. Isis Unveiled (1877) had a threefold man [M]. The septenary man and the seven planes entered Theosophy in 1881 to 1883 through Sinnett and the Letters [M].
- She sets it beside other systems: the Vedantic five sheaths (annamaya, pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, anandamaya, with the atman beyond; Taittiriya Upanishad, Brahmananda Valli [M]); Taraka Raja Yoga with three upadhis (sthula, sukshma, karana) plus Atma; and a Kabbalistic column (nephesh, ruach, neshamah, chayyah, yechidah) often matched in Theosophical writing but not placed in the Key [U].
- Scholars: Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold scheme; her buddhi and manas do not match the Sanskrit senses (buddhi is the discriminating intellect, not a "spiritual soul", though buddhi and Buddha share the root budh, "to awaken or know" [M]). Subba Row, the Brahmin scholar in her circle, preferred a smaller Vedantic count and the two disagreed in print [M gist, U detail]. Subba Row's logic: the number of divisions depends on how finely you cut (section 6.7).
- For Alex: traditions count the layers of a person as three, four, five or seven and say one thing, that a core exists which is not the passing self. Do not print the seven as Vedanta; they are "the Theosophists' own count" [M].

### 4.8 Cosmic scale: cycles, globes, planes, hierarchies

Layering matters. Blavatsky (1888 to 1891) supplies the Breath, the cycles, the chain of globes and rounds, the Dhyani hierarchy, three Logoi, the Voice and the Great Renunciation, building on Sinnett and the Letters. Besant and Leadbeater (about 1895 to 1925) supply the named seven planes, the planetary and solar Logos hierarchy, the five initiations with Pali, Hindu and Christian labels, and the clairvoyant root-race history. Bailey (1922 to 1949, with posthumous volumes to 1960) supplies nine initiations, the cosmic planes and the paths beyond the Master [M]. Theosophy built one of the most elaborate numbered maps of stages in the modern West ("largest" cannot be supported) [M].

The Great Breath, manvantara and pralaya.
- She calls the periodic out-breath and in-breath of the unmanifest the Great Breath. A manvantara is a period of manifestation, a pralaya a period of rest, and she applies both at every scale: a round, a chain, a solar system, a universe [M].
- The numbers are Puranic. A day of Brahma (kalpa) is 1,000 mahayugas, 4.32 billion years. A manvantara is 71 mahayugas, about 306.7 million years. Fourteen of them plus their fifteen junctions (each a krita-yuga length, 0.4 of a mahayuga, so 6 in all) fill a kalpa (14 x 71 + 6 = 1,000). The life of Brahma is 100 divine years, 311.04 trillion years. A day and a night of Brahma are 8.64 billion years. A checker verified the arithmetic in code; the Puranic source is [M]. The Puranas list four dissolutions: naimittika, prakritika, atyantika, nitya (Wilson's English "occasional, elemental, absolute, constant" is U; "final" for atyantika is a gloss). A named translation is needed before print.
- Sources: Vishnu Purana in Wilson's English (1840) [M]. Locators unknown.
- Use: a safe source for "even plus four may be a stage": a whole universe is one breath, followed by another. The Puranas also have the plural-in-space version (many universes at once, each with its own Brahma), so both precedents exist [M].

The globes and rounds: the axiom in cosmic form.
- The human Monad travels a chain of seven globes (A to G); one circuit is a Round. Globes A, B and C are the arc of descent (spirit into matter), D, our Earth, is the lowest point, and E, F and G the arc of ascent. There are seven Rounds, and we are in the fourth, past its midpoint: the turning point. The return is not a repeat of the start: the being comes back a conscious god where it began an unconscious one [M].
- The Letters and Esoteric Buddhism give a V-shaped journey: the life-wave goes down into denser matter in the first half and back toward spirit in the second; the nadir is the middle of the fourth round on the fourth globe, our Earth. The soul gains self-consciousness on the way down and carries it back up [M].
- Source layering: the scheme appears in the Letters (private, 1881 to 1883) and in Sinnett's Theosophist articles (1881 onward [U]), in book form in Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (1883); the Secret Doctrine widens it. "First in book form" is the safe wording [M].
- Use: the nearest nineteenth-century cosmic version of Alex's three clauses: one whole, separation, return. Return with consciousness gained answers "why separate at all". Hindu going out and returning (pravritti, nivritti) is a parallel; Neoplatonic procession and return (Plotinus, Proclus) is a comparer's pairing, not hers [M].

The planes.
- The septenary man and seven planes entered Theosophy in 1881 to 1883 through Sinnett and the Letters. In the Secret Doctrine she uses seven principles and planes with cautions that they are states, not places [M; cautions U]. The standard named list (physical, astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic or anupadaka, logoic or adi) is the Besant and Leadbeater systematisation, in its final form Leadbeater's; Besant's Ancient Wisdom (1897) lists seven planes with older names for the top three (nirvanic, paranirvanic, mahaparanirvanic [U]); Leadbeater, Man Visible and Invisible (1902) and A Textbook of Theosophy (1912) [M lineage; U which book carries which name]. Say "the Theosophical list of planes", not "Blavatsky's".
- The buddhic plane is called the plane of unity, where the sense of separation drops away. Buddhi (discernment) and Buddha share the root budh, but the word does not mean "Buddha" and the equation is theirs [M]. In Theosophical terms the plane of unity is where Alex's axiom is lived: the sense of "another" is what goes [M].
- Guenon argued that Theosophy misread the Vedantic constitution of man and the sheaths (Le Theosophisme, 1921; L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta, 1925, where the sheath passage is U); L'Erreur spirite (1923) is against Spiritism and reincarnationism [M].

Root races and sub-races (a problem; leave it out).
- Volume 2 teaches seven root races per globe period, each with seven sub-races. The first two are ethereal, the Third (Lemurian) gets mind and solid bodies, the Fourth (Atlantean) is the peak of materiality, the Fifth (Aryan) is ours, two more follow. Later writers made it concrete: Scott-Elliot (The Story of Atlantis, 1896; The Lost Lemuria, 1904) mapped continents; Besant and Leadbeater (Man: Whence, How and Whither, 1913) gave a clairvoyant history and a coming sixth sub-race [M; every locator U].
- The scheme ranks peoples. Blavatsky treats some living peoples as degenerate remnants fated to die out and puts the Aryan highest among the living; apes are described as degenerate descendants of mindless humans; Semites as a later and in some respects lower offshoot [M for the scheme; U for the passages on Semites and apes]. Her "Aryan" is not the German racists' race: she counts Indians and Persians as Aryans [M]. Max Muller said late in life that "Aryan" was a linguistic term and "Aryan blood" nonsense [M gist].
- Defenders (James Santucci) say her races are stages every soul passes through, and that she attacked colonial racism, and note that the Society's first Object was brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour. Critics say the hierarchy stands in the text regardless [M]. Say both things. The content is unacceptable now; a writer who touches it should say so briefly. If Theosophy is introduced as a stage-mapper, one sentence naming this as the part scholars find hardest to defend would be honest. Later misuse is in section 9 [M].

Hierarchies and Logoi.
- Between the Absolute and humanity Blavatsky places Dhyan-Chohans, planetary spirits and builders, who work by law and are not personal gods; some are said to be perfected humanity of earlier cycles [M; "her own wording" U, the idea also in the Letters and Esoteric Buddhism U]. She uses "Logos" at three levels: unmanifest, the Spirit-Matter pair, manifested universal Mind. The Vedantic scaffolding (Parabrahman, Mulaprakriti, Logos, Pranava) came from T. Subba Row's Bhagavad Gita lectures [M].
- Besant and Leadbeater then built tiers: a Solar Logos (the conscious life of a solar system); seven Planetary Logoi, read into the "seven spirits before the throne" of Revelation; for Earth, a Lord of the World (Sanat Kumara, the Ancient of Days) heading a Hierarchy of Manu, Bodhisattva or World Teacher, Mahachohan, the ray Chohans and the Masters; and a cosmic level left in shadow [M]. Sources: Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897); Leadbeater, A Textbook of Theosophy (1912) and The Masters and the Path (1925). Parallels drawn: Greek Logos (Heraclitus, Stoics, Philo, John 1), Hindu Ishvara and Om, Daniel 7, Revelation 1:4, Sanat Kumara in Chandogya Upanishad ch. 7 [M]. Whether Sanat Kumara equals the Planetary Logos is [U] (the authors differ). This is the planetary, solar, cosmic ladder in plain form and mostly the later layer; do not write "she says" for the tiers.
- Alice Bailey (1880 to 1949), writing for "the Tibetan" (Djwhal Khul), held that a planet's Logos is a "Heavenly Man" whose cells are humans, and that our seven planes are the seven sub-planes of one cosmic physical plane, with six higher cosmic planes above (cosmic astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic, logoic). The pattern repeats at each scale [M]. Sources: A Treatise on Cosmic Fire (1925); Initiation, Human and Solar (1922). Star details (Sirius, the Pleiades, the Great Bear) are U. The strongest statement of a self-similar ladder, each scale's whole being one plane of a larger scale. It resembles Alex's small square inside a 1,000 by 1,000 map, but the likeness is structural only [M]. Critics have flagged passages about Jews in her texts [M that the criticism exists; U for the passages]. Check before any citation.

Adjacent ladders of scale outside Theosophy (needed by the question, [M] unless marked).
- Gurdjieff via Ouspensky (In Search of the Miraculous, 1949): the Ray of Creation runs Absolute, All Worlds, All Suns (the Milky Way), the Sun, All Planets, Earth, Moon, with the number of laws doubling per step down (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96). The nearest galaxy rung in this literature; the best precedent for an Earth, Sun, galaxy ladder.
- Steiner (1910): seven planetary states of Earth's evolution, Saturn to Vulcan. Bucke (Cosmic Consciousness, 1901): simple, self and cosmic consciousness.
- The Anguttara Nikaya has thousandfold, two-thousandfold and three-thousandfold world systems, each a thousand of the one before: 1,000 cubed, about a billion world-units (a reading of the suttas, not a sentence in them; sutta number AN 3.80 [U]). It counts worlds, not a person's potential, but is an older echo of Alex's 1,000 by 1,000 zoom.
- The Puranas speak of numberless universes, each with its own Brahma.

### 4.9 Stages and the Path

The Voice's maps.
- The Three Halls and the Seven Portals (section 3.4): the Seven Portals are dana (charity and love), shila (harmony in word and act, the key that balances cause and effect), kshanti (patience), viraga (indifference to pleasure and pain, illusion conquered), virya (dauntless energy), dhyana (meditation; its golden gate, once opened, leads the Naljor toward the realm of Sat eternal) and prajna (wisdom, the key that makes a Bodhisattva) [list and order M in two files, U on the order of the first four in a third; glosses U, handles only]. Mahayana lists six perfections (paramitas): dana, shila, kshanti, virya, dhyana, prajna; some Mahayana sources have ten (Dasabhumika); the Pali Theravada list is ten paramis. The Voice inserts viraga as the fourth [M]. The seven are not a standard Buddhist list; the sevenfold form is Theosophical (principles, planes, rounds). Viraga is a Buddhist term but not a paramita [M].
- For Alex: a stage map to set beside the arrow, with viraga as the portal nearest to letting go. No one-for-one mapping without his say [M]. A seven-step Path with "indifference to pleasure and pain" fourth is the nearest older text to letting go as the hinge of the path [M].

The Two Paths and the Great Renunciation.
- Fragment II sets two ways side by side. One is private liberation: the Pratyeka Buddha, in her usage a Buddha for himself, who enters nirvana in the Dharmakaya. The other is renunciation: one who has earned release refuses it (the "Great Renunciation") and takes the Nirmanakaya state, to stay in touch with humanity as a protector, a "Guardian Wall" [U]. The close of Fragment III puts the choice to a disciple who has passed the seven portals ("Thou art enlightened, choose thy way", recalled as the end of Fragment III [U for locator]). A recalled line says the Path is one, yet in the end twofold, marked by "four and seven Portals" (handle only) [M content, U locator].
- Scholars: Pratyekabuddha is a real Buddhist category (the Pali paccekabuddha is a silent buddha who awakens alone and does not teach); "selfish" is Mahayana polemic, which she follows. Her Nirmanakaya, an adept who stays, differs from the Mahayana trikaya, where the nirmanakaya is the manifestation body in which a Buddha appears (Shakyamuni is one). Her usage filters Mahayana polemic through Victorian writing such as Edwin Arnold's The Light of Asia (1879) [M]. The Glossary defines Nirmanakaya her way [M].
- Use: a counterweight to "return means merging": at the top there is a choice to stay for others. It links to Part one's "reason bigger than yourself" and could hand off to Part three. It fits the hand-back in Alex's voice. It answers "what comes after the last rung" with return [M]. Do not call it "birth".
- Compassion as the law of laws (section 3.4) is the Voice's ethic side, and one file calls the compassion fragment Theosophy's strongest passage for the DO half (action) [M]. It pairs with Gita 3.20 to 3.25 but is a different school from Guenon's, who ranks knowledge above action [M]. Read Judge before any claim [M].

Probation, discipleship, qualifications.
- The 1880s teaching spoke of probationary, accepted and lay chelas and warned that a pupil is tested long and there are no shortcuts (Blavatsky, "Chelas and Lay Chelas", 1883) [M]. Besant formalised a Probationary Path and a Path of Discipleship (Besant, The Path of Discipleship: four lectures at the Adyar convention in December 1895, published 1896 [U]).
- At the Feet of the Master (1910) is credited to "Alcyone" (the young J. Krishnamurti) with Leadbeater's help; authorship is disputed [U]. It gives four qualifications: discrimination, desirelessness, good conduct (six sub-qualities, recalled as self-control as to the mind, self-control in action, tolerance, cheerfulness, one-pointedness, confidence [M]) and love. The first three adapt the Advaita fourfold means (viveka, vairagya, shatsampatti, mumukshutva, from the Vivekachudamani), with love replacing the desire for release [M]. "Desirelessness" at the probationary stage is Theosophy's own name for what Alex calls letting go [M].
- The Golden Stairs: section 2.9.

The initiations (Besant and Leadbeater; Bailey).
- The Path proper has a Probationary stage, Accepted Discipleship under a Master, then five initiations to the Adept (Asekha, the Master) [M]. Labels come from three traditions. Buddhist: Sotapanna, Sakadagamin, Anagamin, Arhat, Asekha ("beyond training"). Hindu, as recalled: Parivrajaka, Kutichaka, Bahudaka, Hamsa, Paramahamsa [U]. Christian: Birth, Baptism, Transfiguration, Crucifixion (renunciation), Resurrection and Ascension [M; the "Ascension" attachment to the fifth U].
- Besant and Leadbeater equate the four Buddhist stages (stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arhat) with the first four initiations; the fifth is the adept or master [M]. The Buddhist fetters supply content: the first initiation drops the belief in a permanent separate self, doubt and ritualism; the second weakens desire and aversion. The first initiation is said to touch buddhic consciousness, the plane of unity [M]. Reading the Gospels as an initiation map goes back at least to Kingsford and Maitland (The Perfect Way, 1881, the public edition perhaps 1882 [U]) and was taken up by Besant (Esoteric Christianity, 1901) [M]. Sources: Besant, Initiation: The Perfecting of Man (1923); Leadbeater, The Masters and the Path (1925) [M].
- Scholars: in the Pali Nikayas an arahant is an asekha, so splitting "arhat" and "asekha" into two stages is a Theosophical addition. Buddhist scholars define the four stages by which fetters are cut, not by initiations [M]. Alex's essay says early Buddhism has four stages; if he cites five, he should say who made them five. One Theosophical rung is written as Sotapanna, Parivrajaka and Birth at once: the reader feels three religions agree, though the three lists differ in length and meaning and the Theosophists cut and joined them [M].
- Bailey keeps the Christian pattern and extends it to nine initiations: Birth, Baptism, Transfiguration, Renunciation (crucifixion), Revelation (resurrection, the Master); then, as recalled, Decision (sixth), Resurrection (seventh), Transition (eighth), Refusal (ninth) [M for the first four names and the idea of seven Paths; U for labels five to nine]. After the fifth the Master chooses among seven Paths of Higher Evolution (Earth service, magnetic work, training for Planetary Logos, the path to Sirius, the Ray path, the path of our Logos, absolute sonship) [U for titles]. Leadbeater lists seven choices for the Adept, including merging into the Logos and staying in the Nirmanakaya state [M]. Bailey applies the ladder to the gospel life in From Bethlehem to Calvary (1937); The Rays and the Initiations (1960, posthumous) [M]. The fork after the Master (Decision, seven Paths) is a second precedent for "what comes after the top is a choice" [M].
- Collision on "birth". In Bailey and in Besant and Leadbeater, "Birth" is the first initiation, at the start of the Path proper. In Alex's school "birth" is the stage after plus four. Both use the second-birth image for a threshold (John 3:3, dvija, Eckhart), but not the same one. Do not say Theosophy "calls it birth" or that it supports his usage. A second collision: "Resurrection" is the fifth in Besant and Leadbeater's list and the seventh in Bailey's [U].
- Poimandres (Corpus Hermeticum I), in Mead's Thrice-Greatest Hermes (1906): the strongest Western text for one, separation, climb, return, then new birth (section 5.7) [M].

The honest objection from inside.
- Krishnamurti, found by Leadbeater in 1909 and trained as World Teacher, dissolved the Order of the Star in the East at Ommen on 3 August 1929, saying truth is a pathless land that no organisation, creed or sect can reach. He resigned from the Society in 1930 [M for the date, place and thrust; the 1930 resignation U]. The recalled sentence "I maintain that Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect" is a handle, [M], unseen. Copy it from the Krishnamurti Foundation's text of the Ommen address. "Gradual stage" is a gloss; the address speaks of path, religion, sect and organisation, and a paraphrase as an attack on stages is an inference [U]. Still the best internal objection to stage maps, from the person they were built for; it fits Alex's "treat the labels as labels" [M].

### 4.10 Letting go

- Theosophy's own words for it: "desirelessness" among the probationary qualifications and viraga as the fourth portal (section 4.9) [M]. Check verified wording of both lists.
- The Voice's verbs are severe (kill, slay, renounce). Alex's verb is release, which is milder and should stay so [M].
- Judge, Letters That Have Helped Me (1891; a second part after his death U): practical advice for students on inner work: act from duty, drop the grip on results, work on the lower self; the Gita underneath. A plain Western voice on letting go in the 1890s [M for existence and rough dates; U for contents].
- Steiner, Knowledge of the Higher Worlds: six supplementary exercises (control of thought, of will, equanimity, positivity, open-mindedness, balance of the five), an early Western practice of letting go; equanimity is the swing-damping exercise [M; check an edition].
- Subba Row's Gita lectures treat the Gita as a manual of the soul's growth [M].
- Krishnamurti: no method at all [M].

### 4.11 Beside Alex's arrow

- No Theosophical text the files know maps a stage to speed, to a galaxy or to a minus four to plus four scale [M]. The mapping is Alex's, and the essay should say so.
- In Theosophy the Earth's Logos stands above the Master. In his arrow, escape velocity at plus one leaves Earth. They do not map one to one and the essay should not claim they do [M].
- The shared logic: the whole goes down into separation, turns, and returns with something gained [M].
- The system also carries root races, which the essay should leave out [M].

## 5. Later Theosophists

### 5.1 Four kinds of writer

After Blavatsky died in 1891 her movement split into four kinds of writer, and they compare religions in four different ways [M].
- The colleagues who kept her words (Judge, then Tingley at Point Loma, Crosbie's United Lodge, Purucker) wrote plain handbooks of her system. They compare by repeating her equivalences and by stressing karma and rebirth.
- The Adyar school (Besant, Leadbeater, Jinarajadasa, Hodson) added claimed clairvoyant observation as a second witness, and ranked maps of the path (probation, five initiations). They lay one list of terms over each religion and read each religion as the public face of an inner teaching.
- The scholars and translators (Olcott on Buddhism, Subba Row on Vedanta, Mead on Gnostic and Hermetic texts) compare most checkably, because they work from named texts.
- The heirs and dissenters (Steiner, Bailey, Krishnamurti) kept the frame and changed the centre: Christ, the Hierarchy, or nothing at all.

All share one frame: one boundless source, and each soul a pilgrim who descends into matter, lives many lives under a law of consequence, and returns. That is close to the three clauses of Alex's axiom (one whole, it experiences separation, the separation ends) and is the nearest precedent in English for his structure [M]. Much of the Adyar layer rests on observation nobody can repeat [M].

### 5.2 William Quan Judge (1851 to 1896)

- Irish-born New York lawyer and founder-member of 1875 [M]. The Ocean of Theosophy (1893) is the briefest orderly summary of Blavatsky's system: the one source; the seven principles; death; a period of shedding desire (kama-loka); a long rest of assimilation (devachan); rebirth. Karma is ethical causation and adjustment, not a verdict. What carries over is the reincarnating ego, not the personality. He argues for rebirth from unequal births, child prodigies and continuity of character. Parallels he draws: Pythagoras, Plato, Origen, Elijah and John the Baptist, Hindu texts (his exact list U) [M for author, year and outline; U for chapter order].
- Use: a clear answer to "who carries over". The devachan rest is the nearest nineteenth-century picture to Alex's saved state between runs. For lives and karma in plain English, Judge and Besant are easier than Blavatsky [M].
- As editor: The Path (monthly, from 1886); the Yoga Aphorisms of Patanjali (1889); an English Bhagavad Gita with notes (1890), best described as a recension of earlier English versions and not a translation from Sanskrit [U, about 70 percent]; Letters That Have Helped Me (first part 1891 [M]; a second part after his death, one file says 1905 [U]) [M].
- He led the American section out of Adyar in 1895 after charges against him (forging Master messages, said his accusers) [M; exact charge U]. He died in 1896.
- Judge and Besant say the Church condemned rebirth at the Council of 553; historians find no such condemnation (section 4.4). Do not repeat it [M].

### 5.3 Henry Steel Olcott (1832 to 1907)

- President of the Society until his death in 1907 [M]. His Buddhist Catechism (Colombo, 1881) was written in question-and-answer form as an answer to missionary catechisms, endorsed by the monk Sumangala, and went through many editions and languages. It presents rebirth by karma without a permanent soul [M; number of editions U].
- The "Fourteen Fundamental Buddhist Beliefs": 1891 is the accepted form; an earlier draft is recalled at 1884 [U]. It was accepted by Sinhalese and Japanese authorities and, as recalled, others; who signed is [U] and must be checked before the essay says "both Southern and Northern" schools [U]. The aim was a list common to Southern and Northern Buddhists, the only comparison in this literature shown to the people compared. Use: a model of honest common ground, a short list each side would sign, and a test for the wording of "the separation ends" [M].
- Olcott said Buddhism and Theosophy agree on the soul because what continues is a set of consequences. Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism was disliked by Buddhists and Orientalists; Max Muller (1893) and Rhys Davids are believed to have written against it, but name neither until checked [U].
- He took the five precepts at Galle (25 May 1880), helped found the Buddhist Theosophical Society, Buddhist schools (the Colombo school that became Ananda College was first run by Leadbeater), the Buddhist flag (1885; year U), and in 1891 the fourteen propositions [M]. The revival began earlier (the Panadura debate, 1873) [M]. Scholars would say the Theosophists helped shape and fund it and did not start it. Gombrich and Obeyesekere's "Protestant Buddhism" (coined by Obeyesekere, 1970 [U]; developed in Buddhism Transformed, 1988) places Olcott's catechisms and schools inside it [M].
- Old Diary Leaves (six series, 1895 to 1935; only the first four in his lifetime [U]). Prothero, The White Buddhist (1996) is the biography; it shows the Society's Buddhism as a Protestant-flavoured, Victorian composite [M].

### 5.4 Annie Besant (1847 to 1933)

- Joined in 1889 after reviewing the Secret Doctrine; president of the Society from 1907 to 1933 [M]. Moved to India in 1893; founded the Central Hindu College (1898, the core of Banaras Hindu University in 1916); led the Home Rule League (1916); interned in 1917; president of the Indian National Congress in 1917 [M]. Critics in Madras saw her Hinduism as Brahmin-centred [M]. Biographies: Nethercot (1960, 1963) and Taylor (1992) [M].
- Books [M for titles and years unless marked]: Seven Principles of Man (1892; Sanskrit names lined up with Christian spirit, soul and body and the Kabbalah's soul names); Reincarnation (1892); Karma (1895); The Self and Its Sheaths (1895 [U]); The Ancient Wisdom (1897); Four Great Religions (1897); Esoteric Christianity, or the Lesser Mysteries (1901); A Study in Consciousness (1904); Initiation: The Perfecting of Man (1923). A Gita translation: about 1895, and one with Bhagavan Das in 1905 [U].
- The Ancient Wisdom is the long manual of the Adyar school: planes of nature, the constitution of man, after-death states, rebirth, karma, the path of discipleship. Spirit descends into matter, reaches a low point of density, and climbs back through the human kingdom to higher ranks. Part of the detail rests on Leadbeater's observation. The descent and climb match Alex's separation and return in broad form; the low point is the nearest Theosophical counterpart to a turning point [M].
- Four Great Religions: four lectures on Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, Buddhism and Christianity, each presented as a form of one teaching about the Self, its descent, its law and its path. She matches the Hindu triple aspect of the Divine with the Zoroastrian Ahura Mazda and the holy immortals (six Amesha Spentas, seven if Ahura Mazda is counted; the count she gives is U), the Buddhist wheel and the skandhas, and the Christian Trinity. Her explanation of difference: each Great Teacher gives the same truth in the form suited to a people and an age [M]. She is the source of the "different forms, one teaching" idea. Specialists reject several of her matchings (the Zoroastrian seven with the Theosophical seven). The venue of the lectures is U (an Adyar convention, 1895 or 1896). Read the four chapters before borrowing an equation [M].
- Esoteric Christianity: Christianity has an outer creed and an inner teaching, the lesser mysteries. The Trinity, Incarnation, Atonement and sacraments are read as statements about the Logos, who limits himself in matter, and about the Christ born inside the soul. The gospel life is read as the path of a soul. Clement and Origen knew the inner meaning. Early Christianity taught rebirth and lost it. Kingsford and Maitland's The Perfect Way is the earlier model [M]. Her reading of the Atonement as the One limiting itself in form is close to Alex's reason why the whole would separate; name it as Besant's reading. That she pairs it with Revelation 13:8 and Rigveda 10.90 is [U].
- A Study in Consciousness and The Self and Its Sheaths: section 4.2.
- With Leadbeater she wrote Man: Whence, How and Whither (1913) and Occult Chemistry (1908), clairvoyant "observation" of atoms later said by some Theosophists to anticipate particle physics [M; that it is Hammer's main example U].

### 5.5 Charles Webster Leadbeater (1854 to 1934)

- Former Anglican curate [M]. Books [M unless marked]: The Astral Plane (1895); The Devachanic Plane (about 1896); Man Visible and Invisible (1902); The Other Side of Death (1903 or 1904 [U]); A Textbook of Theosophy (1912); Lives of Alcyone (1924); The Masters and the Path (1925); The Chakras (1927).
- Method: observation by trained clairvoyance. He describes after-death states in detail and labels them with the world's terms: devachan is his heaven-world, matched with the Pure Land and the Christian heaven; the lower astral regions are matched with hell and purgatory [M]. Not as evidence; as a witness to the equation habit. Historians treat his reports as unverifiable [M].
- He found Krishnamurti in 1909. His 1906 scandal (he resigned and was readmitted in 1908) is in Tillett, The Elder Brother (1982). His visions produced a hierarchy of Masters and the "World Teacher" scheme: the source of many "Ascended Master" ideas, and the reason careful writers separate Blavatsky from "neo-Theosophy" [M].

### 5.6 Mabel Collins, Kingsford and Maitland

- Mabel Collins: Light on the Path (1885, also said to be dictated by a Master); The Idyll of the White Lotus (1884), source of the "Three Truths". Sections 2.5 and 4.3 [M].
- Anna Kingsford and Edward Maitland, The Perfect Way (1881, public edition perhaps 1882 [U]): a parallel body of teaching and the earlier model for reading the Gospels as an initiation map [M].

### 5.7 G. R. S. Mead (1863 to 1933)

- Blavatsky's secretary from 1889 [M]. Edited the Glossary (1892) [M]. Left the Society in 1909 over the Leadbeater affair and founded the Quest Society and its quarterly [M]. Mead's career is a case of comparison moving from a society into scholarship.
- Fragments of a Faith Forgotten (1900) argued that the Gnostics were not heretics but the thinking half of early Christianity. He sets out the Valentinian myth: the Fullness (pleroma) is complete; its last aeon Sophia falls through wanting to know the Father; a Limit (horos) fixes the boundary; a redeemer gathers what fell and restores it. An ancient order of one, wall, return [M]. His Pistis Sophia (1896) has souls reborn in new bodies until they know; his text base is recalled as the Latin of Schwartze, printed 1851 [U]. The Doctrine of the Subtle Body in Western Tradition (1919) traces the soul's "vehicle" (Greek ochema) in Platonism and the Fathers [M]. The Gnostic John the Baptizer (1924) takes up the Mandaeans [M].
- Thrice-Greatest Hermes (1906), three volumes, "Studies in Hellenistic Theosophy and Gnosis" [M]. The Poimandres (Corpus Hermeticum I) tells how Man, born of Mind, leans down, loves his reflection in Nature and is joined to her; so man is mortal in body and immortal in essence. After death the soul climbs through seven planetary spheres, dropping one fault at each (growth, scheming, desire, arrogance, daring, greed, falsehood), reaches the eighth sphere and becomes God. Corpus Hermeticum XIII is a talk on rebirth: Tat sheds twelve torments, receives ten powers, and sees himself everywhere, in heaven, in earth, in animals, in the womb and before it [M; paragraph numbers U]. Correction: "an eighth and ninth beyond" is not in Poimandres; the ninth belongs to the Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth (Nag Hammadi VI,6). Write "seven shed faults, then the eighth, then the Father" [M]. Corpus Hermeticum XI.20 (to know God one must become equal to God, grow immeasurably, leap out of every body and become eternity) is the nearest ancient text to Alex's zoom-out; XIII gives rebirth as a seeing in this life with a teacher, not a post-mortem birth [M; numbers U]. In Hermes rebirth is a birth into seeing oneself everywhere; in Alex's school "birth" is the stage after plus four; they may be the same or not. Say the Theosophical gloss is Mead's. Check a modern translation (Copenhaver 1992).
- The Hymn of the Pearl (Acts of Thomas 108 to 113), published by Mead as "The Hymn of the Robe of Glory" (1908): a prince sent from the East loses his robe of glory, goes to Egypt for a pearl guarded by a serpent, forgets his origin and sleeps; a letter from his parents wakes him; he returns; the robe meets him and he sees himself in it. Scholars long called it Gnostic; Klijn and others place it in Edessene Syriac Christianity and doubt the label, so write "often called Gnostic" [M]. The Apocryphon of John (found at Nag Hammadi in 1945, also in the Berlin Codex known since the 1890s) has souls cast into another body until they awaken, which backs the Theosophical claim that some Gnostic texts teach rebirth; locator U [M].
- Scholars: Mead is respected as an editor and dated as a translator. The Hermetica now rests on Nock and Festugiere (1945 to 1954) and Copenhaver (1992), who read the texts as Greek philosophical and religious writing of the early centuries CE, and on Fowden (1986), who stresses the Egyptian setting and a lived "way of Hermes"; none finds a secret ancient doctrine in the Theosophical sense. Nag Hammadi transformed Gnostic studies and the label "Gnosticism" is now contested (Williams 1996, King 2003). Burkert (Ancient Mystery Cults, 1987) shows the mystery cults were rites and experience without systematic doctrine, which weakens the claim that they taught rebirth and oneness as a creed [M].

### 5.8 Purucker, Jinarajadasa, Hodson

- Gottfried de Purucker (1874 to 1942) led the Point Loma and later Pasadena society from 1929. Fundamentals of the Esoteric Philosophy (1932), Occult Glossary (1933), The Esoteric Tradition (1935, two volumes). The universe is a living hierarchy in which every being is a ray of a greater being; the organism picture supports karma as one part of a body harming another, the fingers. He prefers "reimbodiment" to "reincarnation". His Glossary sets Sanskrit, Hebrew, Greek and Tibetan terms side by side; he treats the Zohar as a form of the same tradition [M for dates and titles; U for the organism emphasis and the Kabbalah matches]. A reference book, not a source to quote.
- Curuppumullage Jinarajadasa (1875 to 1953), Sinhalese, Buddhist-raised, Cambridge-educated, president of the Society 1946 to 1953: First Principles of Theosophy (1921), Christ and Buddha (1907), How We Remember Our Past Lives (1915) [M for authors and titles; U for contents and some dates].
- Geoffrey Hodson (1886 to 1983), clairvoyant and lecturer: The Hidden Wisdom in the Holy Bible (several volumes from 1963), Reincarnation: Fact or Fallacy? (1951), The Seven Human Temperaments (1952), which ties differences in religious feeling to seven types [M]. His temperaments are one more Theosophical answer to "why do the religions differ": because people differ [M].

### 5.9 Rudolf Steiner (1861 to 1925)

- Led the German section from 1902 until the break of 1912 to 1913, partly over Besant's claim that Krishnamurti was the coming World Teacher; founded the Anthroposophical Society (1912 to 1913) [M]. Christianity as Mystical Fact (1902), Theosophy (1904), Knowledge of the Higher Worlds (serialised 1904 to 1905, book 1909 [about 75 percent]), Occult Science (1910), Karmic Relationships (lectures, 1924) [M]. He keeps rebirth and karma but makes Christ the central event of earth's development, so he reads the Greek, Egyptian and Persian mysteries as a run-up to Christ. Three higher knowings: Imagination, Inspiration, Intuition [M]. His six supplementary exercises are in section 4.10.
- A comparer who does not say all religions are equal, and a good counter-voice to "all say the same". Critics have pointed to racial passages in his lectures [U]; check before use. Peter Staudenmaier (Between Occultism and Nazism, 2014) argues that Steiner's anthroposophy carried racial doctrine into its politics in the fascist era; anthroposophists contest his reading [M]. A Dutch commission (2000) found some statements discriminatory but the movement not racist [U].

### 5.10 Alice Bailey (1880 to 1949)

- A Theosophist until about 1920 or the early 1920s; founded the Arcane School and Lucis Trust; wrote books she ascribed to a Tibetan teacher, Djwhal Khul [M]. She teaches seven rays, a Hierarchy of Masters, soul and personality joined by a bridge (antahkarana), and the planet as a living being. She names three veils over the mind: maya, glamour and illusion, linked to the physical, emotional and mental levels [broad teaching M; assignment of the veils U]. In The Reappearance of the Christ (1948) she says all religions await the same World Teacher under different names (Christ, Messiah, Maitreya, Imam Mahdi, Krishna): a classic comparer move [M; the list of names U]. The "dictated" source is unverifiable [M]. Hanegraaff treats Bailey as the main bridge to the New Age [M]. Cosmic planes and initiations: sections 4.8 and 4.9.

### 5.11 Krishnamurti

- Found by Leadbeater in 1909 and trained as the coming World Teacher; head of the Order of the Star in the East (1911, an earlier "Order of the Rising Sun" in 1910 [U]); dissolved it at Ommen on 3 August 1929; resigned from the Society in 1930 [U]. His later teaching (the observer is the observed; choiceless awareness) says the division of self and world is the problem and no method undoes it [M]. "The observer is the observed" is a plain-English version of the veil [M]. Huxley wrote the foreword to The First and Last Freedom (1954) and was friendly with him [M]. Details: section 4.9.

### 5.12 Point Loma, the United Lodge, neo-Theosophy

- After the dispute over Judge, the American section left Adyar at its Boston convention in April 1895 [M]. After Judge's death (1896) the section's president was Ernest Hargrove, and Katherine Tingley had full control by 1898 [U, about 70 percent], moving to Point Loma, San Diego (the move usually dated 1900, after a 1897 foundation stone [U]). The United Lodge of Theosophists was founded in Los Angeles in 1909 by Robert Crosbie and keeps only Blavatsky and Judge [M]. "Pasadena" belongs to a different Blavatsky-loyal body (the Theosophical Society, Pasadena, the Point Loma line); the United Lodge and Pasadena are two bodies [M].
- These groups call the Besant and Leadbeater layer "neo-Theosophy" [M]. De Zirkoff's Collected Writings of H. P. Blavatsky (1950 to 1991) is the standard text for separating her words from later ones [M]. One caution: "Theosophy" names at least three bodies of teaching that disagree.

### 5.13 The anthologists of rebirth

- E. D. Walker, Reincarnation: A Study of Forgotten Truth (Boston, 1888); Joseph Head and S. L. Cranston, Reincarnation: An East-West Anthology (1961; reissued as Reincarnation: The Phoenix Fire Mystery, 1977) [M]. The catalogue method: short quotes sorted by thinker and religion, which shows the spread of the idea and hides the context of each text [M]. The anthologies repeat the Council of 553 claim and the Jerome line (sections 4.4 and 12) [M]. Arguments by reason (unequal starts, prodigies, one life too short) do not depend on any text [M].

## 6. The Mahatma Letters, Sinnett and Subba Row

### 6.1 A. P. Sinnett and the two books

- Alfred Percy Sinnett (1840 to 1921) edited The Pioneer at Allahabad, met Blavatsky and Olcott around 1879 to 1880 and received letters from K.H. and M. from October 1880 [M]. The Occult World (Trubner, 1881) argues that adepts exist, built on his own experience of phenomena (the Simla episodes of autumn 1880) and extracts of K.H.'s letters, with no real doctrine. Esoteric Buddhism (Trubner, 1883) carries the doctrine. A fifth, annotated edition (1885) added notes after criticism from inside the Society [M; the annotated edition U]. The Occult World is no source for the traditions [M].
- Esoteric Buddhism teaches [M; chapter titles U]: a person has seven principles; death is a sorting (the lowest principles fall away, the animal soul lingers in a shadowy state, kama-loca, and fades as a "shell", the human and spiritual souls pass into a long subjective rest, devachan); after the rest the person returns, and the sum of a life's causes (karma) shapes the next; all this happens on a chain of seven globes that the life-wave tours seven times (the rounds), and humanity moves through seven root races; nirvana lies at the far end and is not annihilation. The text uses Sanskrit and some Tibetan terms and, as recalled, no Pali texts [U]. The title claims the Buddha's own secret teaching.
- The title problem. The Masters and Sinnett said the word Buddhism in the title did not mean the religion as practised: the Letters play on Budh (wisdom, from the root budh, to know) against Buddha, so the real thing is a wisdom-teaching of which Gautama was one great exponent. Blavatsky later said in the Key that Theosophy is not Buddhism. Who said what and when is [U]; that the naming dispute existed is [M]. Scholars saw it as an after-the-fact fix, since the book was sold as the Buddha's secret doctrine. If Alex calls anything Buddhism he should let Buddhists speak [M].
- Buddhist scholars (Max Muller, Rhys Davids) said it was not Buddhism. Critics said the book was neither esoteric nor Buddhist; that line is carried from a sibling's search [C]; do not print it as Guenon's own words or as Muller's until the source is found.
- "If Part two names Sinnett, say 'an English newspaper editor in India, 1883'" [M].

### 6.2 The Letters themselves

- About 140 to 150 letters (the count depends on the edition) [U], mostly from 1880 to about 1884, from K.H. and M. to Sinnett and Hume. Edited by A. T. Barker: The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett from the Mahatmas M. and K. H. (T. Fisher Unwin, London, 1923). Vicente Hao Chin Jr., The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett in Chronological Sequence (Theosophical Publishing House, Manila, 1993), re-orders them by date, restores some passages and gives a concordance with Barker's numbers. The manuscripts are in the British Library [U on shelf-marks]. Any locator must name the edition [M].
- Provenance: the Hodgson report judged Blavatsky the author of the K.H. letters; Harrison attacked its handwriting method (section 2.7); the Kiddle incident (1883). Treat the Letters as Theosophical scripture. Alex can say "the Letters claim" and "Theosophists hold", not that Tibetan Masters taught this [M].
- Scholars treat the Letters as founding writing of a new religious movement, not as a witness to an Asian tradition. The Masters have no corroboration in Tibetan sources (Lopez, Prisoners of Shangri-La, 1998; Godwin, 1994; Hanegraaff, 1996) [M gist].
- Defenders of authenticity are mostly Theosophical authors (for example Daniel Caldwell, The Esoteric World of Madame Blavatsky, 2000 [U]). Campbell's Ancient Wisdom Revived (1980) is a historian's account and not an argument for authenticity. K. Paul Johnson, The Masters Revealed (1994), argues that the Masters were real historical Indian figures but that the Theosophical picture (Tibetan Mahatmas, a Great White Lodge) was constructed; that is not a defence of the Letters' own claims. Vernon Harrison criticises Hodgson's handwriting method, which is not the same as proving who wrote the letters. Most academics put authenticity aside [M].
- The "Chiefs" and brotherhood. Theosophists cite a long K.H. letter to Hume (1880, numbered 10 in Barker's edition) for the claim that the Chiefs wanted a real universal brotherhood started [M; letter number and wording U].

### 6.3 The letter on God

- In one of the best known Letters (Barker Letter 10, as remembered [U]; a long K.H. letter to A. O. Hume printed in the Sinnett volume [M/U]) K.H. says the Masters' doctrine denies God "as philosophers and as Buddhists" (recalled; an earlier draft had "philosophical pantheists and theists", which the checker does not recall) [M for the gist; wording U]. They know of planetary and other spiritual lives, and say there is no God in their system "either personal or impersonal". Parabrahm is not a God but absolute immutable law, and Ishvara is the effect of avidya and maya, born of ignorance and the great illusion [handles; gist M, exact words U]. Read the letter in Barker and in Hao Chin (use his concordance) before quoting. A teaching offered as the root of all religions begins by denying the God of most of them [M].
- Letter 10 is about God, not karma. It supports "no God as judge", not a mechanism of karma [M].
- An Advaita specialist would object on two points: Brahman is not "Law" but being and consciousness, and Ishvara is not a mere product of ignorance but Brahman as seen with maya as its power. So Parabrahm-as-Law is a Theosophical reading, a place where the comparer equates what specialists keep apart. A Theravada reader would accept "no creator god" but not "Parabrahm is Law". Attribute it to the Masters, not to Advaita, and note the Letter's own words "as Buddhists" [M]. Olcott's proposition that the universe was evolved, not created, works by law, is a parallel [U on wording].

### 6.4 What the Letters add

- The rounds and the V-shaped journey (section 4.8), kama-loca, devachan and the shells (section 4.6) [M].
- Nirvana and paranirvana. The Letters insist that nirvana is not annihilation. They separate nirvana (a state of highest spiritual existence, entered by the adept) from paranirvana (the state beyond, at the end of a manvantara, when the whole chain re-enters the Absolute). The Masters present themselves as followers of the Buddha's inner teaching, which they call the same as the older "Aryan" wisdom [M]. In Theravada, parinibbana is the death of an arahant with no further rebirth; it is not cosmic. The Theosophical paranirvana reads a Hindu cosmic dissolution (pralaya) back into a Buddhist word [M]. For Alex: nirvana and moksha can carry "the separation ends", but those traditions describe the end in the first person (craving ends, ignorance ends), not as a cosmic event [M].
- The Masters on other faiths, as recalled [U]: Jesus honoured as an adept while church doctrine (a personal God, atonement, hell) and priesthood are rejected; Brahmins honoured as heirs of an old wisdom and criticised for caste and ritual; Southern (Ceylon) Buddhists said to hold the Buddha's letter but to have lost the key to his inner meaning, while the reformed Tibetan Buddhism of Tsongkhapa is said to keep it; the Society must be neutral among religions and aim at brotherhood. The attitudes track 1880s freethought criticism of church Christianity more than any Tibetan source. Alex's own position (the inner teaching of each tradition carries the idea, the official face often does not) is close to this pattern; he should say it as his own and not cite the Letters for claims about Tibet [M].

### 6.5 No-self against the Self

- The hinge of the whole Buddhist and Vedantic equation. The standard Theosophical answer: the Buddha's anatta denies that the personal, changing self (the lower principles) is the real self; it does not deny the One Self, which Vedanta calls Atman and Theosophy Atma. So the Buddha and the Upanishads say the same: not this personal I, but that which is the Whole. The Letters add that the personality does not survive while the individuality (the higher triad) does [M].
- Pali specialists (Rhys Davids, later Rahula, Harvey) read anatta as denying a self in any of the five aggregates, not as a hint of a universal Self. Two complications. In one Pali discourse (SN 44.10, the Ananda Sutta, as recalled [U]) the Buddha is silent when asked whether there is a self. And Caroline Rhys Davids argued in later books (late 1920s and 1930s: Sakya, or Buddhist Origins, 1928; Buddhism: its Birth and Dispersal, 1934; The Birth of Indian Psychology and Its Development in Buddhism, 1936; What Was the Original Gospel in Buddhism?, 1938 [U]) that early Buddhism taught a Self; most specialists have not followed her [M].
- The honest sentence: "the Theosophists reconcile the two by splitting the self in two", which settles nothing. If Alex uses Buddhism he should use the Buddha's own text on the Unconditioned (Udana 8.3, the unborn, unmade, unconditioned [M]) and say that Theravada and Mahayana read such lines differently [M]. Coomaraswamy, a Traditionalist, reads anatta as denying the empirical self and not the Self; Pali specialists dispute this (section 8) [M].

### 6.6 Max Muller and the closed fist

- Max Muller published "Esoteric Buddhism" in The Nineteenth Century (1893, I believe May [U]) and treated the title as a mistake: Buddhism has no secret doctrine. His Gifford Lectures (delivered 1892, published 1893), Theosophy, or Psychological Religion, separate the older meaning of theosophy (mystic knowledge of God) from Blavatsky's [M]. T. W. Rhys Davids, founder of the Pali Text Society, also rejected the Theosophical use of Buddhism; no title is known [U].
- The standard Buddhological reply is the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16, second chapter), where the Buddha tells Ananda at Beluva near Vesali that he has taught with no "closed fist of a teacher" (Pali acariyamutthi) and held nothing back [M on place and Pali]. The paragraph number differs by translator (2.25 in Walshe's numbering; II.32 and PTS D II 100 in Rhys Davids' older numbering [U]); give no number until a translation is open. The line is the general Buddhological reply and is not claimed to be Muller's [M]. Tantric and Mahayana traditions do have restricted teachings, so "no esoteric Buddhism" holds for the Pali canon's own claim and not for every school; Asian "esoteric Buddhism" (Shingon, Tantra) means something else again [M].
- Use: do not write "the secret teaching of the Buddha". Write that the Buddha says he kept nothing back. The Theosophical reply (a private teaching to initiates) cannot be tested. A fitting quiet echo for an essay that uses a fist for holding on; check a modern translation and quote only that [M].

### 6.7 T. Subba Row (1856 to 1890)

- Tallapragada Subba Row, a Telugu Brahmin, lawyer in the Madras High Court and a learned Advaitin. He joined the Society in the early 1880s [U], wrote in The Theosophist (articles 1881 to 1887 [U]), acted as its authority on Hindu thought, was said to have a teacher among the Masters [U], and died aged 33 (born July 1856, died June 1890) [M for 1856 to 1890; U for the exact age]. Works: Notes on the Bhagavad Gita (book date unknown) and Esoteric Writings (1895) [M].
- The Bhagavad Gita lectures at the Adyar convention. The year is not settled. The files give: December 1885, printed 1886; delivered 1886, printed 1887; late 1885 or 1886, printed 1887 (with a lean toward December 1886 and 1887). All [U]. Print no year. Gist, from memory [M]: Krishna is the Logos (Ishvara) speaking to Arjuna, the individual soul (jivatma). Behind the Logos is Parabrahman, the Absolute that cannot be described. Mulaprakriti, root-matter, is the veil of Parabrahman, and the Logos arises through it as a reflection arises on a surface. The Gita's point is that the individual must find its Self one with the Logos and with the Absolute behind it. The "Light of the Logos" with Blavatsky's Fohat is an equation [U]. Scholars: the Logos language is Christian and Neoplatonic, not Advaitic; Advaita would call root-matter maya or avidya; academics treat the lectures as a real meeting of Advaita and Western occultism [M]. Use: the best early example of a veil made of the One itself; verify the wording before quoting; Subba Row is a better witness for Vedanta than Blavatsky [M].
- "Aryan-Arhat Esoteric Tenets on the Sevenfold Principle in Man" (The Theosophist, I believe 1886 to 1887 [U]). The title says that the Hindu (Aryan) and the Buddhist (Arhat) inner teachings are one. As recalled [M for the title and the claim of equivalence; U for the grouping and the complaints]: the Theosophical seven is one way of dividing a person, from the Arhat side. The Brahmanical way groups fewer divisions, as bodies and states (gross, subtle, causal, with the Self as witness). The counts do not conflict, because the number of principles depends on how finely you cut, and both describe the same facts. He also complained that Sinnett's Sanskrit names did not match their Sanskrit sense (buddhi is the discriminating intellect, not a "spiritual soul"). This is the honest method: it concedes that vocabularies differ and offers a way to read them as compatible [M]. Read the original and find the sentence before quoting [U].
- Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold person. Advaita uses the five sheaths (Taittiriya Upanishad 2) and, in later Advaita manuals, the three bodies (gross, subtle, causal). The Mandukya Upanishad gives four states of consciousness (waking, dream, deep sleep, turiya); the doctrine of three bodies belongs to later Advaita and not to the Upanishad (a correction to one file's "three bodies with four states") [M]. Mapping Buddhist aggregates onto Vedantic sheaths is a comparer's move [M].
- His dissent from Blavatsky and Sinnett, as remembered [U for every specific point; M only that there was an open disagreement and that he was the Society's authority on Sanskrit and Vedanta]: (a) the number and kind of principles; (b) whether Atma is a principle of a person (for an Advaitin the Atman is the one reality, not a part of anything, and the individual is its conditioned appearance); (c) the Sanskrit sense of buddhi and manas; (d) aspects of the Logos doctrine in the Secret Doctrine. He is reported to have declined to contribute to that book (about 1888 [U]). Blavatsky answered him and defended the seven as the older Arhat form. The "same teaching" claim was argued among the comparers themselves: their own Hindu scholar did not agree with their founder on how to describe a person [M]. Who brought sutratman in (Subba Row is believed) is [U] (section 4.4).

## 7. How the Theosophists compare religions: the method and how they explain differences

### 7.1 The moves, with examples

1. One source and genealogy. The Wisdom-Religion is the trunk and the world religions its branches. Likeness proves descent. Pythagoras and the Druids are taken to share the Eastern teaching. The proof is the agreement itself plus a lineage (Ammonius, the Neoplatonists, the Masters). A historical claim, and the weakest part [M].
2. Concordance by term. A word in one language is glossed by a word from another: Parabrahm with the Absolute and the Kabbalistic Boundless; Maya with illusion; Atma with the Over-Soul; Alaya with the Universal Soul, Anima Mundi and the Over-Soul; devachan with Sukhavati; karma with Nemesis; skandhas with karmic germs. The Glossary is that table alphabetised. The equations pass through Sanskrit and Tibetan terms read in nineteenth-century translations (Wilson, Eitel, Beal, Muller's series) [M]. Antoine Faivre (Access to Western Esotericism, 1994) names the practice of concordance as a frequent but secondary component of Western esotericism, beside transmission, and not among his four intrinsic marks (correspondences, living nature, imagination and mediations, the experience of transmutation) [M]. The weakness: each system's own problem drops out. Brahman is a ground of being, Buddhist emptiness is not a One, Ain Soph is a hidden divine infinity [M]. It works where the items really are alike (karma and kamma) and fails where they only look alike (anatta and atman) [M].
3. Concordance by symbol. Egg, serpent, lotus, tree and cross are read as one shared vocabulary with several levels of meaning. Nearly any story can be read this way, so the method cannot be tested [M].
4. Templates and tables. A master list is laid over each religion and an item is found in each. The seven principles are set beside the five sheaths, the three upadhis plus Atma, and Sinnett's seven names. The sevenfold shape is imposed on every source: five Hindu sheaths become seven principles, six Mahayana perfections become seven portals, Puranic cycles become seven Rounds. Comparers and scholars object most here: the fit comes from the template, and the sources are bent to it [M]. Match by position in a sequence (four Buddhist stages become the first four initiations; the gospel life becomes five; the Advaita fourfold means become four qualifications) and by number (Trinity, Trimurti and Osiris, Isis and Horus; seven principles with seven rays, holy immortals, planes, heavens): order is taken to prove identity. Number is the weakest step [M].
5. Outer and inner. Every religion has an outer form for the many and an inner teaching for the few. Where religions seem to disagree, the outer forms disagree and the inner meaning agrees. This lets any difference count as agreement, since the outer is never the real. It cannot be refuted, which is its strength and its flaw [M]. This is also the move Schuon makes with the exoteric and the esoteric. It is the version that Alex's own rule for Part two (public face and inner face) most resembles, and it needs the same care [M].
6. Explaining difference. Corruption by priests, literal reading of allegory, lost keys; adaptation to each people and age, with a new messenger in each age (Buddha, Jesus, Krishna read as teachers of one wisdom; Besant's Four Great Religions); deliberate "blinds"; different temperaments need different religions (Hodson, Bailey's rays); different words for the same item (the Glossaries); and suppression: where the West lacks the doctrine, a church is said to have removed it (Justinian, the Councils of 553 and 869), which historians reject [M]. They do not concede that a tradition may disagree [M].
7. Quote and gloss; pile up authorities. In the notes to the Voice and the Secret Doctrine she quotes Gospel, Gita, Upanishad and Buddhist texts and glosses them in Theosophical terms. Isis cites authorities from Plato, Philo and Origen to Max Muller in great number. Coleman argues that many quotations were second hand. The nosegay and the string (section 3.2.2) [M].
8. Argument from reason. Justice, unequal starts, prodigies, one life too short. These need no text [M].
9. The catalogue. Short quotes sorted by tradition, from Walker to Head and Cranston [M].
10. Authority beyond check. The Masters, and later Leadbeater's clairvoyance and Occult Chemistry, supply what the texts do not; this turns a textual comparison into a report that only the reporters can check [M]. Olav Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, Brill 2001, paperback 2004 [U]) names three recurring strategies in this literature: appeal to tradition (a hidden, ancient, universal wisdom), appeal to science, and appeal to experience [M].
11. Negotiated common ground. Olcott's Fourteen Propositions (1891) are the one case where authorities of two schools were asked to sign [M].
12. Translating and editing named texts. Mead on the Hermetic and Gnostic texts, Subba Row on Vedanta, Judge on the Gita, Olcott's Catechism. The most checkable and the most useful to Alex [M].
13. Different counts, same facts (Subba Row, section 6.7) [M].
14. Splitting the self in two (section 6.5) [M].
15. The counterexample as a check. The Buddha's closed-fist line (DN 16) is the line any claim of a hidden Buddhism must answer [M].
16. The ethical test. The Voice judges the two paths by whether they leave the world to suffer. The cost: the counter-type (Pratyekabuddha) is polemic [M].
17. Assumed older Wisdom-Religion, concealed or corrupted. Differences are loss and concealment [M].

### 7.2 Her stance

- Concordance by term, genealogy, outer and inner. She does not compare to test whether the traditions agree; she compares to show that they do, and she knows the answer in advance [M]. The method cannot fail: agreement confirms her and disagreement is called corruption [M]. It reads Indian and Buddhist terms through a Western occult frame (Buddhist anatta against seven principles) and selects outer forms to suit [M]. The Pali scholars of her time said she and Sinnett misread Buddhism [M].
- Defenders say it was a bold act of respect for Asian traditions in an age of missionary scorn [M].
- Her explanation of agreement is descent from a hidden source, with Masters as the authority. Her authority is a text and teachers that no outsider has checked [M].

### 7.3 Where they equate what specialists keep apart (warning list, all [M] unless marked)

- Parabrahm with Ain Soph. Spirit and Matter as aspects of one root (classical Samkhya is dualist and Advaita non-dual; she blends them). Alaya with a universal soul. Devachan with Sukhavati. Anatta with a "lower self" denial. Pratyekabuddha and Nirmanakaya in her sense. Seven Dhyani-Buddhas; seven Amesha Spentas. Karma with Nemesis. Buddhi with Buddha. Sutratman with a thread through lives. Parabrahm with Law. Paranirvana with a cosmic end. The skandhas with karmic germs of a reincarnating Ego. A Pure Land with a rest between lives.

### 7.4 How this differs from Alex's method

- Blavatsky's explanation is historical (one source, then corruption). Schuon's is metaphysical (one Reality, many forms). Campbell's is psychological. Alex's is a fourth: common logic. The agreement follows from one axiom [M].
- He explains agreement by one axiom and difference by vocabulary and by layers (public teaching, inner teaching). That avoids her weakest parts, the lost original and the corruption story, and keeps her strength: saying where terms may match [M]. His approach needs no hidden teacher and no archive. His claim is the more modest [M].
- Seven (negotiating common ground) and twelve (translating named texts) in the list above are worth copying; the outer-as-veil move used as a rule is the one to avoid [M].
- Hammer's three strategies (tradition, science, experience) are not Alex's method (examples that fit, no proof), and he can say so [M].
- The Traditionalists (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) argue from metaphysical doctrine and a claimed primordial tradition; the experience line (James, Underhill, Stace) argues from mystics' reports. The Theosophists' later heirs report from observation. Alex claims no original, no template and no clairvoyant source [M].

## 8. Lineage, and the relation to the perennial philosophy

### 8.1 Three things called Theosophy

- First, an old word: theosophia, Greek for divine wisdom. From the seventeenth century it named a school of speculative Christian thought around Jakob Boehme, carried on by William Law, Oetinger, Saint-Martin and Swedenborg's followers. Scholars still call Kabbalah and the Shi'a hikma tradition "theosophies" in that technical sense [M].
- Second, a society: the TS of 1875, which drew on Spiritualism, Mesmerism, Freemasonry, Hermetic and Rosicrucian literature, Kabbalah, Emerson's circle and then Indian texts. Blavatsky said her Theosophy was the Wisdom-Religion, the one source of all religions, and that the name came from the school of Ammonius Saccas. Historians treat that genealogy as a nineteenth-century reconstruction [M].
- Third, a way of knowing: teaching said to come from living Masters [M].
- Word hygiene. Scholars use "theosophy" for the Kabbalistic doctrine of the sefirot (Scholem, Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism, 1941; Idel); for Shi'a and Persian hikma, as in Mulla Sadra's The Transcendent Theosophy (al-Hikma al-muta'aliya), built on four journeys of the soul toward the Real and back into creation [M, details U]; and for Boehmian thought. The essay's bibliography already lists Elior on Habad theosophy (The Paradoxical Ascent to God, 1993) [M]. Write "the Theosophical Society" or "Blavatsky's Theosophy" in full, and "theosophy" in lower case only for these technical meanings. Sadra's four journeys are a witness for the arrow of return [M].

### 8.2 Before Blavatsky

- Late antiquity and after. The Tubingen Theosophy (an anthology of pagan oracles and sayings compiled by a Christian, late fifth century, about 500 [M, about 80 percent]) is a late example. Lutheran mystical writers adopted the word, and Gichtel's Amsterdam edition of Boehme (1682) was titled Theosophia revelata [M].
- Boehme (1575 to 1624, a shoemaker of Gorlitz): Aurora (1612), Mysterium Magnum (1623), De signatura rerum (1622), The Way to Christ (1624). His God is an abyss (the Ungrund, the "unground") that becomes manifest to itself through will, opposition and nature. The soul's task is to return [M for the gist; wording and locators U]. Followers: the Philadelphian Society (Jane Leade, London, 1694); William Law (1686 to 1761), after whom the four-volume "Law edition" of Boehme in English (The Works of Jacob Behmen, the Teutonic Theosopher, 1764 to 1781) is named, though it was published after his death and edited by George Ward and Thomas Langcake mostly from the seventeenth-century English translations [M, about 85 percent]; Oetinger, Baader, Saint-Martin; Hegel and Schelling read him. Antoine Faivre calls this "Christian theosophy" [M]. Franz Hartmann, a Theosophist, wrote The Life and Doctrines of Jacob Boehme (1891) [M]. Use: a Western precedent for a whole that reveals itself through opposition; check a source before any sentence about Boehme goes in.
- Swedenborg (1688 to 1772) taught correspondences between the visible and spiritual worlds, close to Blavatsky's "Analogeticists", and described heaven as a Grand Man (Maximus Homo) in which each soul and community has a place like an organ (Heaven and Hell, 1758, about sections 59 to 102 [U]). He rejected reincarnation: one earthly life, then development in the afterlife. His followers formed a "Theosophical Society" in London in 1783 [U]. Emerson wrote on him (Representative Men, 1850) [M]. The Grand Man is a Western picture very near Alex's fingers of one hand, and Swedenborg's denial of rebirth shows that the Christian theosophical stream did not supply the reincarnation in the Theosophical package [M].
- Saint-Martin (1743 to 1803), "the Unknown Philosopher" (his own pen name), wrote Des erreurs et de la verite (1775), Le Tableau naturel (1782), L'Homme de desir (1790) and translated Boehme into French. His teacher Martines de Pasqually (died 1774) left a Traite de la reintegration des etres (written about 1770, printed 1899) about spirits fallen into matter and their return; the Elus Coens and later Martinism came from this [M]. "Reintegration" is an old Western word for the axiom's third clause [M].
- The Society's milieu. Masonry supplied lodge form, degrees and the "Unknown Superiors" trope (the Strict Observance), ancestor of the Masters. Rosicrucian texts (Fama Fraternitatis, 1614) and the Kabbala Denudata (1677 to 1684) fed the Hermetic and Kabbalistic parts. Mesmerism and Eliphas Levi supplied the "astral light". Kingsford and Maitland's The Perfect Way is a parallel body [M]. Godwin argues Theosophy continues eighteenth-century esotericism; Campbell is the standard history. A safe sentence: "Theosophy grew from Spiritualism, Freemasonry and the older Hermetic and Christian mystical traditions" [M].
- Emerson. His essay "The Over-Soul" (Essays, First Series, 1841) pictures one soul of the whole in which each person takes part, and says we live in division, in parts [M]. The third proposition of the Secret Doctrine is the clearest historical forerunner of Alex's axiom; offer it as "an 1888 statement of the same shape" after checking the wording [M].
- Plotinus, the structure behind the axiom. Enneads IV.9 ("Are all souls one?" in MacKenna's title) argues that all souls are one and many [M; Armstrong's Loeb title U]. Neoplatonism's pattern of remaining, procession and return (Proclus, Elements of Theology, propositions about 30 to 35) and Plotinus's closing line of the Enneads (VI.9.11, Greek phyge monou pros monon, usually rendered "flight of the alone to the Alone" but worded differently by different translators, MacKenna's recalled as something like "the passing of solitary to solitary" [U, about 65 percent]) are the shortest ancient form of one, separation, return [M for the ideas; locators U]. Do not copy the English wording from this dossier. Eriugena's Periphyseon carries the scheme into the Latin West as procession and return (processio, reditus; "exitus and reditus" is a later scholars' label [U]). This is the lineage Blavatsky claimed through Ammonius, and Alex can use Plotinus directly, with a checked translation (MacKenna is public domain), naming the translator [M].
- Mead's Hermetic and Gnostic texts: section 5.7.

### 8.3 How "perennial philosophy" is not one book

- The idea and the name. Plethon (1438), Ficino and Pico (the "prisca theologia"), and Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), which gave the phrase wide currency [M]. Leibniz used "philosophia perennis" in a letter to Nicolas Remond, 26 August 1714 [C, repository notes; other files say 1714 or 1715, U]. Huxley's Foreword credits Leibniz with coining the phrase, which is wrong, since Steuco is earlier; the standard scholarly correction is Charles B. Schmitt, "Perennial Philosophy: From Agostino Steuco to Leibniz", Journal of the History of Ideas 27 (1966) [M on the paper; U on the year]. Steuco meant a deposit of revealed truth that the Church guarded; Leibniz meant gold panned out of every school [C].
- The movement is a family of houses, not one thing [M]: the older line (Plethon, Ficino, Pico, Steuco, Leibniz); Theosophy; the Traditionalist or Perennialist school (the twentieth-century core, hostile to Theosophy); the popular and anthology line (Huxley 1945; Perry 1971); the academic "common core" line (Stace; the critics Katz and Proudfoot; the replies of Forman and Hick); the developmental or transpersonal line (Wilber).
- One true thing to say: the perennial philosophy is a family of related claims with real disagreements inside it (the Traditionalists and the Theosophists disagree about rebirth, initiation, Masters and progress), not one doctrine and not one book [M].
- Huxley's place. The Perennial Philosophy (1945; British edition 1946) is an anthology of passages under themes with his commentary, the best known book under that name and a late voice in a body of work that existed before him. His Foreword defines the thing in three parts: a metaphysic that recognises a divine Reality substantial to the world of things, lives and minds; a psychology that finds in the soul something similar to, or even identical with, that Reality; an ethic that places the final end of man in the knowledge of the immanent and transcendent Ground of all being [gist M; wording U; the repository notes say it has been checked only through secondary quotation]. His phrase for the thing is recalled as "the Highest Common Factor in all preceding and subsequent theologies" [M, not for quotation]. Chapter 1 is "That Art Thou"; the chapter on the saint is chapter 3 ("Personality, Sanctity, Divine Incarnation") and chapter 14 ("Immortality and Survival") speaks to lifetimes [U]. He read the mystics in English translations of his day (for Eckhart, Pfeiffer's 1857 edition, which mixes authentic and doubtful texts, is the usual source behind such anthologies) [M; which translation U]. He belonged to the Vedanta circle in California and was a friend of Krishnamurti [M]; he drew on Coomaraswamy [U on the extent]. Blavatsky comes well before him: Isis Unveiled (1877) 68 years, the Secret Doctrine (1888) 57, the Key (1889) 56 [M].
- Use of Huxley in the essay: say "best known", not "best"; use his three clauses only as a handy outline; do not use him as the voice of the Traditionalists or of Blavatsky [M].

### 8.4 The Traditionalists and the feud with Theosophy

- Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951) was in the Paris occult milieu (Gnostic Church, Martinism, Masonry), left it, and wrote Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923). The gist of the attack [M, details unchecked]: Theosophy mixes Western occultism with a distorted Orientalism; it lacks regular initiatory transmission; its Masters are unverifiable; it rests on a modern belief in progress and evolution; its reincarnation is a misreading. He also attacked Besant's Krishnamurti project [M]. Whether he aimed more at the Adyar wing than at Blavatsky herself is [U]; the book attacks Theosophy as a whole. Coomaraswamy and Schuon shared the hostility [M for Guenon; U for the other two on Theosophy specifically]. Do not say "all perennialists attacked Theosophy": Huxley did not [M].
- Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (Oxford, 2004): Traditionalism took shape partly against Theosophy while sharing its shape (a primordial tradition, an esoteric core, cycles), and owed much to the occult milieu it condemned [M].
- The hidden centre. Blavatsky has her Masters and Guenon has his Agarttha (Le Roi du monde, 1927). Guenon denied the likeness. Schuon and Coomaraswamy have no such doctrine. It is not a shared doctrine of the two movements [M].
- Where they agree, in outline (the files' mapping of two sets of ideas, not the authors' own claim of agreement) [M]: (a) one ultimate Principle beyond the world (Parabrahman for Blavatsky; the Absolute, "Beyond-Being" for the Traditionalists); (b) the human core is not other than it (the third proposition; the second clause of Huxley's definition; Schuon; Coomaraswamy's one Self); (c) reality has levels (planes; states of being); (d) the world is veil or maya; (e) religions are forms of one truth with an inner and an outer side; (f) liberation is return, by knowledge and conduct; (g) modern materialism misses all of it; (h) both take the Hindu cyclic scheme (yugas, a present dark age) seriously. Points (a), (b), (d), (e) and (f) can be said as "both movements point to" with little risk if the wording is kept general.
- Where they diverge [M]:
  - Reincarnation. Theosophy teaches repeated rebirth of the higher individuality (and changed its own teaching between 1877 and the 1880s). Guenon rejected it as an error, teaching that the being passes to other states of existence and cannot re-occupy the same state (carried from a first-pass search [C]); the Traditionalists are not one voice here. The Islamic members (Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt) hold the mainstream Islamic position of one earthly life, barzakh and resurrection [M for the position of Islam; U for what each says in print]. Swedenborg also rejected rebirth.
  - Initiation. Open membership with a later Esoteric Section (1888) and graded "initiations" under Besant and Leadbeater, against regular transmission in an existing tradition (Guenon's Shadhili initiation of 1912 [M]; Schuon's Maryamiyya of about 1965, a branch of the Alawiyya, with a first initiation recalled at 1932 to 1933 [U]).
  - Orthodoxy. "No religion higher than truth", with no need to belong to any religion, against the need for a real tradition and its forms.
  - Masters against lineages. Living Mahatmas as source against authority from actual lineages and from intellectual intuition.
  - Direction of history. Evolution of souls and races against cyclic decline; Perry's The Widening Breach (1995) attacks evolutionism.
  - Attitude to modernity. Theosophy sought a synthesis of science, religion and philosophy (the subtitle of the Secret Doctrine); Traditionalism is anti-modern.
  - Method. The Traditionalists start from a metaphysical principle and show that each authentic tradition states it in its own terms; differences are different forms revealed for different peoples, each valid, the unity at the formless level and not in history, with no claim of a single historical source [M].
- Rival vocabularies, to keep apart: Masters, root races, Wisdom-Religion are Theosophical; exoteric and esoteric, Primordial Tradition, religio perennis, sophia perennis are Traditionalist [M]. Do not credit a Traditionalist with Blavatsky's descent claim or Blavatsky with their metaphysics.
- Where Alex says "the perennial philosophy and Theosophy", he is joining two groups whose members would object to each other. A one-line note will do. The Traditionalists would reject a seeker with no tradition and condemned Theosophy, which he also leans on; Alex borrows the comparisons and not the rules [M].
- Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947). He joined the Theosophical Society around 1907 [C; the year U] and left it later; his mature writing followed his correspondence with Guenon in the 1930s [M]. The Theosophical Forum (Pasadena, Point Loma lineage, March 1944) reviewed his Hinduism and Buddhism as showing Hinduism as one face of the philosophia perennis [C]. "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3, 1944) argues from the Upanishads that the one thing passing through all lives is the one Self, not a separate personal soul; it opens with Shankara's dictum (Brahma Sutra Bhashya 1.1.5) in a translator's English, "there is no other transmigrant but the Lord" [C; locator U]. The Traditionalist text nearest Alex's axiom, and it separates him from the Theosophical individual-ego model; its price is that the individual is not what carries over [M]. Whether Coomaraswamy repeated Guenon's rejection of individual return in print is [U]. Eliade's 1979 essay "Some Notes on Theosophia Perennis: Ananda K. Coomaraswamy and Henry Corbin" (History of Religions 19:2, pp. 167 to 176) uses "theosophia" in the old sense and is no evidence of a link between Coomaraswamy and the Society [M; citation C].
- Schuon (1907 to 1998). De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953). The thesis: religions differ in outer forms (exoteric) and converge in an inner, formless reality (esoteric); "religio perennis" is his name for it; initiation and orthodoxy within one tradition are the way. The unity lies above the forms, not in their overlap, and he does not say the outer forms are equal. The cleanest answer to why religions differ, and a Traditionalist answer, not a Theosophical one; close to Alex's own correction of 2026-10-07 (public understanding against inner teaching) [M]. Publishing detail: an edition with a foreword or introduction by Huston Smith (Harper Torchbooks, 1975; a later Quest Books edition from the Theosophical Publishing House) [U for all of it]. Serious allegations have been reported about Schuon's circle; cite his books, not his authority [M, carried].

(1975); Pallis, A Buddhist Spectrum (1980); Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981); Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a very large thematic anthology and the school's counterpart to Huxley's; Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976; subtitle The Primordial Tradition, later The Common Vision of the World's Religions; "primordial tradition" is Guenon's phrase) and The World's Religions (first The Religions of Man, 1958); Sedgwick (2004) and Oldmeadow (2000, sympathetic) as guides to the school; non-Traditionalist comparers: James (1902), Underhill, Mysticism (1911), Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926, English 1932), Stace (1960), Watts, The Supreme Identity (1950), Wilber (1977, 1980), Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence (2006), Panikkar. - On stages the schools differ in kind. Theosophy and Wilber describe growth through time (a Path, initiations, a spectrum). The Traditionalists describe degrees of Reality and cycles of the world and treat the evolutionary picture and reincarnationism as modern errors. Alex's arrow is a growth picture, so its natural comparers are Theosophy, Wilber and Underhill (whose Mysticism lays out five stages: awakening, purification, illumination, the dark night, union; exact labels U), not Guenon or Schuon. Where he cites the Traditionalists, cite them for "different outer forms, same inner truth", not for stages [M].
- Critics of the common-core project, to name once: Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957); Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978), the constructivist argument; Proudfoot (1985); Forman (ed.), The Problem of Pure Consciousness (1990), a reply; Hick, An Interpretation of Religion (1989), a pluralist who accepts mediation and still argues for one Real; Ferrer (2002); Richard King, Orientalism and Religion (1999); Prothero, God Is Not One (2010) [M]. Katz's attack aims in part at Stace and bites on claims about experiences, not on the more modest claim that traditions say similar things [M].
- The Traditionalists are not Theosophical, but several perennialists came through Theosophical doors: Alan Watts (London Buddhist Lodge at about fifteen, founded 1924 by Christmas Humphreys as a Theosophical lodge) [M]; Joseph Campbell (Krishnamurti a young man's first door to Indian thought [U]; Eranos founded by Olga Frobe-Kapteyn, who had a Theosophical background [M on Frobe-Kapteyn; U on Campbell's first contact]); Ken Wilber's first books came out from Quest Books, a Theosophical Publishing House imprint [U]; Huston Smith reached the primordial tradition through Schuon, Huxley and Gerald Heard more than through Theosophy [M for the Schuon link; the rest U]. ## 9. Scholarly assessment and influence (for the author, not the essay)

### 9.1 The headline

Scholars of religion no longer ask whether Blavatsky was right about an ancient wisdom. They ask what the Theosophical Society was and what it did. The answers fall into four groups: her sources; her authority; her influence; the harm [M]. The "ancient wisdom" claim is treated as a legitimising move to be studied (Hanegraaff, Hammer), not adopted. Open: whether Blavatsky was a fraud, a sincere creator of fictions, or both. Scholarship does not need the answer [M].

The file-writers' own summary judgements, all [M], offered as pointers: "a skilled synthesizer with a poor way of settling disputes between sources, and good primary evidence of what Theosophists believed and equated"; "early and sincere comparers, whose method mixes sound observation (karma is karma) with strained equations (anatta as a lower self)"; "best used as a witness to the idea of unity in nineteenth-century English, and not as an authority on any tradition".

### 9.2 Sources and the accuracy of her comparing

- Coleman. William Emmette Coleman, an American Spiritualist writer who read Sanskrit, set Isis Unveiled and the Secret Doctrine beside the books she had access to. He argued that most quotations and doctrines were taken, often at second hand, from nineteenth-century works, and that the Stanzas were her own composition. The piece is "The Sources of Madame Blavatsky's Writings", an appendix to Vsevolod Solovyoff, A Modern Priestess of Isis (translated and abridged by Walter Leaf for the SPR, London, 1895), recalled as Appendix C, about pp. 353 to 366 [M; U for appendix letter and pages]. Cite 1895 as the printing; the date 1893 in Alex's brief may belong to Solovyoff's Russian original (Russkii Vestnik serial 1892, book 1893 [U]). Sources he stressed for the Secret Doctrine: Wilson's Vishnu Purana, Winchell's World-Life, Donnelly's Atlantis, Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism; for Buddhism and Tibet, Eitel, Csoma de Koros, Schlagintweit, Koppen [M]. Coleman was a hostile partisan: a prosecutor's brief with solid parallels. Theosophical editors (de Zirkoff) traced many of the same quotations and answered that Victorian authors borrowed freely. Later scholars accept heavy borrowing and argue about what it shows: fraud, or a collage of the period that she partly described herself as a nosegay [M]. Use: do not treat her as a primary witness for what a tradition teaches; cite the primary text and cite her as a comparer [M].
- Name caution. A Modern Priestess of Isis is by Vsevolod Sergeyevich Solovyov (1849 to 1903), a novelist and the elder brother of the philosopher Vladimir Solovyov (1853 to 1900) [M, 90 percent]. The English title page spells the author Solovyoff. Whether Vladimir wrote on Theosophy is [U]. Project notes should not mix them up.
- Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment (SUNY, 1994): a European lineage in an eighteenth- and nineteenth-century occult underground (Masonic and Rosicrucian orders, Neoplatonism, mesmerism, Swedenborg, Spiritualism); less original, and less Eastern at the start, than its self-image [M]. With Chanel and Deveney, The Hermetic Brotherhood of Luxor (1995) [M].
- Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture (Brill, 1996; SUNY, 1998) and Esotericism and the Academy (Cambridge, 2012): Theosophy was the main bridge from older esotericism and Spiritualism to the New Age; occultism in his technical sense: esotericism adapted to a disenchanted, scientific world, with evolution as a core idea; the ancient wisdom claim descends from the Renaissance prisca theologia [M]. His view of Bailey as the main bridge to the New Age is in 5.10.
- Hammer, Claiming Knowledge (section 7.1) and Hammer and Rothstein (eds), Handbook of the Theosophical Current (Brill, 2013) [M].
- Bevir (1994): the eastward turn (section 2.6) [M].
- Lopez, Prisoners of Shangri-La (1998) and The Tibetan Book of the Dead: A Biography (2011): her Buddhism and her Tibet came from a few European books (Csoma de Koros, Schlagintweit, Koppen, Hodgson on Nepal, Eitel) plus her own invention; "Svabhavat" came from Brian Hodgson's account of the Svabhavika school of Nepal; "Dzyan" is usually explained as a variant of dhyana; her Mahatmas of Tibet have no corroboration in Tibetan sources. The Theosophical Masters turned Tibet from "degenerate Lamaism" in missionary writing into a land of hidden wisdom, which fed Shangri-La, Roerich and Bailey. Evans-Wentz joined the Society in about 1901 and gave the Bardo Thodol its English title and a Theosophical gloss (1927). The colourful chakra system common in the West comes from Woodroffe's The Serpent Power (1919) and Leadbeater's The Chakras (1927), not from a classical source (Karl Baier and others [U]) [M]. Do not cite Blavatsky to explain Tibetan Buddhism [M].
- Chajes, Recycled Lives: A History of Rebirth in Blavatsky's Theosophy (Oxford University Press, 2019). The subtitle says "Rebirth", not "Reincarnation", at about 80 percent from memory in several files; one file wrote "Reincarnation". Open the title page; use the short title and publisher until then [M, subtitle U]. Chajes and Huss (eds), Theosophical Appropriations (2016) shows how traditions are translated and reshaped [M].
- The biographies. Meade, Madame Blavatsky: The Woman Behind the Myth (1980): sceptical, a gifted fraud, with a private life Theosophists contest. Washington, Madame Blavatsky's Baboon (Secker and Warburg, 1993): witty, hostile, a history of the "Western guru" from Blavatsky to Krishnamurti, Gurdjieff and Steiner; the subtitle differs by edition (UK "Theosophy and the Emergence of the Western Guru" [M]; the US Schocken paperback of 1995 has a longer subtitle about mystics, mediums and misfits [U]); check his facts. Cranston, HPB (1993): the sympathetic insider. Lachman, Madame Blavatsky: The Mother of Modern Spirituality (2012): the balanced middle. Campbell, Ancient Wisdom Revived (University of California Press, 1980): a sociologist's history of the whole movement from the founding to Bailey, the standard general reference and now dated. Lavoie, The Theosophical Society: The History of a Spiritualist Movement (2012): more Spiritualist than it admitted. K. Paul Johnson, In Search of the Masters (1990), The Masters Revealed (SUNY, 1994), Initiates of the Theosophical Masters (SUNY, 1995): the Masters were historical Indians (Morya was Maharaja Ranbir Singh of Kashmir, Koot Hoomi the Sikh reformer Thakar Singh Sandhanwalia) and the "Great White Lodge" is a myth (his subtitle); most academics treat the identifications as ingenious and speculative and Theosophists reject them because they make the Masters ordinary men; do not rely on it. Santucci edited Theosophical History from 1985 and is the careful archival voice. Rudbog and Sand (eds), Imagining the East: The Early Theosophical Society (Oxford University Press, 2020), is the recent study (an edited volume) [M for all].
- Tillett, The Elder Brother (1982), the standard Leadbeater biography, including the 1906 scandal; Nethercot (1960, 1963) and Taylor (1992) on Besant; Prothero (1996) on Olcott [M].

### 9.3 What specialists judge wrong, by tradition [M]

- Hinduism: real Sanskrit terms in a re-arranged Western scheme. The sevenfold constitution is not a classical Indian one. Rebirth as a ladder of evolution is a nineteenth-century Western idea laid over Indian sources, with the qualification in section 4.4 (Gita 6.41 to 45).
- Buddhism: "esoteric Buddhism" is a misnomer, the Buddha taught no secret doctrine (DN 16), and an enduring reincarnating Ego strains against anatta. Max Muller said in 1893 there is no esoteric Buddhism and attacked the movement as unhistorical [C for the claim; M for the article title].
- Tibet: the Mahatmas and the Book of Dzyan have no Tibetan source.
- Method: its history (Lemuria, Atlantis, root races, the Stanzas) is fantasy, and its authority rested on unverifiable Masters and proven borrowing.
- The "ancient wisdom" claim, three treatments. As history it fails: no primordial religion has been found, the Hermetic books were redated by Casaubon (1614), and Egyptian, Indian and Greek religions are not one teaching. As an actors' category it is a rhetorical strategy (Hanegraaff, Hammer, von Stuckrad 2005). As reception history it shows how traditions are translated and reshaped (Chajes and Huss). Richard King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) reads the "mystic East" as an Orientalist construction. Defenders such as Huston Smith (a primordial tradition, Forgotten Truth, 1976) and Wilber (a perennial philosophy of levels) keep a form of it. Walker, The Ancient Theology (1972) and Yates (1964) on the prisca theologia [M].
- Credited by historians: it gave Asian religions a hearing in the West and gave some Asians self-respect under colonial rule; it spread karma, reincarnation, states after death and the habit of comparative reading. It was a main channel and not the only one: Kardec's Spiritism came first. Historians credit the TS with being among the first Western organisations to treat Asian religions as sources of truth, not as errors [M]. "One of the first organised bodies to compare religions" is defensible (Max Muller's Sacred Books of the East began in 1879, Kardec in 1857); avoid "first". Earlier organised comparers exist, for example the Brahmo Samaj (1828) and the Free Religious Association in Boston (1867 [U]), so "an early, international one" is safer [M].

### 9.4 Influence in Asia

- Ceylon and Buddhism. Olcott (section 5.3). Critics of "Protestant Buddhism" argue the revival has older local roots (eighteenth-century monastic reform) and that "Protestant" overstates the debt (Anne Blackburn, Locations of Buddhism, 2010) [M]. If Alex says the Theosophists revived Buddhism in Sri Lanka, scholars would say they helped shape and fund it and did not start it. Tweed, The American Encounter with Buddhism, 1844 to 1912 (1992) [M].
- Dharmapala. Anagarika Dharmapala (1864 to 1933) joined the Society in 1884, met Blavatsky (when and where U), took her advice to study Pali and became its most important Asian recruit. In 1891 he founded the Maha Bodhi Society to recover Bodh Gaya and in 1893 he spoke at the Chicago Parliament. He later distanced himself from Theosophy because its Masters and its mixing of schemes conflicted with his Theravada; the date of the break is U. The best example of an Asian who used the Theosophical frame and then left it (Kemper, Rescued from the Nation, 2015; Prothero) [M].
- McMahan, The Making of Buddhist Modernism (Oxford, 2008): modern Buddhism is a new form shaped by Protestantism, Romanticism, scientific rationalism and esotericism, with three marks (detraditionalisation, demythologisation, psychologisation); Theosophy is one channel; he does not call the result inauthentic [M].
- India. Besant (section 5.4). A. O. Hume, a Theosophist for a time (joined 1880), founded the Congress in 1885 [M]. Lubelsky, Celestial India (2012), argues that Theosophy fed early Indian national consciousness. The Society allied with Dayananda's Arya Samaj in 1878 and broke with it by 1882 [U on dates]. Nehru was introduced to Theosophy by a tutor (F. T. Brooks [U]) and enrolled as a boy (his Autobiography) [M]. Vivekananda (1863 to 1902) kept his distance from the Society and from Spiritualism and disliked the claim to Masters [U]; De Michelis notes that his Raja Yoga (1896) still draws on the same Western esoteric field [M]. Viswanathan, Outside the Fold (1998) [M]. Theosophy made many educated Indians feel that a Western audience respected their texts, and it was also a Western reading of them [M].
- Gandhi and the Key (the version corrected across the files). In London, about 1889 to 1890 [U] (he arrived in September 1888; his text places the meeting towards the end of his second year in England), two Theosophist brothers, who had been reading Edwin Arnold's translation of the Gita (The Song Celestial), asked him to read the Sanskrit original with them. By his own account he had not read it at all, in Sanskrit or in Gujarati, was ashamed to say so, and read it with them; he read Arnold's translation himself later. He read Blavatsky's Key to Theosophy, which, by his account, made him want to read books on Hinduism and cured him of the missionaries' idea that it was full of superstition. He met Blavatsky and Besant and was invited to join; he declined, saying he knew too little of his own religion to belong to any body. Source: Gandhi, An Autobiography, Part I, chapter XX, "Acquaintance with Religions" [number and title U; gist M]. A retrospective account written in the 1920s [M]. Paraphrase only; open the chapter before quoting. Earlier files had it differently ("gave him Arnold's Gita and urged him to read the Key"; "knew the Gita in a translation"); the corrected version above is the better recall but is still memory. A bridge to the talk's Gandhi; it shows what the Key did: it sent a reader back to his own scriptures [M].

### 9.5 Influence in the West

- Kandinsky read Blavatsky, Besant and Leadbeater (Thought-Forms, 1901) and Steiner, and in Concerning the Spiritual in Art treated the Theosophical movement as a spiritual force (Ringbom, The Sounding Cosmos, 1970). Mondrian joined the Dutch Theosophical Society in 1909. Hilma af Klint, Scriabin, Yeats and AE (George Russell) moved in the circle. Yeats met Blavatsky in 1887, joined the Esoteric Section in 1888 and left in 1890 [U]. Carlson (No Religion Higher Than Truth, Princeton, 1993) shows its large role in Russian culture; Dixon (Divine Feminine, 2001) shows it as a route for women's work in England; Owen (The Place of Enchantment, 2004) [M; Kandinsky's formal membership U].
- Steiner split off and founded Anthroposophy (1912 to 1913); Bailey left in the early 1920s and founded the Arcane School; Krishnamurti was made head of the Order of the Star in the East and dissolved it. Hanegraaff and Campbell show the New Age and the "Ascended Masters" groups continuing these themes [M]. Do not blame Blavatsky for the offshoots: most took her scheme and changed it [M].
- The perennialists who came through Theosophical doors are in section 8.4.

### 9.6 Race, Ariosophy and misuse, stated carefully

- What the Secret Doctrine says (volume 2): seven root races, each with seven sub-races, and the ranking of living peoples described in section 4.8 [M]. The content is unacceptable now; a writer who touches it should say so briefly.
- The defence and the critics. Santucci argues, in "The Notion of Race in Theosophy" (Nova Religio 11, 2008, pp. 37 to 63 [U on pages]), that Theosophical "race" meant stages of spiritual and cosmic evolution and not a biological hierarchy of living peoples, and that Blavatsky attacked colonial racism. Critics reply that the vocabulary of higher and lower races was there and was borrowed from her century [M]. Say both things: the First Object (brotherhood without distinction of race) and the root-race scheme sat together [M].
- Ariosophy. Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke (The Occult Roots of Nazism, 1985; Hitler's Priestess, 1998; Black Sun, 2002) showed that Austrian and German groups around Guido von List and Jorg Lanz von Liebenfels fused the Theosophical root-race Aryan, Atlantis and an esoteric hierarchy with pan-German racial nationalism. "Ariosophy" is Lanz's term (1915). The Thule Society and Sebottendorf touched the early Nazi party and Himmler used Wiligut. Goodrick-Clarke is careful: these were fringe groups, the main effect was an ideological climate, and the regime later suppressed them; he does not say Theosophy caused Nazism. Later work (Hermann Gilbhard, Die Thule-Gesellschaft, 1994 [U on first name and year]) cuts the Thule claims back further [U]. Sensational books (Pauwels and Bergier, 1960) made the popular legend. Savitri Devi is a Hindu-Aryan variant [M]. Historians debate how far this implicates Theosophy itself; none says Theosophy is Nazi [M].
- Ramaswamy (2004) follows Lemuria from a zoologist's land bridge of 1864 (Sclater) through Haeckel to Theosophy; plate tectonics removed the geological basis. Trautmann, Aryans and British India (1997), traces the British Indian side of "Aryan" [M].
- If anyone asks, a safe reply: Theosophy's cosmic race scheme was one raw material among several for a small racial-occult fringe, and the Nazi regime banned Theosophy and Anthroposophy [M; the dates and the 1937 ban on Theosophical lodges U].
- Staudenmaier on Steiner: section 5.9. Bailey's texts: section 4.8 [U]. Theosophical root-race theory also ranks primal peoples in a racial scheme [M].

## 12. Quotation and attribution traps

- "There is no religion higher than truth" is the Society's motto (the family motto of the Maharajas of Benares, adopted about 1880), not a Blavatsky sentence [M].
- The nosegay of culled flowers with only the string that ties them is Montaigne's (Essais III.12). She quotes it [M]. "String" or "thread": U.
- "The heresy of separateness" has no locator in Blavatsky; credit "Theosophical writers" only [U].
- "Look inward: thou art Buddha" is unplaced in the Voice and circulates credited to many [U].
- "Mind is the great Slayer of the Real" and "Thou canst not travel on the Path before thou hast become that Path itself" circulate on quote sites with the attribution dropped or changed; they are the Voice's in recall; copy from a printing [M].
- "Before the eyes can see they must be incapable of tears" is Light on the Path, not the Voice [M]. "Kill out all sense of separateness" is Collins [M].
- The "Three Truths" are from Collins's Idyll of the White Lotus (1884), and not the Society's creed [M].
- The "most accomplished, ingenious and interesting impostors in history" sentence belongs to the SPR committee's report of 1885, not to Hodgson alone or to a later author [M].
- "Truth is a pathless land" is Krishnamurti's (3 August 1929), not Blavatsky's or Besant's, though often set beside Theosophical material [M].
- The 553 and 869 council claims are Theosophical, not historical [M]. Jerome's Letter 130 is hostile testimony printed as approval [U]. Origen is a witness for pre-existence, not reincarnation [M].
- The claimed Dalai Lama Foreword to a 1989 Voice edition: no text known [U].
- "Eclectic Theosophical School" for Ammonius is an eighteenth-century label; Pot-Amun is hers [M/U].
- Isis Unveiled did not teach reincarnation as the rule; "Blavatsky taught reincarnation" needs a date [M].
- Devachan: Tibetan bde-ba-can renders Sanskrit sukhavati and is not "the word behind" it; the equation is hers [M].
- Sutratman and Brihadaranyaka 3.7: the thread is the wind [M].
- "Cycle of Necessity" is hers; Herodotus 2.123 does not contain it [M].
- Plutarch's word is "robe" (peplos), not "veil" [M].
- The interval of ten to fifteen centuries between lives is not doctrine [U].
- "Lords of Karma": mostly Besant and Leadbeater, though the Lipika as recorders are Blavatsky's [M/U].
- Dhyani-Buddha (five standard Tibetan Buddhas) and Dhyan-Chohan (her usual word for the angelic rank) are different; the Amesha Spentas are six [M].
- The Letter on God: "Parabrahm is not a God but absolute immutable law" is the Letter's own, claimed "as Buddhists"; Barker and Hao Chin number differently [M].
- Hodgson's report, Harrison's paper and the SPR's later statement: use only the 1885 report and Harrison's paper in the SPR's own journal [M].
- Chajes's subtitle: "Rebirth" or "Reincarnation": open the title page [M/U].
- Solovyoff (Vsevolod) is the author of A Modern Priestess of Isis; the philosopher Vladimir Solovyov is another man [M].
- Gandhi did not already know the Gita when the two Theosophist brothers asked him to read it [M].
- Perennial philosophy traps: "Huxley coined the phrase" is false; "Leibniz coined it" is Huxley's own error (Steuco, 1540); "the doors of perception" is Blake's phrase via Huxley's title; "Truth is one, the sages call it by many names" is the Rigveda (1.164.46) and is often credited to Gandhi or Vivekananda, who quoted it; "He who knows one religion knows none" is Max Muller's [M, from checkers].
- Oneness quotations credited on quote sites to "Blavatsky", "Huxley", "Einstein", "Rumi" or "Eckhart" are often misattributed; quote only from a printed text that has been opened [M].
- Rumi's mineral-plant-animal-man lines circulate in loose English versions: name a translator and a numbered verse [M].
- Do not credit a Traditionalist with Blavatsky's descent claim, or Blavatsky with theirs; do not merge the vocabularies [M].
- Seven principles are Theosophy's count, not Hindu; Besant's Gita year, Judge's Gita as a recension, the authorship of At the Feet of the Master, Krishnamurti's resignation year, Leadbeater's Other Side of Death year: all [U].
