### Interpretation presented as translation

- Line 132, teshuvah / tawba / metanoia (the one Alex flagged): 'means' presents root senses as the meaning. Teshuvah comes from shuv (return), but the ordinary word also means 'answer' and 'repentance', and the idiom lachazor bitshuvah is literally 'to return in answer'. Tawba is from t-w-b (return), and is also the ordinary word for repentance. Metanoia is meta + nous, 'change of mind', and its New Testament sense is repentance. Safest wording: 'come from roots meaning return, turning back, and a change of mind'. The sentence about the 'words for repentance' therefore needs rewording as a whole, not teshuvah alone.
- Line 59, maya: 'its scholars warn that illusion translates the word badly: it means something dependent, neither fully real nor simply unreal' is the Advaita Vedanta (Shankara) interpretation, attributed to all Hindu scholars. The word itself runs from 'magic, creative power' to 'illusion', depending on the school. Narrow it to 'Advaita scholars'.
- Line 60, lila and Brahma Sutra 2.1.33: 'creation play' is accurate (lokavat tu lila-kaivalyam, 'it is mere sport, as in the world'). But the sutra's point is that creation has no need or motive, whereas the essay says 'the logic is the one I just gave: a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view'. That is the logic of the hidden-treasure saying (wanting to be known), not the sutra's, so the sutra reads as agreeing when it is answering a different question. Split them or drop 'the logic is the one I just gave'.
- Line 75, the five parts: 'body, feelings, perceptions, habits and awareness' puts 'habits' on sankhara (mental formations, volitions), an interpretive rendering. 'The first thing the path asks you to drop is the belief in a solid, permanent me' is a reading of the first of the ten fetters (abandoned at stream-entry), not of what the path asks first. Fair as a gloss, but it is presented as plain content.
- Line 77, Ephesians 4:18 and Quran 7:172 under 'the self is built, veiled or forgotten': the ESV wording is right, but the verses' own contexts differ. Paul is speaking of hardened hearts and ignorance among non-believers, and Quran 7:172 is the covenant after which people cannot plead ignorance. The Sufi reading is flagged as a reading ('Sufis read that moment as'), but Ephesians carries no such flag.
- Line 100, Brihadaranyaka 3.7.23: 'the Self is the witness ... no other witness but him' renders drashta (seer) as 'witness' and atman as 'Self'. It is a translator's convention, not a plain gloss, and the essay does not name the translation.
- Line 158, nirvana: 'the ending of ignorance and craving' is a doctrinal definition, not the literal sense. The word is 'blowing out' (as a flame), and the Buddha's own definitions are the end of greed, hatred and delusion. Ignorance and craving come from the dependent-origination and four-truths formulas. Either add 'literally, blowing out' or say 'the ending of craving and ignorance, as the Buddha put it'.
- Line 87, purgatory: 'growth doesn't stop at death for them: the Catholic Church teaches purgatory' stretches the doctrine. Catechism 1030-1031 describes purification, not further growth. Growth fits Gregory of Nyssa (epektasis); purgatory fits it only loosely.
- Line 130, Philippians 2:5-8: 'did not hold on to that status' is a paraphrase of harpagmos, a contested word (ESV: 'did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped'; NRSV: 'something to be exploited'). 'That status' also interprets 'equality with God'. Kenosis = 'self-emptying' is right. If it is meant as a quotation, use the ESV wording.
- Lines 23 and 117, Acharanga and Shantideva: both paraphrases lean on one translator's choice. The essay's 'the one you intend to hurt is as you are' softens what I remember Jacobi (1884) giving as identity ('you are the very one you intend to kill'). Two checkers raised this, and I could not verify it. 'Hurt' for 'kill' is a second softening. For Bodhicaryavatara 8.91, 'all one in wanting happiness' belongs to the Padmakara-style English; the Sanskrit speaks of the world's being alike in suffering and happiness. Name the translation or paraphrase openly.
- Line 115, 'In the Buddhist account nobody [administers karma]' and 'In Advaita, karma belongs to the world of apparent individuals': doctrinal summaries stated flat, with no passage given. Fair as a summary, but they read as textual statements.
- Line 86, gilgul: 'the soul's return in a new body' is the doctrine, not the literal sense (the word means 'rolling' or 'cycle'). It reads as a definition, so no harm, but 'a cycle, the soul's return in a new body' would be exact.
- Lines 63, 89, 134 (closing sentences of the bullet lists): 'Different words, one move', 'The traditions differ on the form, and the axiom needs only that development continues', 'they give the same instruction: stop holding' are the essay's own reading laid over the texts, which say something narrower. This is the declared stance of the essay, so it is a note, not an error, but a reader may take the sentences as what the traditions themselves say.
