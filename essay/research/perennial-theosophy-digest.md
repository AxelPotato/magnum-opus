## 1. The movement is a family, and Huxley is one door into it

You are right that it is much bigger than Huxley. What the files support, as facts and not as a ranking (whether he is "best" is your judgement, and no source ranks the books):

- The phrase is older. Agostino Steuco put "perennial philosophy" on a book in 1540, and Leibniz used it in a letter in 1714. Huxley's Foreword is recalled as crediting Leibniz, which would be a small mistake.
- The strict core is the Traditionalist school: René Guénon (from 1921), Ananda Coomaraswamy, and Frithjof Schuon (The Transcendent Unity of Religions, French 1948, English 1953). They want one tradition, a lineage and initiation, and they say the real unity lies above the forms and cannot be found by averaging them. Around them: Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis, Leo Schaya, Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Whitall Perry and Huston Smith.
- The anthologies: Perry's A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971) is far bigger than Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (1945), which is short, readable and the one most people have met.
- The comparers of experience, who are a separate line: William James (1902), Evelyn Underhill (1911, five stages of the mystic way), Rudolf Otto (Eckhart beside Shankara), W. T. Stace, and R. C. Zaehner, who argued that the mystical types differ. Then Ken Wilber and the transpersonal line.
- Huston Smith (Forgotten Truth, 1976) is the best readable map: four levels of reality and four of selfhood.
- On how Huxley is rated, the files have no sourced statement from the Traditionalists. Say "best known" and leave "best" to yourself.

## 2. Your axiom in their words

Plain: there is one whole; it looks at itself from a vast number of positions, each a life; from inside one position the positions feel separate, which is an accurate report of a local fact read as a fact about the whole; the looking ends when the whole knows itself as one.

In the perennial words, the nearest statement is Schuon's with Guénon's logic under it. The Absolute is also the Infinite. Nothing can lie outside the Infinite, so it includes the possibility of something that looks like not the Absolute. That possibility is Maya: real in its own order, illusory only when taken as separate. The Self (Atma) is the Absolute seen from inside a person. Deliverance is recognition of what was always so. Coomaraswamy adds the pair Self and self, and the claim that the only one who transmigrates is the one Self. Smith makes it readable with his levels. Note the kind of answer: Schuon and Guénon give a logic with no motive (manifestation follows from infinity). Your second clause gives a motive (a whole that wants to know itself needs a position to know from). The oldest motive text is Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.1 to 4, where the Self alone is afraid, takes no delight, wishes for a second and divides in two.

In the Theosophical words, the nearest statement is the opening of Blavatsky's The Secret Doctrine (1888), which has three propositions. As recalled: one boundless principle beyond thought; universes that appear and vanish in cycles; and the third, the identity of all souls with a universal Over-Soul, together with an obligatory pilgrimage for every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Your first and third clauses sit close to her first and third. Her second has no counterpart in your axiom and should not be imported. Her third is one step softer than your first, because her Over-Soul is itself "an aspect of the Unknown Root". Two differences matter for how you present it. She gave it as received teaching from Masters, and you give it as an assumption to be tested for coherence, with no lost original and no hidden teachers. And her reason for the descent is that unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity only by passing through experience, which is close to your "knowing needs a gap". The exact wording of the Proem is [U] in every file, so open the book before you quote it.

Other historical statements of the same three clauses, nearest first (all unchecked): Proclus (remaining, procession, reversion), Plotinus (Enneads IV.9, V.1.1, VI.9.11), Chandogya 6.8.7 and Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10, Emerson's The Over-Soul (1841, the line about living "in succession, in division, in parts, in particles" while within us is "the soul of the whole"), and Sri Aurobindo's The Life Divine (the One involves itself, forgets, evolves back).

## 3. How they compare religions, and why they say the religions differ

The methods the files catalogue (about fourteen; the main ones):

- The thematic anthology (Huxley, Perry, Stace, Wilson, Novak): headings chosen by the anthologist, passages from many traditions set under each. Strength: fast, checkable line by line. Weakness: the headings and the translations already carry the equation, and context is cut. Huxley subtracts to a "highest common factor"; Schuon rejects that arithmetic.
- The parallel-passage method (Coomaraswamy, Otto): one sentence or symbol followed through Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and German, or Eckhart set against Shankara term by term (Godhead and Brahman, God and Ishvara, soul and Atman). Otto also lists the differences.
- The glossary of equated terms (Dara Shikoh in the 1650s, Blavatsky's Key and Glossary, Subba Row, Schuon's grid).
- Outer form and inner teaching (Schuon, Guénon, Besant): disagreement lives on the outside, agreement on the inside. A scholars' complaint is that this rule cannot lose, since any disagreeing text can be filed as outer.
- Levels of reality (Schuon, Smith), stage maps (Underhill, Teresa, Wilber, the Theosophical Path), one Reality through different filters (John Hick), comparison by experience (James, Stace), descent from one lost teaching (Plethon, Steuco, Blavatsky, Guénon in part), and a claimed esoteric revelation (Blavatsky's Masters).

Ten families of answer to "why do the religions differ if they say the same thing": one teaching that decayed; the same idea in other words; one faith in many rites; stages of an education or a teacher adapted to an age; outer form versus inner teaching with the diversity willed; levels of reality; temperament and path; partial standpoints (the blind men and the elephant, Jain many-sidedness); one Real through different filters; and the view that the differences are real and should be argued, not explained away (Griffiths, Zaehner, Katz, Prothero). Each is the comparer's claim and not the tradition's own.

Where you fit: three old explanations for the agreement itself are common revelation, common transmission and common experience. Yours is a fourth, common logic: the ideas recur because they follow from one axiom. That explains why the same ideas turn up. It says nothing about why the words differ, so you can leave that open. Every comparer in history also had a home team (Ficino and Steuco put Christianity on top, Vivekananda put Advaita highest, Dara read the Upanishads in Sufi words), and yours is the axiom, which you already say.

## 4. Idea by idea: who says it best

All unchecked, and "best" here means most useful to you.

- The One and why it separates: Schuon and Guénon for the logic, the Brihadaranyaka for the motive, Smith for the readable version.
- The veil: Shankara's superimposition, Schuon on Maya, James's "filmiest of screens". The sources give two causes (a fault of knowing and a fault of will), and your machine picture is the first and your BE axis the second.
- Who carries over: the Theosophical Ego (lasting individuality, as against the personality) fits your "account, not session". Coomaraswamy's one transmigrant fits the axiom itself. Trap: their Ego is your lasting self and their personality is your ego. Gaudapada (Mandukya Karika 2.32) goes further: from the highest standpoint nobody is bound and nobody is freed.
- Karma as the fingers: Schopenhauer derives compassion from oneness (tat tvam asi) almost exactly as you do, though his ethics ends in denying the will. Also Gita 13.28, Wang Yangming's one body, Marcus Aurelius 8.34, Luria's souls as limbs of Adam. Blavatsky's karma is a law of harmony with no judge ("we reward or punish ourselves"), which is the nearest Theosophical match. In most traditions karma comes first as a law of consequence and unity grounds the ethic as a separate step; deriving karma from oneness has its clean precedents in Schopenhauer, Emerson and Wang Yangming.
- The golden rule sits on three rungs: reciprocity (Hillel, Confucius, Matthew 7:12), analogy (Dhammapada 129, Gita 6.32), identity (Isha Upanishad 6 and 7, Gita 6.29, Paul). The rabbis argued it too: Akiva said love of neighbour is the great principle, and Ben Azzai said the line about Adam's generations is greater.
- Stages: Underhill's five, Teresa's seven mansions, the Jain fourteen gunasthanas, Dante's heavens, the Theosophical seven portals and five initiations, Wilber's levels. The old maps were scale maps too (Dante's rising, the Jain freed soul rising to the edge of the cosmos). I found no map numbered symmetrically about zero. Theosophy has planetary, solar and cosmic Logoi, so a scale outward exists there, but it has no speed picture and no minus four to plus four, so that arrow is your school's own.
- "Birth" after plus four: in Bailey, Besant and Leadbeater "Birth" is the first initiation, so do not say Theosophy supports your usage. Eckhart's birth of God in the soul is followed by a breakthrough into the Godhead, and Gregory of Nyssa's endless stretch toward God both say the stage after the last stage is not an end.
- Letting go: Schuon's discernment, concentration and virtue; Yoga Sutras 1.12 (practice and dispassion in one sentence, which is your "stillness and work together" for Part three); James's "by their fruits"; Wilber's injunction, apprehension, confirmation.

## 5. The two movements split, and it matters for lives

This is the most useful single finding. The two movements you lean on are rivals. Guénon wrote Le Théosophisme (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923) against Blavatsky's movement, while sharing the idea of a primordial teaching. The strict Traditionalists reject the Western doctrine of the same individual returning (Guénon: no state is repeated; Schuon followed in outline; Nasr, Lings and Burckhardt hold the Islamic one life plus the barzakh). Coomaraswamy keeps transmigration and moves the subject to the one Self. Theosophy teaches rebirth, and teaches it as a received law, then argues for it by reason in your own style (unequal starts, prodigies, one life too short).

So on lives you sit with the Theosophists, Lessing (The Education of the Human Race, 1780: why should he not come back as often as he can acquire fresh knowledge) and John Hick, and you should not cite "the perennial philosophy" for it. The same research also says Isis Unveiled (1877) was not a main route to karma and reincarnation: it argued against the reincarnation the Spiritualists promoted, and the doctrine as Theosophy teaches it appears in the 1880s, through Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism (1883), The Secret Doctrine and The Key to Theosophy (1889). Edwin Arnold's The Light of Asia (1879) was another route.

## 7. Traps to avoid (from the files)

- Do not say the Traditionalists are allies of the Theosophists, or that Schuon, Coomaraswamy or Guénon teach the return of the same individual.
- Do not say the Rigveda, the Gita or the Udana were written to say all religions are one. They were not (the Gita's 4.11, 7.21 and 9.23 are inclusivist and rank other worship as imperfect; Quran 5:48 says each community has its own law and way).
- Do not quote "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names" as Rigveda wording, or any Rumi line from a quotation site.
- Misattributions to Blavatsky: the motto "There is no religion higher than truth" came from a Benares family and was adopted by the Society; the nosegay line is Montaigne's; "Kill out all sense of separateness" is Mabel Collins's; "pathless land" is Krishnamurti's. The claims that a Church council banned rebirth (553, 869) are rejected by historians.
- Do not say emptiness is the One. "Not two" does not mean "one", and maya and mithya mean "dependent" more than "illusion".
- Keep any Schuon, Guénon, Coomaraswamy, Perry or Smith sentence as a paraphrase unless you copy a short one from the book with edition and page.
