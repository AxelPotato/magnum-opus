## 1. The picture in brief

Islam and Judaism have the best texts for "one whole, seen from its own side": the Sufi line of Ibn Arabi and Ghazali, and Habad's Tanya. Christianity has the best texts for "the separation ends" and for letting go (Paul, Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Eriugena, Eckhart, Teresa), and the clearest record of the Church policing the strongest language. All three keep a guardrail: union by participation, not identity, and separation as a real descent or fall, not an illusion. The essay should say that once.

Two essay claims could not be sourced. The Baal Shem Tov wrote no book; the hiding-of-the-face and Psalm 16:8 teachings are credited to him in the Hasidic books and are real in gist, but "a test of how we respond" is the weaker half and I could not place it. Eckhart's eye line "raised against him in Cologne in 1326" is unconfirmed; the better attested condemned item is "something in the soul that is uncreated" (bull of 27 March 1329).

Best new finds: Ibn Arabi's Adam chapter (God wants to see Himself and needs a mirror: the essay's "knowing needs a gap"); Ghazali on unity and then trust; Teresa's two windows; Nachman's narrow bridge; Gregory's single humanity. Among modern comparers Griffiths and Abhishiktananda equate, while Panikkar refuses identity and speaks of equivalents, the more careful method.

## 2. Findings

### Judaism

J1. Where the Baal Shem Tov's words come from.
Israel ben Eliezer, the Besht (about 1700 to 1760, Medzhybizh), left no book. His teaching comes through (a) disciples and kin: Jacob Joseph of Polonne, Toldot Yaakov Yosef (1780, often "I heard from my teacher"); the Maggid of Mezritch, Maggid Devarav LeYaakov (1781; critical edition Schatz-Uffenheimer 1976); his grandson Moses Hayyim Ephraim of Sudilkov, Degel Machaneh Ephraim (about 1810 [U]); (b) compilations that mix early and late material: Keter Shem Tov (1780s to 1790s [U]) and Tzava'at HaRibash (1793 [U]; Schochet's Kehot edition 1975); (c) Shivhei HaBesht (1814 to 1815), hagiography; (d) two letters. Rosman, Founder of Hasidism (1996), treats the compilations as late and the man as a charismatic local healer-preacher; Etkes, The Besht (2005), is more generous; Buber retold the tales; Scholem called his Hasidism selective. Tier [M]; dates [U].
Use: "reported, through a disciple" is the right hedge; better "teachings credited to the Baal Shem Tov in the Hasidic books".

J2. The hiding of the face.
Deuteronomy 31:18: God will hide His face (haster astir panai, a doubled verb). Hasidic teaching credited to the Besht hears a hiding within a hiding: feeling that God is hidden is itself the concealment, since no place lacks Him. Likely sources: Degel Machaneh Ephraim on that verse; Keter Shem Tov [U, no paragraph]. A related story: a child hid in a game, nobody looked, and he ran crying to his grandfather R. Baruch of Medzhybizh (the Besht's grandson), who wept and said God says the same: I hide and no one seeks Me (Buber's tales [U]). That is invitation, not test. The word "test" fits a parable credited to the Besht of a king who sets illusory barriers to find which child truly wants him [U]. Tier [M] gist; [U] locator and "test".
Use: "teachings credited to the Baal Shem Tov say that God's hiding is itself a form of presence and a call to look". Drop "a test of how we respond" until a page is found.

J3. Psalm 16:8, shiviti, equanimity.
The verse says, in gist, "I have set the Lord always before me; because He is at my right hand I shall not be moved". Hasidic teaching credited to the Besht (also the Maggid and Elimelech of Lizhensk) hears shiviti ("I have set") as shaveh ("equal"): hishtavut, equanimity, praise and insult weighing the same, is how God stays before you. Sources: Keter Shem Tov and Tzava'at HaRibash [U paragraph]. Background: the Rema's gloss on Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 1:1 opens with shiviti as a great principle, hence the synagogue plaques [M]. Scholars: Scholem, "Devekut, or Communion with God"; Schatz-Uffenheimer, Hasidism as Mysticism (1993), on bittul, hishtavut and ayin as a quietist line. Tier [M] pun and attribution, [U] locator.
Use: sound in gist and it fits Part one. Say "heard the Hebrew for 'I have set' as 'equal'". Pair bittul with hishtavut.

J4. Tanya, chapter 32, the Gate of Unity and Faith, acosmism.
Shneur Zalman of Liadi, Likutei Amarim (Tanya, 1796). Part 1 ch. 2: the soul is "a part of God above, literally". Ch. 32: love of one's fellow Jew rests on one root of all souls; bodies are apart, souls are one, hatred is a fact about bodies and ego; Hillel's "whole Torah" (Shabbat 31a) is cited. Part 2, Sha'ar HaYichud VeHaEmunah: divine speech creates the world at every moment; from the higher view (yichuda ila'ah) it has no independent existence (ein od milvado, Deut 4:35); from the lower (yichuda tata'ah) creatures exist and are nullified before God. Tzimtzum is read as concealment, not withdrawal. Debate: Scholem (Major Trends, lecture 9) used "acosmism", a German word from the Spinoza dispute [M]. Elior, The Paradoxical Ascent to God (1993; Hebrew 1982), reads a deliberate dual perspective: nullified in thought, real in practice, so commandments bind. Jacobs (Seeker of Unity, 1966) and Wolfson (Open Secret, 2009) write on panentheism and the veil [U on positions]. Habad insiders (Schochet 1988) reject "acosmism" as denial of the world; the Mitnagdim (Chaim of Volozhin, Nefesh HaChaim, 1824) feared pantheism yet use two-level language [M]. Tier [M].
Use: the essay's sentence matches Elior. Add "for us it is real and the commandments bind".

J5. Zohar III 73a.
The Torah, the Holy One and Israel are one (Oraita, Kudsha Brikh Hu ve-Yisrael, chad hu [M sense]). Locator III 73a, Acharei Mot [M]; main text or a later layer [U]. The Tanya quotes it [M]. Tishby (1989) reads it as about the Jewish people. Use: keep the word "Israel".

J6. The rabbinic cluster ([M] for sense, [U] for small numbering).
- Sotah 1:7: "with the measure a man measures, they measure to him" (Samson, Absalom). Retribution fitted to the deed.
- Avot 4:2, Ben Azzai: one commandment draws another, one transgression another. Habit and consequence, nearer a chain than fingers.
- Eduyot 2:10: Akiva puts the judgment of the wicked in Gehinnom at twelve months, Yohanan ben Nuri shorter [attribution U]. Rabbinic Gehinnom is mostly temporary and purifying.
- Shevuot 39a (also Sanhedrin 27b): all Israel are guarantors for one another (kol Yisrael arevim zeh ba-zeh). Liability, not oneness.
- Shabbat 31a: Hillel's negative golden rule. Sifra Kedoshim 4:12: Akiva calls Lev 19:18 the great principle.
- Genesis Rabbah 24:7: Ben Azzai prefers "This is the book of the generations of Adam" (Gen 5:1), lest you say, since I was shamed let my fellow be shamed; R. Tanhuma adds: know whom you shame, for in God's likeness He made him. Ground: common descent and the divine image; the nearest rabbinic text to the essay's logic.
- Sanhedrin 4:5 (not in the essay): Adam was made single so none can say "my father is greater"; destroying one soul is destroying a world. Quran 5:32 parallels it. Some manuscripts add "of Israel" [U].
- A parable of a hand cutting the other hand, attached to Lev 19:18, may be in Yerushalmi Nedarim 9:4 [U]; if found, a Jewish fingers image.
Use: these rest on reciprocity, descent and the image of God, not literal oneness. Print them as the same ethic, not the same metaphysics.

J7. Luria and Vital.
Isaac Luria (1534 to 1572, Safed) wrote almost nothing; Hayyim Vital recorded the system (Etz Chaim; Shemonah She'arim, including Sha'ar HaGilgulim). The infinite withdraws to leave a space (tzimtzum); light enters; the first vessels shatter (shevirat ha-kelim); sparks fall into husks; people repair the break (tikkun) by deed and intention. All human souls are contained in Adam's one soul, which divides. Gilgul is a soul's passage through lives to complete its repair; ibbur is a temporary joining. Scholars: Scholem, Major Trends lecture 7; Fine, Physician of the Soul, Healer of the Cosmos (2003). Tier [M]; gate and chapter [U].
Use: line 75 is accurate. It is the Jewish text nearest "lives and a continuing soul", and it makes separation a catastrophe, not an illusion. Say so once.

J8. Abulafia.
Abraham Abulafia (b. Zaragoza 1240, active to about 1291) taught meditation on letters of the divine names with breath and chant to "untie the knots" (hatarat ha-kesharim) binding the soul to nature, so the freed intellect joins the divine; knower, knowing and known become one [wording U]. Idel (1988) uses him against Scholem to show Jewish unio mystica. Use: "untying knots" is a letting-go image outside Hasidism. Add only after opening Idel.

J9. Nachman of Breslov, Likutei Moharan II:48.
Nachman (1772 to 1810): the whole world is a very narrow bridge, and the main thing is not to be afraid at all (ve-ha-ikar lo lefached klal [M tag; Breslov texts vary between lefached and yitpached, U]). LM II:24 calls constant joy a great commandment; the practice is hitbodedut, talking to God alone outdoors [M]. Use: a Jewish line on fear released, the braced-to-released axis, in one sentence.

### Islam

I1. Quranic verses (renderings from memory; Pickthall, Arberry, Yusuf Ali and Abdel Haleem read alike on most of these as far as I recall, and differ where noted).
- 2:115: "wherever you turn, there is the face (wajh) of God" [M]. Pickthall "countenance", Arberry "Face", Yusuf Ali "presence" [U]. Revealed about prayer direction; Tabari and Razi read wajh as direction or favour; Sufis read it ontologically [M].
- 2:156: inna lillahi wa-inna ilayhi raji'un, "we belong to God and to Him we return" [M].
- 7:172: God took offspring from Adam's loins and asked "Am I not your Lord?" (a-lastu bi-rabbikum), "Yes, we testify" [M]. Sufis (Junayd, Rumi) read it as the soul's origin; Zamakhshari read it figuratively, as natural disposition (fitra) [U].
- 17:7, last clause: "if you do good, you do good to your own selves (li-anfusikum), and if you do evil, fa-laha", literally "for it". Arberry has "to yourselves"; Yusuf Ali adds "against yourselves" [U]. Many commentators read the lam as "against".
- 23:99 to 100: the dying one says "send me back (irji'uni)"; the answer is "Never! behind them is a barrier (barzakh) until the day they are raised" [M gist].
- 57:3 to 4: the Outward and the Inward (az-zahir wal-batin); "He is with you wherever you are" [M]. Akbarians read it ontologically; Ash'ari and Salafi commentators as knowledge and power.
- 99:7 to 8: an atom's weight of good or evil will be seen [M]. Dharra is an ant or mote; "atom" is the translator's choice [U].
- Used by Akbarians, not in the essay: 28:88 ("everything perishes except His face"), 50:16 (nearer than the jugular vein), 4:1 (a single self) [M].
Use: keep 2:115 but add that classical commentary read "face" as direction.

I2. Hadith and numbering.
- Bukhari 6502 (Fath al-Bari numbering; Nawawi's Forty no. 38), the nawafil hadith: the servant draws near by obligations then voluntary acts until God loves him, and then "I am his hearing, his sight, his hand, his foot" [M]. Single chain, with one narrator questioned [U]. Sufis cite it for fana and baqa; Ibn Taymiyya read a union of will and rejected hulul [M].
- Bukhari 6011 and Muslim 2586 (al-Nu'man ibn Bashir): the believers in mutual love and mercy are like one body; when one limb aches the rest answer with sleeplessness and fever [M].
- Bukhari 13 and Muslim 45 (Nawawi no. 13): none of you believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself [M].
- Numbering: Bukhari is cited by Fath al-Bari number or by USC-MSA volume, book and number; Muslim by Abd al-Baqi number (2586, 45) or by book and hadith [U]. Name the edition or cite by chapter.
- Also: "the souls are conscripted armies", those who knew one another before are close (Bukhari 3336, Muslim 2638 [numbers U]). Pre-existence of souls in orthodox Islam, without rebirth.
Use: 6011 and 2586 are solid for the fingers image; say "believers".

I3. Ghazali, Mishkat al-Anwar, the veils, the book on unity.
Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058 to 1111). The Mishkat comments on the Light Verse (24:35) and on the report of seventy (or seventy thousand) veils of light and darkness. It sorts the veiled into three: pure darkness (deniers, naturalists), darkness mixed with light (senses, imagination, mistaken reason), pure light (a high but partial vision). The arrivers see that nothing exists but God: each thing has a face to itself, where it is nothing, and a face to its Lord, where it exists; he reads 28:88 so [M gist, U section]. The report: Muslim 179 has a veil of light (some wordings fire) with no number; the numbered versions have chains hadith critics call weak [U]. Authenticity: Gairdner and Watt ("A Forgery in al-Ghazali's Mishkat?", 1949) doubted the final section; Landolt and Treiger defend it [M dispute, U dates].
Also Ihya' Book 35, Tawhid and Tawakkul (Burrell, 2001): four levels of tawhid (the husk of the husk, the husk, the kernel, the kernel of the kernel, seeing only the One); trust follows from seeing all as from God [M].
Use: the Mishkat gives the veil three classes. The Tawhid book gives axiom then letting go in one author.

I4. Ibn Arabi.
Fusus al-Hikam (1229), first chapter (Adam): the Real willed to see the identities of His Beautiful Names, or, if you like, His own identity, in a total being holding the whole affair, which being qualified by existence would show Him His mystery. A thing does not see itself in itself as in another that serves as a mirror. The cosmos was an unpolished mirror; Adam, the perfect human, its polish and spirit [M gist, U wording].
"He/not He" (huwa la huwa) holds incomparability (tanzih) with similarity (tashbih) [M]; Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge (1989). Tarjuman al-Ashwaq (1214), ode 11 in Nicholson (1911, public domain): "laqad sara qalbi qabilan kulla surah" [M opening]: my heart is capable of every form, a pasture for gazelles, a convent for monks, a temple for idols, the pilgrim's Kaaba, the Torah's tablets, the Quran; I follow the religion of Love wherever its camels turn. His own commentary says the poems are mystical, not erotic [M].
Limits: he held Muhammad's religion complete, so "religion of Love" is not plain relativism (Chittick) [M]; wahdat al-wujud is not his own phrase [M, U].
Use: the Adam chapter is the best Abrahamic text for "a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view", better than "hidden treasure" because it is in his own book. Ode 11 is the best Muslim all-religions line.

I5. Mulla Sadra (1571/2 to 1640).
Al-Hikma al-muta'aliya fi l-asfar al-'aqliyya al-arba'a: being is one but graded; substantial motion (al-harakat al-jawhariyya); the soul is "corporeal in origin, spiritual in subsistence"; after death an imaginal body forms from its character and deeds (tajassum al-a'mal, tied to 99:7 to 8); transmigration into this world is rejected, transformation (maskh) in the afterlife allowed [M]. Four journeys: from creation to the Real; in the Real; from the Real to creation, by the Real; in creation, by the Real [M]. Rahman (1975), Rizvi (2009). Whether the perfected soul still develops after death [U].
Use: qualify "keeps developing" as "grows by its own character". The fourth journey, return into the world, parallels Part one's action after the door.

### Christianity

C1. Eckhart's sermons: numbers and sayings.
The eye saying (the eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me; one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love) is in "Qui audit me" (Sirach 24:30), Quint 12 [M sermon, U number]. Walshe and Colledge and McGinn mostly follow Quint but number a/b pairs and some sermons differently; Pfeiffer's 1857 numbering differs again [U].
"Rid me of God": Sermon 52, "Beati pauperes spiritu" [M]. The poor in spirit want nothing, know nothing, have nothing; Eckhart prays God to make him free of God (daz ich gotes quit werde [U]) because his essential being is above God as origin of creatures. "Rid me of God" is Blakney's 1941 wording; Walshe and McGinn say "free of God" [U]. God and Godhead as far apart as heaven and earth: usually Sermon 56 [U].
Letting go: gelazenheit (releasement) and abegescheidenheit (detachment). Sermon 86 argues that Martha, who acts, is the mature one [M].
Use: cite by opening words plus translator and page, not by number alone.

C2. Cologne 1326 and the bull of 1329.
Archbishop Henry II of Virneburg opened an inquiry at Cologne in 1326 on the complaint of two Dominicans, Hermann de Summo and William of Nidecken. Eckhart replied in writing, made a public protestation (early 1327), appealed to the pope, went to Avignon and died before 30 April 1328 [M]. John XXII issued In agro dominico on 27 March 1329: 28 propositions, 17 condemned as heretical, 11 as ill-sounding, rash and suspect [M; the 17/11 split U], adding that Eckhart had recanted whatever could be read heretically [M]. They include: the world created from eternity (1 to 2); God's glory shines equally in every work, evil included (4); we are changed into God as bread into Christ's body (about 16); God is neither good, better nor best (26); something in the soul is uncreated and uncreatable (27); God is one in every way (28) [M sayings, U numbers]. The eye line: I cannot place it among the 28 and believe it is not there [U]. Whether it was on the Cologne lists of 1326 I cannot confirm.
Use: cut "raised against him in Cologne in 1326", or say "was tried for heresy and, after his death, the pope condemned 28 propositions from his works". For a condemned saying beside the eye line, use the uncreated-something one.

C3. Gregory of Nyssa on epektasis.
Gregory (about 335 to about 395), from Philippians 3:13 (epekteinomenos, "stretching forward"): growth in God has no limit because God is infinite; each attainment opens a further desire, and the vision of God is never-ending desire (Life of Moses II.225 to 239 [U]). He also teaches final restoration (apokatastasis) and reads "God all in all" (1 Cor 15:28) as the end (On the Soul and the Resurrection) [M]. In On the Making of Man (ch. 16 [U]) the "man" in God's image is the whole of human nature as one. He rejects both Origen's pre-existence and transmigration [M]. Use: the essay's sentence is right; Gregory does not teach rebirth.

C4. Eriugena and 1225.
John Scottus Eriugena (about 800 to about 877), Periphyseon (about 864 to 866): nature in four: what creates and is not created (God as source), what is created and creates (primordial causes), what is created and does not create (temporal things), what neither creates nor is created (God as end, to which all returns). Exitus and reditus; creation as theophany; the end is God "all in all". Condemned in 1225, Honorius III ordering copies handed to the bishops for burning [M year; council U]. Moran (1989) reads a Christian Neoplatonist; pantheism is the traditional charge. Use: the Christian text nearest "a whole that goes out and returns".

C5. Teresa of Avila, the Interior Castle, seventh mansion.
El Castillo Interior (1577). The sixth mansion is the spiritual betrothal, the seventh the spiritual marriage. In 7.2 she says that in betrothal the two can still part but in marriage not, with images: rain falling into a river, a stream into the sea, and a room with two windows through which strong light enters in two places and becomes one light (7.2.4 [U]). The fruit is not ecstasy but works (7.4.6 [U]), loss of fear of death, forgetfulness of self. The fifth mansion's silkworm that dies to become a butterfly is her second-birth image [M]. Union is with the Trinity, by participation [M].
Use: two windows pairs with the essay's own; fruit as work pairs with Part one; add the silkworm to the "birth" paragraph.

C6. Catechism, 1030 to 1031 (and 1013).
1030: those who die in grace but imperfectly purified are assured of salvation, but undergo purification after death to reach the holiness needed for heaven. 1031: the Church calls this Purgatory, wholly different from the punishment of the damned, formulated at Florence and Trent; Tradition speaks of a cleansing fire (1 Cor 3:15). 1032: prayer for the dead [M]. 1013: death ends the earthly pilgrimage (Hebrews 9:27) and there is no reincarnation after death [M content, U wording]. Use: 1030 to 1031 support the essay; cite 1013 for "one life".

C7. Hymn of the Pearl (Acts of Thomas 108 to 113).
Sung by Thomas in prison in the third century Syriac Acts (also one Greek manuscript). A prince sent from the East loses his robe of glory, goes to Egypt for a pearl guarded by a serpent, wears local clothes, eats local food, forgets his origin and sleeps. A letter from his parents flies to him like an eagle and wakes him; he remembers, charms the serpent with his father's Name, takes the pearl and returns. The robe of glory comes to meet him and he sees in it himself, two yet one in form [M]. Scholars long called it Gnostic (Bousset, Jonas 1958); Klijn (1962) and others place it in Edessene Syriac Christianity and doubt the label [M]. The Theosophist G. R. S. Mead published it as "The Hymn of the Robe of Glory" (1908) [M]. Use: accurate in the essay; soften to "often called Gnostic".

C8. Origen, De Principiis (about 220 to 230).
Survives mostly in Rufinus's Latin (about 398). Rational beings were made equal and fell into bodies of varied kinds (I.4, II.8 to 9); there are ages before and after this world (II.3); all are restored and God is all in all (I.6, III.6) [M, locators U]. He rejected transmigration into animals (Commentary on Matthew XIII.1; Contra Celsum) [M, U]. The sixth century anathemas (543 or 553, disputed) name the "fabulous" pre-existence of souls and the restoration that follows; no clause on reincarnation as such [M]. Crouzel and Edwards deny a reincarnation reading; advocacy books (Head and Cranston 1977) assert it. Use: the essay's sentence is right.

C9. Other Christian inner voices ([M]).
Julian of Norwich: the soul's "substance" is made of God and never apart from Him (Long Text about chs. 53 to 56 [U]). The Cloud of Unknowing and Centering Prayer (Keating 1986): letting go of thoughts as the stated method. Athanasius, On the Incarnation 54, and Maximus: theosis; Palamas's essence-energies distinction is the Orthodox guardrail. Use: Julian and the Cloud are the best plain voices.

C10. Modern Christian comparers: what each equates.
- Bede Griffiths (1906 to 1993), Return to the Centre (1976), The Marriage of East and West (1982): sat-cit-ananda (being, consciousness, bliss) with Father, Word and Spirit; Advaita as a pointer to Christian communion.
- Abhishiktananda (Henri Le Saux, 1910 to 1973), Saccidananda (1974): the Upanishadic experience of the Self and "I and the Father are one" as one awakening; late letters say Christian language does not hold it (du Boulay 1998).
- Thomas Merton (1915 to 1968), Zen and the Birds of Appetite (1968): Zen "original face" with Christian "true self". His Louisville passage (Conjectures of a Guilty Bystander, 1966; 18 March 1958) speaks of loving everyone at once and release from an "illusory difference".
- Raimon Panikkar (1918 to 2010), The Intrareligious Dialogue (1978): no common essence; Brahman, Tao, God and nirvana are "homeomorphic equivalents", doing similar work in different systems.
- D. T. Suzuki, Mysticism: Christian and Buddhist (1957): Eckhart's Godhead and breakthrough beside sunyata and satori; the eye line as subject-object collapse [U which passage].
Tier [M] books, [U] specifics. Use: Panikkar for a specialist's caution, Griffiths for a convergent claim, Merton for the experiential line.

## 4. How they compare

The Traditionalists (Schuon, Guenon, Coomaraswamy): each religion has a necessarily different outer form and a shared inner core; differences come from audience and form, and outer claims are exclusive because a form must be. Schuon's Transcendent Unity of Religions (1948) is the model, with pairs such as salvation (public) and deliverance (inner). The cost: the Church condemned the Eckhart proposition he leans on.

The Hasidic and Sufi method is internal: one text read on two levels. Habad reads one creation from two perspectives; Ibn Arabi holds incomparability and similarity together; Ghazali sorts the veiled into classes. These are doctrines of perspective, not comparisons between religions.

Panikkar looks for homeomorphic equivalents and refuses a common essence; Griffiths and Abhishiktananda equate by structure; Merton compares experience first; Suzuki sets passages side by side. Differences are explained by language and culture, outer form against inner truth, the seeker's stage, fulfilment (the other path reaches what Christ reveals), or real doctrinal difference that experience may still cross.

## 5. What scholars say

Scholem read Hasidism as devekut (attachment), not union; Idel disputes this. On Habad, Elior's two-perspective reading is most cited. Ibn Taymiyya and the Salafi line reject the Akbarian reading; Chittick defends Ibn Arabi. The Mishkat's last section is disputed. McGinn places the mystics inside the Church's tradition while recording that it policed their language (Eriugena 1225, Porete 1310, Eckhart 1329). Katz (1978) argues that experience is shaped by tradition, so identity claims fail; Forman, Hick and Smith defend modest versions [M, not researched].
