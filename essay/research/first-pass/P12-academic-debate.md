## 1. The picture in brief

"Perennialism" in the scholarly argument means one of three claims, and most quarrels are about which one is meant. The first is about experience: mystics in every tradition have had the same experience and describe it in different words (Stace; in part William James; Forman in a narrowed form). The second is about doctrine: the inner teachings of the great traditions say the same things about reality, the self and the end of life (Schuon, Huston Smith, Huxley's anthology, Theosophy). The third is about origin: it all descends from one primordial source (the old prisca theologia, Guenon's primordial tradition, Blavatsky's Wisdom Religion).

Since 1978 the academic study of religion has leaned against the first claim. Katz argued that no experience is unmediated. Proudfoot and Sharf questioned "experience" as a category. Forman answered that an awareness with no content cannot be shaped by concepts it does not contain. The second claim was argued mostly in theology and philosophy: Hick said the traditions respond to one Real; Griffiths, Plantinga and D'Costa said that this is itself a doctrine few of the people it describes would accept. The third claim became intellectual history: Hanegraaff and Sedgwick study Theosophy and Traditionalism as modern movements with founders, quarrels and politics.

Above all of these sits a question of method: how to compare at all. Jonathan Z. Smith says comparison is the scholar's act, always made "with respect to" a chosen question, and must include difference. Defenders (Panikkar, Clooney, Schmidt-Leukel, Taves, Freiberger, Kripal, the family-resemblance tradition) propose ways to compare without flattening.

For Alex: his own claim (examples that fit, witnesses and not authorities, the same ideas in different words) is a modest form of the second claim and can be held as a family resemblance. The strong forms (same experience, one source, all traditions say the same) are the ones scholars contest hardest. And the two movements he leans on disagreed with each other, sharply, about rebirth (Finding 14).

## 2. Findings

### A. The experience debate

1. Stace, Zaehner, Smart: before Katz

Who: W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960). R. C. Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (Oxford, 1957). Ninian Smart, "Interpretation and Mystical Experience," Religious Studies 1 (1965). Locators unknown.

Argument (paraphrase): Stace held a universal core: an introvertive experience of undifferentiated unity, the same in every culture, with interpretation added afterwards. His marks of the core include unity, timelessness, a sense of reality, blessedness, holiness, paradox and ineffability. Zaehner, a Catholic scholar of Zoroastrianism and Hinduism, argued the reverse: monistic, nature and theistic mysticism are different things, and the theistic kind is higher. He disputed Huxley's mescaline claims. Smart took a middle road: doctrine feeds into how a state is experienced and described.

Against: Zaehner is the best early answer to Stace and Huxley: the goals differ, and one cannot average them.

Parallels cited: Stace uses Eckhart, Plotinus, the Upanishads, Sufi and Buddhist reports, via James and Underhill. Tier: [M].

Use: this is the vocabulary a knowledgeable reader brings: core versus interpretation. Alex's "same things in different words" is Stace's move if applied to experience, and safer applied to ideas.

2. Katz: the contextualist turn

Who: Steven T. Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism," in Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (London: Sheldon; New York: Oxford UP, 1978). Later volumes he edited: Mysticism and Religious Traditions (1983), Mysticism and Language (1992), Mysticism and Sacred Scripture (2000). Locators unknown.

Argument (paraphrase): The mystic's beliefs, training and expected goal shape the experience itself, and not only the report. A Christian seeking union with God, a Jew seeking devekut (cleaving to God) and a Buddhist seeking nirvana therefore have different experiences, so their words are not variant descriptions of one event. Mystical experience tends to confirm the tradition it grows in (its conservative character). Method: study each mystic in context and distrust any core.

Against: the premise (all experience is conceptually mediated) is a philosophical assumption, stated more than shown, and untestable, since every report arrives already described. Forman's answer is Finding 6.

Parallels cited: Jewish, Christian, Buddhist, Hindu; the devekut example is [U]. Tier: [M] for thesis and volumes.

Use: Katz is the name an informed critic will cite against any "mystics all agree" sentence. Write about texts and ideas; say "reports," not "the same experience."

3. Peter Moore: the in-between position

Who: Peter Moore, "Mystical Experience, Mystical Doctrine, Mystical Technique," in Katz (ed.), 1978. Also a survey article, "Recent Studies of Mysticism," Religion (1973) [U on year].

Argument (paraphrase, partly [U]): Moore separates experience, doctrine and technique, and holds that interpretation runs through the experience at several levels, so a clean core cannot be pulled out of a wrapper. Different techniques tend to produce different results, so some differences between traditions may trace to practice and not only to belief. He does not conclude that nothing is shared.

Against: a strict contextualist says even technique is cultural; a perennialist says he concedes too much.

Tier: [M] for the essay; [U] for the detail.

Use: source for the sentence "experience and interpretation are tangled."

4. Proudfoot: the protective strategy

Who: Wayne Proudfoot, Religious Experience (University of California Press, 1985). Locator unknown.

Argument (paraphrase): The subject identifies an experience through concepts and beliefs, so it cannot be a free-standing, self-authenticating datum. Schleiermacher, James, Otto and Eliade treated religious experience as autonomous and beyond explanation, and Proudfoot calls this a protective strategy that shields religion from criticism. He separates a descriptive reduction (redescribing an experience in terms the subject would reject, which he thinks a mistake) from an explanatory reduction (explaining it by causes, which he thinks legitimate).

Against: William Alston, Perceiving God (Cornell, 1991): a person can be justified in taking a religious experience as direct perception of God, as in ordinary perception, with no need to explain it away. [M]

Tier: [M] for book and terms.

Use: a reminder that "experience proves it" is not available to Alex. His essay already says it is not a proof.

5. Sharf: "experience" as a modern import

Who: Robert H. Sharf, "The Zen of Japanese Nationalism," History of Religions 33 (1993); "Buddhist Modernism and the Rhetoric of Meditative Experience," Numen 42 (1995); "Experience," in Mark C. Taylor (ed.), Critical Terms for Religious Studies (Chicago, 1998).

Argument (paraphrase): The idea that Buddhist, especially Zen, practice centres on a private inner experience was built in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries by Japanese modernizers (D. T. Suzuki, the Kyoto School) answering Western criticism, and by Western readers shaped by James. Premodern monastic life ran on ritual, doctrine and lineage. Reports of meditative states are checked by the tradition's authorities, and "experience" explains nothing.

Against: the Pali discourses and later manuals (Buddhaghosa) describe meditative states in detail, so "experience" talk is not wholly modern even if its packaging is. This is the standard rejoinder, reconstructed by me [U]. Agreeing in part: David McMahan, The Making of Buddhist Modernism (2008); Evan Thompson, Why I Am Not a Buddhist (2020). [M]

Tier: [M] for the articles and thesis; [U] for the rejoinder.

Use: if Alex cites Zen or Buddhist meditation, quote what the texts say, not what monks "experience."

6. Forman: pure consciousness events

Who: Robert K. C. Forman (ed.), The Problem of Pure Consciousness (Oxford UP, 1990); (ed.) The Innate Capacity (Oxford UP, 1998); Mysticism, Mind, Consciousness (SUNY, 1999).

Argument (paraphrase): Katz assumes every experience has content for concepts to shape. Mystics in many traditions report wakeful awareness with no thought, image or sense of self, which Forman calls a pure consciousness event (PCE). With no content there is nothing for tradition to mould, so the PCE can be the same across cultures while the interpretation differs. He calls the way it is known knowledge by identity, and distinguishes later dualistic and unitive mystical states, which tradition shapes more. I recall that he treats forgetting and letting go (Eckhart's Gelassenheit) as the mechanism that produces a PCE. That bears directly on Part one but is [U].

Against: Paul Griffiths, On Being Mindless (Open Court, 1986), and, I believe, an essay in Forman's own volume [U]: Indian Buddhist scholastics debated the attainment of cessation as a state where mind is absent, not as pure consciousness. Calling these one event reads a Yoga or Advaita category into Buddhism. Sharf adds that reports of a contentless state are retrospective and checked by tradition.

Parallels cited: Eckhart, Advaita, Yoga, Zen, Buddhist sources (exact texts: check). Tier: [M] for books and thesis; [U] for letting go.

Use: Forman is the best scholarly ally for a limited cross-cultural claim: one state, interpretation differs.

### B. Pluralism and the question of the Real

7. Hick: the pluralist hypothesis

Who: John Hick, An Interpretation of Religion (Yale UP, 1989; Gifford Lectures 1986 to 87). Earlier: God and the Universe of Faiths (1973); Death and Eternal Life (1976).

Argument (paraphrase): The great post-axial traditions are different human responses to one ultimate Reality, the Real, which in itself (an sich) lies beyond every human concept. Borrowing Kant's split between thing-in-itself and appearance, Hick says the Real is experienced through cultural filters, as persons (Yahweh, Allah, Vishnu, the Trinity) or as impersonal absolutes (Brahman, the Dharmakaya, the Tao). The test is soteriological: whether a tradition moves people from self-centredness to Reality-centredness, and by that test they look roughly alike. I believe Death and Eternal Life also proposed a series of lives in other worlds, a Western philosopher's version of rebirth [U].

Against: Plantinga, "Pluralism: A Defense of Religious Exclusivism" (1995): pluralism is itself an exclusive claim about those who disagree. D'Costa, "The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions," Religious Studies (1996): the theory is not neutral and does not match what the traditions say. A Buddhist version: nirvana is not a manifestation of a ground, so the Real is an import. And if nothing can be said of the Real, "it is one" is out of bounds. [M]

Tier: [M].

Use: Hick gives the philosophical form of what Alex does: one thing, many words. His critics name the price: you must say the traditions are partly mistaken about their own claims.

8. Griffiths: difference is doctrine

Who: Paul J. Griffiths, An Apology for Apologetics (Orbis, 1991); Problems of Religious Diversity (Blackwell, 2001).

Argument (paraphrase): Religious communities hold doctrines they treat as non-negotiable, and some contradict each other (a permanent self versus none; a creator versus none). The respectful response is to say so and argue, not to stack the traditions into a common core. Hick-style pluralism is one more doctrinal stance, so it is not above the fight.

Against: Hick and Schmidt-Leukel reply that the disputes concern models and metaphors, and the shared fact is the transformation produced.

Tier: [M].

Use: a ready counter to "you make traditions agree when they do not." Alex's essay already says where a specialist objects. Keep that habit.

9. Schmidt-Leukel: diversity as a fractal

Who: Perry Schmidt-Leukel, Transformation by Integration (SCM, 2009); Religious Pluralism and Interreligious Theology: The Gifford Lectures (Orbis, 2017).

Argument (paraphrase): The patterns of difference seen between religions recur inside each of them, a fractal structure. The contrast of a personal God and an impersonal absolute appears inside Christianity, Hinduism and Buddhism (for example Pure Land and Zen), so differences between traditions are the kind found within each. He argues the pluralist case inductively from this, with less reliance on Kant than Hick, and wants interreligious theology to change each tradition by integration. His examples are [U].

Against: the Griffiths objection: the pattern may be real while the doctrinal contradictions remain.

Tier: [M] for books and idea.

Use: fits Alex's practice of quoting inner layers; his point that the popular layer misses what the inner layer carries has a scholarly cousin here.

10. Ferrer: participatory pluralism

Who: Jorge N. Ferrer, Revisioning Transpersonal Theory (SUNY, 2002); with Jacob Sherman (eds.), The Participatory Turn (SUNY, 2008).

Argument (paraphrase): Perennialism, Wilber's included, assumes one pregiven spiritual ultimate that all traditions apprehend, with experience as a window on it. Ferrer proposes that spiritual worlds are co-created by human participation and a generative Mystery (drawing on Varela's enactivism). There may be several ultimates, and he rejects ranking traditions on one ladder, while avoiding relativism by judging paths by their fruits.

Against: Wilber and followers answer that Ferrer still presupposes a shared referent and a hidden hierarchy, and that "different ultimates" cannot be checked [U].

Tier: [M] for books and thesis; [U] for the rebuttal.

Use: the clearest voice from inside the spiritual camp that "same thing" may be too strong, and that a single ladder (as in the arrow from minus four to plus four) is contestable.

11. Loy: nonduality as a family

Who: David Loy, Nonduality: A Study in Comparative Philosophy (Yale UP, 1988).

Argument (paraphrase): Advaita Vedanta, Madhyamaka and Yogacara, Zen and Daoism all deny dualisms, but in several distinct senses (negating dualistic thinking, the non-plurality of the world, the non-difference of subject and object, the identity of appearance and Absolute). The family is real, and "they all say One" blurs it. "Not two" does not mean "one": Buddhist emptiness is not a Brahman. How many senses he lists: [U].

Against: classical Advaitins and Madhyamikas argued against each other for centuries (Shankara against the Buddhists; Candrakirti against a self), so a comparer who joins them takes a side.

Tier: [M].

Use: a model of comparing that names the differences; it backs Alex's caveat that Buddhist interdependence is not a One.

### C. History, esotericism, Traditionalism

12. Hanegraaff, Faivre, Hammer: esotericism as a historical field

Who: Wouter J. Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture (Brill, 1996); Esotericism and the Academy (Cambridge, 2012); Western Esotericism: A Guide for the Perplexed (2013). Antoine Faivre, Access to Western Esotericism (SUNY, 1994). Olav Hammer, Claiming Knowledge (Brill, 2001).

Argument (paraphrase): Hanegraaff treats esotericism as a scholarly category for currents (Hermetism, Neoplatonism, Christian Kabbalah, alchemy, Rosicrucianism, Theosophy, occultism) that churches and the Enlightenment pushed out as rejected knowledge. He criticises "religionism," scholarship that assumes a transhistorical inner truth (with the Eranos circle in view [U]). He sees Theosophy as turning Western esotericism toward Asia. Faivre defined esotericism by four marks (correspondences, living nature, imagination and mediations, transmutation) plus two relative ones, transmission and the practice of concordance, meaning the search for what traditions share. Concordance is thus on his list of what esotericists do. Hammer studies the rhetoric of appeals to ancient tradition, science and experience.

Against: Faivre's definition is called too narrow; Kocku von Stuckrad treats the field as a discourse about higher knowledge. [M]

Tier: [M] for books and gist.

Use: Part two's sentence that scholars treat Theosophy as an influential esoteric movement with a mixed reputation rests on this. Hammer's appeals-to-tradition point is the footnote for "the ancient traditions agree."

13. Sedgwick: Traditionalism as history

Who: Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World: Traditionalism and the Secret Intellectual History of the Twentieth Century (Oxford UP, 2004). Also Western Sufism (Oxford, 2016).

Argument (paraphrase): Traditionalism is a small, well-networked current from Rene Guenon through Schuon, Coomaraswamy, Evola and a wider circle (including Seyyed Hossein Nasr), with traces in comparative religion, art, architecture, Sufism and, via Evola, the radical right. Its core is a primordial tradition, decline through ages (the Hindu Kali Yuga), rejection of modernity, and the need for initiation inside one real tradition. Sedgwick is a historian: he tracks people, orders and publications and does not rule on the metaphysics.

Against: Traditionalist authors object that politics and Evola get too much space and that Guenon and Schuon wrote metaphysics [U on specifics]. Sedgwick's reply is that influence is a historical fact.

Tier: [M].

Use: naming Schuon borrows a figure of a movement hostile to modernity and to mix-and-match religion. The book also covers controversies around Schuon's later community; I could not check them and do not repeat them. Read Sedgwick before naming Schuon, Guenon or Evola in print.

14. The two movements disagree: Guenon against Theosophy, and rebirth

Who: Rene Guenon, Le Theosophisme: histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921); L'Erreur spirite (1923). A. K. Coomaraswamy, "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (1944).

Argument (paraphrase): Guenon wrote against Theosophy and Spiritism as counterfeits, and against the reincarnation of the individual through repeated earthly lives, which he called a modern Western error and not a doctrine of the Hindu texts. In his reading the traditional teaching concerns many states of being, and the human state is not repeated. Coomaraswamy argued that what passes on is the one Self, not a series of persons. I believe Schuon followed Guenon [U].

Against: Theosophists defended reincarnation as the Eastern doctrine.

Tier: [M] for titles and thesis; [U] for Schuon.

Use: Alex says the perennial philosophy and Theosophy are the two movements that compared religions. On lives they pull opposite ways, and the Traditionalists are no source for reincarnation. Say so, or lean on other witnesses (Plato, Buddhist texts, Hindu texts, gilgul), as the essay already does.

15. The orientalism and inclusivism critiques

Who: Richard King, Orientalism and Religion (Routledge, 1999). Paul Hacker on inclusivism (1983, German) [U]. Wilhelm Halbfass, India and Europe (SUNY, 1988). Edward Said, Orientalism (1978). W. C. Smith, The Meaning and End of Religion (1962). Alan Race, Christians and Religious Pluralism (1983).

Argument (paraphrase): King argues that the Mystic East and the idea of a universal mysticism were shaped by colonial-era Western writers and Indian reformers answering them (Schopenhauer, Max Mueller, Theosophy and Vivekananda are in the story). Hacker named the Hindu habit of absorbing other religions into one's own scheme inclusivism, and Halbfass shows Neo-Vedanta speaking in this key. Race's typology (exclusivist, inclusivist, pluralist) is the standard map. W. C. Smith argued that "religion" as a countable thing is a recent concept.

Against: a late history of a concept does not show its contents false. [M]

Tier: [M] for books; [U] for Hacker's dates.

Use: "all religions are one" is an old Hindu-modernist line with a name and a history. Alex's "witnesses, not authorities" avoids it.

### D. How to compare

16. Jonathan Z. Smith: comparison is an act

Who: J. Z. Smith, Imagining Religion (Chicago, 1982), especially the chapter "In Comparison a Magic Dwells"; Drudgery Divine (1990); Map Is Not Territory (1978); Relating Religion (2004).

Argument (paraphrase): Similarity is made by the comparer. A comparison has three parts (two things and a respect) and is never a plain "A is like B." Done honestly it states the respect, includes difference, and treats resemblance as a puzzle to explain. He described comparison as a disciplined exaggeration for the sake of knowledge (wording [U]). He criticised morphological comparison by essence (Eliade, Frazer) and the habit of making one's own religion unique. I think he sorted comparison into ethnographic, encyclopedic, morphological and evolutionary modes [U].

Against: Kimberley Patton and Benjamin Ray (eds.), A Magic Still Dwells (California, 2000), defend broader comparison; Robert Segal also defended the method. [M]

Tier: [M] for books and idea.

Use: one cheap discipline: say "both say X about Y" and name X. For example, both say the sense of a separate self is made and can end.

17. Family resemblance and polythetic classes

Who: Ludwig Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations (1953), sections 65 to 67. Applied to religion by Ninian Smart, William Alston, Benson Saler (Conceptualizing Religion, 1993) [U who exactly]. Rodney Needham on polythetic classification [U].

Argument (paraphrase): Games share no single feature, only overlapping similarities, as family members do. A class defined this way needs no essence, and no member needs every feature. For religions or mystical ideas, this allows "these traditions share a cluster of themes" without a shared core and without forcing every tradition to hold every theme.

Against: a polythetic class can be stretched to cover almost anything, which makes the claim thin. [M]

Tier: [M] for Wittgenstein; [U] for applications.

Use: for "same things in different words," "a family of ideas that recur" is the careful form. It fits the essay's own statement that rebirth is not unanimous.

18. Defenders who compare carefully

All [M] unless marked.
- Raimon Panikkar, The Intrareligious Dialogue (1978): diatopical hermeneutics (understanding across different places of thought) and homeomorphic equivalents (terms that play a similar role in different systems without being identical).
- Francis X. Clooney, Comparative Theology (2010): read a few texts of another tradition slowly and let them change your own reading.
- Ann Taves, Religious Experience Reconsidered (Princeton, 2009): break "religious experience" into building blocks and attributions, and compare what people deem special without assuming one thing underlies them.
- Oliver Freiberger, Considering Comparison (Oxford, 2019): make the comparative category explicit; aims differ, so methods differ [U on detail].
- Ninian Smart: compare along dimensions (doctrine, ritual, narrative, experience, ethics, institutions, material).
- Bernard McGinn, The Foundations of Mysticism (1991): define mysticism by consciousness of God's presence and the way of life that prepares for it, not by one experience.
- Huston Smith, "Is There a Perennial Philosophy?" (JAAR, 1987): the claim concerns doctrines about levels of reality, not experiences [U]. Wilber: shared deep structures, different surface structures (P10).


19. Kripal: comparison with reflexivity

Who: Jeffrey J. Kripal, Roads of Excess, Palaces of Wisdom (Chicago, 2001); Authors of the Impossible (2010); Comparing Religions (Wiley-Blackwell, 2014); The Flip (2019).

Argument (paraphrase): Kripal argues for comparison that admits the scholar's own position and takes the odd, erotic and paranormal seriously as sources of religious ideas. He is not a classical perennialist, since he sees cultures coding experience differently, but he treats the humanities as able to speak of a deeper consciousness (a filter or transmission model of mind in the line of Myers, James, Bergson, Huxley). In The Flip he argues consciousness is basic and materialism fails on its own terms.

Against: Russell McCutcheon, Critics Not Caretakers (SUNY, 2001): scholars must explain religion as human activity and not take its claims as true. Kali's Child (1995) drew sharp criticism from Hindu writers. [M]

Tier: [M].

Use: the contemporary scholar closest to the first view in Alex's opening (consciousness is not the brain's product). Most of the field does not share his stance.

## 4. How they compare: the method, with examples

Stace: collect reports (James, Underhill, Eckhart, the Upanishads), sort by features (unity, timelessness, objectivity, blessedness, holiness, paradox, ineffability), split each report into core and interpretation, and put differences in the interpretation.

Schuon, Huxley, Smith: compare doctrine, not experience; explain differences as form versus essence, exoteric versus esoteric, or levels of reality (P04 to P09).

Theosophy: treat the religions as exoteric offshoots of one older Wisdom Religion, decode symbols and myths, and explain differences by distortion, priesthood and adaptation to time and place. [M]

Hick: keep the Real beyond concepts, read traditions as filtered appearances, test by soteriological fruit. Katz: compare to find difference; each mystic belongs to a context. Forman: pick one narrow phenomenon, the contentless state, compare reports of it, and put differences in the interpretation after the event. Loy: compare arguments by structure and give each sense of the shared word its own name. J. Z. Smith: state the respect of comparison, include difference, admit the comparer's interest. Schmidt-Leukel: compare patterns of diversity across scales. Panikkar: look for functional equivalents and say what each frame adds or loses. Taves: break the compared thing into parts first.

How the comparers explain difference, in one list: form versus essence (Schuon); filter (Hick); level of reality (Huxley, Smith, Wilber); distortion of an original teaching (Blavatsky); real and part of the experience (Katz); different ultimates enacted (Ferrer); conflicting claims to argue about (Griffiths).

Rules most responsible defenders share: state the respect in which you compare; use the tradition's own term; report difference as well as match; quote inner or scholarly sources and not the popular version; do not let a similar word hide a different meaning (Smith, Loy, Griffiths).

## 5. What scholars say, short

There is no consensus, and a reader should not be told there is. My impression of the field since about 1980: strong experiential perennialism is a minority position, and the contextualist view that tradition shapes experience is the baseline, although strict contextualism has critics (Forman) and a quieter middle (Moore, McGinn, Smart). "Religion" and "mysticism" are treated as modern categories every comparer must handle. Theosophy and Traditionalism are studied as modern movements with histories and politics, not as bearers of ancient wisdom. A doctrinal perennialism (the inner teachings overlap) is argued by theologians and philosophers (Hick, Schmidt-Leukel, Huston Smith) and attacked by others (Griffiths, Plantinga, D'Costa). Comparison is accepted if openly stated, as discovery and not as proof.

## 6. Leads for Alex's essay

1. Use "overlap" and "family of ideas." Part two already says overlap is the point and that the traditions are not unanimous on lives. Avoid "the same experience" and "all traditions say."
2. Keep the claim about texts and doctrines, not about what mystics felt. The experience claim is what Katz, Proudfoot and Sharf contest, and Forman's defence covers one state.
3. Keep naming where a specialist objects. Griffiths, Loy, Hick's critics and Sharf supply the objections: self and no-self, Brahman and emptiness, a Real that cannot be named, "experience" as a modern word.
4. Guenon wrote against Theosophy and against reincarnation. If the essay shows the two movements as allies on lives, a specialist will object. Check the Traditionalist position (Guenon, Schuon) before writing a sentence about it.
5. The Traditionalists insist on one real tradition, initiation and no syncretism. A writer who is not religious and quotes many traditions is what Guenon opposed. Alex need not apologise for that, but should not claim Guenon's backing for his approach. James (1902), Underhill (1911) and Max Mueller are safer authorities for the project of comparison: public domain and cited by both camps.
6. Apply Smith's discipline in each comparison: "both say X about Y," with X named.
7. If a reader asks "isn't this orientalism?", King's point is that the "mystic East" is a Western construct. The essay already answers by quoting Western, Jewish and Muslim sources too, as witnesses.
8. A line to consider, in his own voice: scholars still argue whether mystics share one experience; I'm not claiming that, only that the ideas, as written, overlap.
9. Likely reader questions: isn't this cherry-picking (say how you chose: inner layers, close scripture); isn't it circular (the essay says it tests coherence, not truth); do the traditions mean the same by illusion (the essay warns the words differ); isn't Theosophy discredited (the essay borrows the comparison, not the Masters).
