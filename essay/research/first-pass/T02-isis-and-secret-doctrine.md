## 1. The picture in brief

Blavatsky wrote two big comparative books twelve years apart, and they do different jobs. Isis Unveiled (1877) is an attack and a claim. Materialist science and Church dogma have both lost a key, she says, and the key is an ancient Wisdom-Religion kept in Hermetic, Kabbalistic, Neoplatonic and Gnostic streams, with Brahmanism and Buddhism as older witnesses. Its examples are mostly Western, and on rebirth it rejects the Spiritist doctrine.

The Secret Doctrine (1888), subtitled The Synthesis of Science, Religion and Philosophy, is the system. A Proem states three fundamental propositions: one boundless Principle beyond thought; the periodic appearing and vanishing of universes; and the identity of all souls with a Universal Over-Soul, with an obligatory pilgrimage for every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Volume 1 (Cosmogenesis) gives a cosmogony as commentary on seven Stanzas, then a long part on symbolism. Volume 2 (Anthropogenesis) gives twelve more Stanzas on human origins and a part on the symbolism of the world religions.

Her explanation of agreement is descent: all religions come from one archaic teaching kept by initiates. Her explanation of difference is loss: symbols read literally, lost keys, priestcraft, adaptation to each age. Her authority is a text (the Book of Dzyan, in Senzar) and teachers (the Masters) that no outsider has checked. Coleman traced much of both books to nineteenth-century printed sources. Scholars still count her as historically important.

For Alex: the Proem is the nearest nineteenth-century statement of his axiom, offered as received teaching. Her Preface states a stance he can borrow, gathering what lies scattered, and her karma passage sits well beside his fingers. And he can show where he differs: his axiom is argued, not received, and he needs no lost original.

## 2. Findings

1. Isis Unveiled: the book and its veil

Claim: Two volumes, J. W. Bouton, New York, 1877. Full title: A Master-Key to the Mysteries of Ancient and Modern Science and Theology. Volume 1, Science, attacks materialist science, argues that the ancients knew nature and force better than moderns allow, and treats magic and Spiritualist phenomena. Volume 2, Theology, attacks Church dogma (Rome and the Jesuits above all) and argues that Christian doctrine and ritual were borrowed from older systems, with Gnostics, Kabbalists and Neoplatonists as the true heirs. The title takes up the old image of Isis whose veil no mortal lifts. Plutarch (On Isis and Osiris, about section 9) reports the inscription at Sais: she is all that was, is and will be, and nobody has lifted her robe.
Who: Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled, 1877; written in New York with Olcott's help.
Parallels: Egyptian (Hermes, the Mysteries), Greek (Pythagoras, Plato, Plotinus), Kabbalah (Zohar, a "Chaldean Book of Numbers"), Gnostic, Hindu, Buddhist.
Tier: [M]. Chapter titles, Plutarch's section number and the title-page mottoes are [U].
Use: "her first book, in two volumes, on science and on theology". It is Hermetic and Western in weight; the Sanskrit vocabulary came after her move to India in 1879. The veil of Isis is a Western precedent for Alex's veil, older than the Vedanta words.

2. Isis Unveiled: her statement of the comparing aim

Claim: In the Preface she calls the work a plea for recognising the Hermetic philosophy, the anciently universal Wisdom-Religion, as the key to the absolute in science and theology. Find-handle: "a plea for the recognition of the Hermetic philosophy". She presents herself as a gatherer of what others said and says the great teachers drew on one source ([U] on that last part).
Who: Isis Unveiled, Preface, vol. 1.
Tier: [M] (T01 recalls the same sentence, which is not evidence).
Use: "Wisdom-Religion" first appears here. She later uses Esoteric Philosophy, Secret Doctrine, Archaic Wisdom and Theosophy as near synonyms.

3. Isis Unveiled: souls and rebirth in 1877

Claim: In 1877 she rejected the Spiritist (Kardec) doctrine of reincarnation and allowed a return to earth only in exceptional cases. She read Pythagorean and Platonic metempsychosis as the soul's passage through states, not a return to animal bodies. By 1888 she taught the reincarnation of the individuality (the Ego) over many lives as the rule.
Who: Isis (1877); Secret Doctrine (1888); Chajes, Recycled Lives (OUP, 2019).
Tier: [M]. Reported by sibling P02 as [V], from results on Chajes and the Theosophy Forward article "Madame Blavatsky's Two Doctrines of Reincarnation".
Use: the Part 2 sentence that Isis and the Secret Doctrine were "one of the main routes" for English readers to karma and reincarnation is too wide for Isis. See lead 6.

4. The Secret Doctrine: structure

Claim: Two volumes, The Theosophical Publishing Company, London, late 1888. Motto: "There is no religion higher than truth", the motto of the Maharajas of Benares ([U] that the 1888 title page has it). Volume 1, Cosmogenesis: Preface, Introductory, the Proem, Part I Cosmic Evolution (seven Stanzas from the Book of Dzyan, each with commentary), Part II The Evolution of Symbolism (mundane egg, days and nights of Brahma, lotus, tree and serpent, the creative gods) and a part setting the teaching against science. Volume 2, Anthropogenesis: Part I twelve Stanzas on the origin of man (root races, Lemuria, Atlantis), Part II The Archaic Symbolism of the World-Religions (Genesis, the Fall, Adam Kadmon, the Kabbalistic scheme), and addenda. A third volume was made from her papers after her death under Annie Besant (1897 [U]).
Tier: [M]. How the science part divides between the volumes is [U].
Use: "a two-volume work, first on how the universe unfolds, then on how humans came to be, each with a long part on symbols from many religions".

5. The Preface: aim and stance

Claim: She states the aim: to show that Nature is no chance concurrence of atoms, to place man in the scheme of the universe, to rescue from degradation the archaic truths that are the basis of all religions, and to uncover, to some extent, the fundamental unity from which they all spring. Find-handle: "the fundamental unity from which they all spring". She disclaims the role of revealer. What she gives lies scattered through thousands of volumes of Asiatic and early European scripture, hidden under glyph and symbol, and she tries to gather "the oldest tenets" into "one harmonious and unbroken whole" (find-handles). She says the book is a partial statement of what she was taught by more advanced students, with a few details of her own. In Montaigne's phrase it is a "nosegay of culled flowers" with only the string her own (find-handle; whether in Preface or Introductory is [U]).
Who: SD vol. 1, Preface.
Tier: [M] on the gist; the find-handles are about 85 percent; no locator.
Use: the best short statement of the comparer's stance, and close to Alex's "I'm putting examples next to each other". The difference is the last clause. She says she was taught the string. He says the string is an axiom and an assumption.

6. The Introductory: one religion behind all, and an archive

Claim: The Introductory says the Secret Doctrine was the universally diffused religion of the ancient and prehistoric world. It adds that proofs, a complete chain of documents showing its presence in every land and the teaching of its adepts, exist to this day in the secret crypts of libraries belonging to the Occult Brotherhood. Find-handle: "the universally diffused religion of the ancient and prehistoric world". She links the teaching to Theosophia and the Alexandrian Eclectic Theosophical School of Ammonius Saccas (see T01 finding 15).
Tier: [M] (SD vol. 1, Introductory); find-handle about 80 percent; page unknown.
Use: the clearest "all religions come from one source" sentence in her pen, and the weak point, because the proof sits in an archive no outsider has seen. Alex's version needs no archive: the same ideas recur because they follow from the axiom, and the reader can check each text.

7. The Proem opens with a picture

Claim: An "Archaic Manuscript" of palm leaves lies before the writer. On its first page is a white disk on a black ground, Kosmos in eternity before energy stirs. On the second the same disk has a central point, the dawn of differentiation, the germ in the mundane egg. The circle is divine Unity from which all proceeds and to which all returns; the disk's plane is the Universal Soul.
Who: SD vol. 1, Proem, first page.
Tier: [M]; "from which all proceeds, whither all returns" is recalled, [U] as exact wording.
Use: a picture for Alex's axiom (one whole that differentiates and returns) that fits his squares. Check before use.

8. The three fundamental propositions

Claim: Under the words "the Secret Doctrine establishes three fundamental propositions" the Proem lists, in my paraphrase:
(a) An omnipresent, eternal, boundless and immutable Principle, on which speculation is impossible because it lies beyond human conception. She cites the Mandukya Upanishad for "unthinkable and unspeakable" (the Upanishad reference is [U]).
(b) The eternity of the Universe as a boundless plane, periodically "the playground of numberless Universes" (find-handle) that manifest and vanish, called manifesting stars and sparks of Eternity. Their appearing and vanishing is like the regular ebb and flow of a tide.
(c) The fundamental identity of all Souls with the Universal Over-Soul, itself an aspect of the Unknown Root, and the obligatory pilgrimage of every soul, a spark of the Over-Soul, through the "Cycle of Incarnation" (also called Necessity) under "Cyclic and Karmic law", for the whole term.
Who: SD vol. 1, Proem. The three occupy a few pages near the start; I recall pp. 14 to 17 in the 1888 pagination [U].
Parallels: Vedanta (Parabrahm, Mandukya), Hindu cosmic cycles, Emerson's Over-Soul.
Tier: [M], high on structure and key terms, medium on the order of clauses inside (c).
Use: see lead 1. Sentence-level evidence that a nineteenth-century comparer organised the traditions as one source, a cycle of appearing and vanishing, and a return of the many. Hers starts from an Absolute beyond consciousness, his from a whole that experiences.

9. Glosses on propositions one and two

Claim: For the first she uses Vedantic terms. The Absolute is Parabrahm, the field of Absolute Consciousness, out of all relation to conditioned existence. Once thought leaves it, duality enters: Spirit (Consciousness) and Matter, subject and object, which are not independent realities but two aspects of the Absolute. The Great Breath is precosmic ideation, source of force and individual consciousness. Precosmic root-substance is Mulaprakriti, the aspect that underlies all objective planes, which she presents as the veil of Parabrahm ("veil" wording [U]). Maya is her word for the manifested universe: all is temporary in it, from a firefly's life to the Sun's (gist [M]).
For the second she names the absolute universality of the law of periodicity: flux and reflux, day and night, waking and sleeping, life and death, which she says physical science already records. For the cosmos the periods are Manvantara (manifestation) and Pralaya (rest), the days and nights of Brahma, with Puranic figures drawn from Wilson's Vishnu Purana. Find-handle: "the law of periodicity".
Who: SD vol. 1, Proem; Part II, "The Days and Nights of Brahma".
Parallels: Advaita and Samkhya terms; the Kabbalistic En Soph; Hindu Puranas.
Tier: [M] for terms and roles; exact wording [U].
Use: this is where veil and illusion sit in her system. Two honesty points. Her Absolute has two aspects, Spirit and Matter, so it is not plain Advaita (her adviser T. Subba Row, a Madras Advaitin, is said to have disagreed with parts of her scheme [U]). And her Maya means "temporary and changing", which agrees with part-2-notes.md that maya and mithya mean "dependent" more than "illusion".

10. Gloss on proposition three: the pilgrimage, and why the one becomes many

Claim: The spark of the Over-Soul, the Monad (Atma-Buddhi), cannot have conscious independent existence until it has passed through every elemental form of the phenomenal world in that Manvantara and gained individuality, first by natural impulse and then by its own effort, checked by its karma. It rises through every degree of intelligence from mineral and plant to the highest archangel (she writes Dhyani-Buddha). The pivotal doctrine, she says, admits no privileges or special gifts in man, only what his own Ego wins by effort and merit across many metempsychoses and reincarnations. Find-handle: "no privileges or special gifts". In short: the One becomes many so that each spark can become self-conscious by its own work, then the many return.
Who: SD vol. 1, Proem; see also The Key to Theosophy (1889).
Parallels: Hindu atman and reincarnation; Pythagorean and Platonic metempsychosis; the "Cycle of Necessity", which she ties to Egyptian and Orphic teaching ([U] on the source; Herodotus 2.123 is one candidate).
Tier: [M]; clause order [U].
Use: it answers what Alex's notes call the unargued premise, why a whole would experience separation at all. Her answer is that self-consciousness is earned by passing through separation. He can offer it as one tradition's answer without adopting it. "No privileges, only what effort wins" also fits Part one's agency theme.

11. The Stanzas of Dzyan

Claim: Volume 1 is built on seven Stanzas, volume 2 on twelve, said to be translated from the Book of Dzyan, written in Senzar, a secret priestly tongue, and called the first volume of commentaries on the Seven Secret Folios of Kiu-te (read as Tibetan rgyud, tantra). "Dzyan" echoes Sanskrit dhyana. Each Stanza is a few verses of cosmic poetry followed by her commentary, which carries the parallels.
Who: SD vols. 1 and 2.
Tier: [M]. Scholars: no manuscript, no trace of Senzar, no outside witness. Critics read the Stanzas as her own composition from nineteenth-century sources (Coleman); Theosophists hold them translated.
Use: if Alex mentions the Stanzas, "she said she translated them" and "nobody else has seen the text".

12. Symbol as the shared language, and her equations

Claim: Both books treat symbols as the common language of the religions: egg, circle and point, triangle, cross, lotus, tree, serpent, number seven. Each is read at several levels (she speaks of seven keys; the list is [U]), and a myth that looks different in each culture is read through the key to show the same content. Her guide is analogy, "as above, so below" (find-handle "Ariadne's thread"; locator [U]). As I recall, with the gist [M] and each particular [U], her equations include:
The triad: Hindu Trimurti, the three upper Sephiroth (Kether, Chokmah, Binah), the Christian Trinity and the Egyptian triads (Osiris, Isis, Horus), one idea.
The Unmanifest: Parabrahm, the Kabbalistic En Soph, and the Rigveda hymn of creation, which she cites in English translation.
The seven: seven Rishis, seven Amshaspends of Zoroastrianism, seven archangels, seven lower Sephiroth, seven Dhyani-Buddhas.
The egg: the Orphic egg, the Egyptian egg of Kneph, Hiranyagarbha or Brahmanda.
The fall: the revolt of the angels, the Asuras, Prometheus, and Satan as Lucifer the light-bringer, whom she says the Church turned into the Devil.
Tier: [M] for the method; [U] for the particulars (SD Part II of each volume; Isis passim).
Use: she does not argue that the traditions agree; she reads them so that they must. Alex's computer picture is open about being a picture, which is the honest form of the same move.

14. Why the religions differ today

Claim: Five parts, the first being the master idea. (1) Every religion has an outer form for the many and an inner teaching for the few; disagreement lies in the outer form. (2) Symbols were read literally: allegory became history. (3) The keys were lost: the Mysteries closed, initiates withdrew, priestcraft kept the husk. (4) Adaptation: in each age a teacher restates the one teaching in terms that age can take; Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster and Jesus are read this way. (5) Cycles of decline: in the dark age (Kali Yuga) the inner teaching shrinks.
Tier: [M] for the pattern; locators unknown (SD Introductory and Part II of each volume; Isis vol. 2; Key to Theosophy).
Use: it parallels Alex's "public face against inner face". Care with (2) and (3): her examples attack Church Christianity and the Hebrew God as lesser beings. His position, that popular teaching often carries the idea in other words, is gentler and he should say so.

15. Karma as harmony

Claim: In the Secret Doctrine karma is the law that restores equilibrium. Recalled gist: the one decree of Karma is absolute harmony in the world of matter and of spirit, so it is not Karma that rewards or punishes but we who reward or punish ourselves, according to whether we work with nature or break her laws. Find-handle: "we who reward or punish ourselves". She names the law Karma-Nemesis in places, tying the Hindu word to the Greek. She links it to the unity of all life ([U] that she says harm to another is harm to oneself).
Who: SD vol. 1, near the end, around p. 643 of the 1888 edition [U].
Parallels: Hindu and Buddhist karma; Greek Nemesis.
Tier: [M] for the gist; locator [U]. T01 recalls the same passage with the same doubt.
Use: the nearest nineteenth-century line to "something I did to another part of myself". It has no fingers but it has no judge. Public domain, so it can be quoted in full once checked.

16. What not to borrow

Claim: Three parts of her scheme should stay out. Her Absolute is impersonal, and she treats the personal God of the Bible religions as a lower being or tribal power (Jehovah as a lunar or Sephirothic power, not the Absolute) [M, gist]. The root-race scheme of volume 2 ranks races by spiritual worth, and scholars trace its later use by racist occultists (Goodrick-Clarke 1985) [M]. And the Anthropogenesis makes claims about Lemuria, Atlantis and human descent that science does not support.
Use: Alex's rules are "do not claim a tradition says something it does not" and "do not say the Abrahamic religions fit badly". Her treatment breaks both. Cite only the Proem's structure and the Preface's stance, and add one honesty clause that the book contains material he does not endorse.

## 3. Cross-tradition parallels these people draw

These are hers, not my judgment. Where specialists keep terms apart I say so.

- One Absolute beyond thought | Hindu, Kabbalah | Parabrahm; En Soph (the Boundless); the "unthinkable and unspeakable" she ties to the Mandukya Upanishad. Parabrahm and En Soph are different concepts; she equates them.
- Spirit and Matter as aspects of one root | Samkhya, Vedanta | Purusha and Prakriti; Mulaprakriti as veil. Classical Samkhya is dualist and Advaita non-dual; she blends them.
- Universe appears and vanishes in cycles | Hindu | Manvantara and Pralaya; days and nights of Brahma; Vishnu Purana in Wilson's translation.
- A universal soul | Hindu, Tibetan Buddhist, Emerson, Greek | Paramatman, Alaya, Over-Soul, Anima Mundi. Yogacara alaya-vijnana is a storehouse consciousness, not a universal soul; the equation is hers.
- The soul's long journey | Egyptian, Greek, Hindu | the "Cycle of Necessity", metempsychosis, samsara; Plato's Phaedo and Republic Book 10.
- The world as veil | Hindu, Egyptian | Maya; the veil of Isis.
- A first manifesting triad | Hindu, Kabbalah, Christian, Egyptian | Trimurti; Kether, Chokmah, Binah; Father, Son and Spirit; Osiris, Isis, Horus.
- Seven hierarchies | Hindu, Persian, Hebrew, Christian | Rishis; Amshaspends; archangels; Sephiroth.
- Karma as harmony | Hindu, Buddhist, Greek | karma, kamma, Nemesis.
- An inner doctrine behind each outer religion | Buddhist, Hermetic, Christian | "Heart Doctrine" against "Eye Doctrine" (from her Voice of the Silence, T01 finding 16); the Mysteries.

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

Six moves, each with an example.

First, line up by function: the same role side by side across cultures, likeness taken as evidence. Example: Trimurti, the upper Sephiroth, the Trinity, the Egyptian triads.

Second, concordance by term: a word in one language glossed by a word from another. Example: Parabrahm glossed as the Absolute and then as the Kabbalistic Boundless.

Third, symbol as key: one figure read at several levels, the deeper level giving the common content. Example: the mundane egg.

Fourth, genealogy: a hidden source behind everything, so that likeness proves descent. Example: the Introductory's universal religion and its archive.

Fifth, outer and inner: apparent disagreement is put in the outer form, and the inner teaching is said to agree. Example: Genesis read through the Kabbalah in volume 2.

Sixth, analogy as law: "as above, so below" carries a pattern from cosmos to soul and back. Example: cycles of the universe and cycles of incarnation.

The method cannot fail: agreement confirms her and disagreement is corruption. It reads Indian and Buddhist terms through a Western occult frame and needs the hidden source to hold the picture together. Defenders call it a bold act of respect for Asian traditions in an age of missionary scorn. Alex can keep her openness about gathering and the table of one idea in many vocabularies, and drop the genealogy and the step that cannot fail.

## 5. What scholars say

William Emmette Coleman, "The Sources of Madame Blavatsky's Writings", written in the early 1890s and printed as an appendix to Solovyoff's A Modern Priestess of Isis (English, 1895): he argued that Isis took roughly two thousand passages from about a hundred books, and that the Secret Doctrine leaned on Wilson's Vishnu Purana, Winchell, Donnelly, Jacolliot and Sinnett, often at second hand [M; numbers U]. Later scholars accept heavy borrowing and argue about what it shows: fraud, or a collage of the period that she partly described herself as a nosegay. De Zirkoff's source notes in the Collected Writings, by a Theosophist, are said to confirm the dependence [U].

Wouter Hanegraaff (New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996; Esotericism and the Academy, 2012) places Theosophy as a main channel from nineteenth-century occultism and Spiritualism into the New Age, a response to disenchantment, and sees the Wisdom-Religion as a late form of the older prisca theologia [M on framing; page claims U]. Joscelyn Godwin (The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994) shows earlier European roots in Masonic, Rosicrucian and Hermetic circles, so her sources are less original than she claims [M]. Olav Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, 2001) analyses how she builds authority from ancient tradition (the Stanzas, the Masters), science and experience [M]. Bruce Campbell (1980) is the standard history. Julie Chajes (2019) shows the change in her doctrine of rebirth between 1877 and 1888 [reported by sibling P02 as V]. Meade (1980), Washington (1993) and Lachman (2012) are the sceptical, the witty and the balanced biographers; Cranston (1993) is the insider.

Outside the field: Max Muller said in 1893 that there is no "esoteric Buddhism" and attacked the movement as unhistorical [reported by sibling P01 as V]. Guenon's Le Theosophisme (1921) calls it a counterfeit while sharing its idea of one central tradition [reported by sibling P02 as V]. Donald Lopez (Prisoners of Shangri-La, 1998) is the usual reference on the Tibet myth Theosophy helped to build [M]. Nobody outside the Society has found the Book of Dzyan or the Senzar language.
