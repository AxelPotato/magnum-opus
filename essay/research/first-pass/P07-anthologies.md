## 1. The picture in brief

An anthology of the perennial philosophy does one thing. It takes a short passage from a scripture or a mystic, files it under a theme, and files a passage from another tradition under the same heading. The reader does the equating by reading the passages in a row, and the anthologist's commentary says what the row shows. The device is strong, and its weakness is built in: the headings are the anthologist's, the excerpts are cut from their context, and the translations often carry the equation already.

The books differ in how much they claim. Huxley (1945) is a popular, commentary-heavy anthology of mostly Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist and Sufi mystics under about two dozen themes. Perry (1971) is the Traditionalist answer: over a thousand pages, a stricter frame (outer forms differ, inner truth is one), and a hostile stance toward Theosophy and Western-style reincarnation. Huston Smith supplies the map the anthologies fill in: four levels of reality, four of selfhood. Wilson (1991) and Novak (1994) are interfaith projects with less metaphysical commitment. Wilber and Campbell compare structures. Hastings's encyclopedia and Max Muller's Sacred Books of the East are not perennialist, and are the right tools for checking a quotation.

Three points matter for Alex. The movement is not one voice, and its founders reject the Theosophical reincarnation his Part two leans on. The Traditionalist rule that outer layers differ while the inner layer agrees is his own policy, and he can credit it. Scholars object strongly to the anthology method, and one honest sentence would protect the page.

### B. Perry and the Traditionalist frame

5. Perry's Treasury.
Claim: Whitall N. Perry's A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom is a very large thematic anthology (well over a thousand pages), 1971 (Simon and Schuster, New York; Perennial Books, London), Foreword by Huston Smith, with Perry's commentary between blocks of quotations. Sources I recall: Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist, Islamic, Christian, Jewish, Greek, Native American, probably Zoroastrian and Egyptian. A later reissue came from World Wisdom.
Who: Perry 1971. P04 and P06 give the same description independently.
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for scale, date, publishers and Smith's Foreword; [U] for part and chapter headings. I recall a movement from the Principle through manifestation and man to the return, unchecked.
Use for the essay: the best themed quotation source inside the movement, stricter than Huxley because it draws on traditional authorities. Open the contents first.

6. The rule for differences: outer and inner.
Claim: each religion has an outer layer (law, ritual, dogma, story) and an inner layer (metaphysics and way). Outer layers differ and may contradict each other. Inner layers agree. Schuon's image: radii of a circle, far apart at the rim and meeting at the centre. He also argued that the formal contradictions are not accidents, since each form must be exclusive to keep its integrity.
Who: Frithjof Schuon, De l'unite transcendante des religions (Gallimard, 1948); The Transcendent Unity of Religions (English 1953; revised 1975 with a Foreword by Smith, per P04); Esoterism as Principle and as Way.
The words: paraphrase. T. S. Eliot praised the book (P04 says "praised by Eliot" is safe in gist).
Tier: [M].
Use for the essay: this is Alex's rule ("the popular understanding often does not carry the idea, but the inner teaching does") in source form. Credit it in one sentence, and say it is a theory about religions that some scholars reject.

7. Hostility to reincarnation and Theosophy.
Claim: the Traditionalist core, from Guenon on, rejects reincarnation in the Western or Theosophical sense (the same person returning to improve). It teaches that Hindu and Buddhist transmigration concerns states of being or a psychic residue, and that a human individuality is not repeated. Guenon wrote against the Theosophists and the spiritists (Le Theosophisme, 1921; L'erreur spirite, 1923). Perry belongs to this school, so I expect his Treasury to follow it on rebirth, but I could not confirm his actual text.
Who: Guenon 1921, 1923. P02 and P05 report the school's position (P02 marks the Guenon items [V]; I did not check them myself).
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for the school's position; [U] for Perry's own treatment.
Use for the essay: the most useful fact in this file. The essay calls the perennial philosophy and Theosophy the two movements that compared religions systematically. True, but they disliked each other, and the Traditionalists reject the reincarnation the essay needs. One sentence belongs in the paragraph: lifetimes come from India, Plato and the Theosophists, and the perennial school's founders kept survival and states of being but not a return to this world.

8. Coomaraswamy's one transmigrant.
Claim: Ananda Coomaraswamy argues that Indian teaching knows a single transmigrant, the Self that passes through all beings, and not the separate individual. This is closer to Alex's axiom than anything in Huxley.
Who: "On the One and Only Transmigrant", Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3, 1944 (I think bound with "Recollection, Indian and Platonic"). P02, P03 and P05 found a catalogue record, which confirms the title only.
The words: paraphrase. As I recall he leans on the Brihadaranyaka line that there is no other seer than the Self.
Traditions: Vedanta; Buddhism (he reads no-self as denying that the empirical person is the Self); Plato.
Tier: [M] for title and thesis; [U] for the year and binding.
Use for the essay: it answers "if there is one mind, who carries over?" from inside the movement. The comparer equates here: Theravada scholars deny that the Buddha taught a Self.

### C. Huston Smith

9. Forgotten Truth.
Claim: Smith's Forgotten Truth (Harper and Row, 1976; later The Common Vision of the World's Religions) sets out a shared map: four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite) and four of selfhood (body, mind, soul, spirit), with consciousness mapped on waking, dreaming, deep sleep and the fourth state (turiya). It leans on Schuon. Following him, it argues that an Infinite without the possibility of expressing itself as finite would not be infinite, so maya is the Infinite's own self-limitation. Guenon's Universal Possibility (P02) is the same argument.
Who: Smith 1976; chapter order and wording [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: Hindu states of consciousness; Christian body, soul, spirit; Platonic chain of being; Islamic and Native American cosmologies; Advaita, Sufi self-disclosure, Kabbalistic tzimtzum, Plotinian procession for the argument.
Tier: [M] for the levels; [U] for the argument's wording in Smith.
Use for the essay: a readable staged map beside the arrow, and a philosophical answer to "why would the whole do this?" that the Safed, Plotinus and lila paragraph lacks: the whole cannot exclude any possibility, and separation is one. It is a metaphysical argument and not evidence.

### E. Structural comparers

13. Ken Wilber.
Claim: three kinds of work. Anthologies he edited: Quantum Questions (Shambhala, 1984), mystical writings of Heisenberg, Schrodinger, Einstein, Planck, Eddington, Jeans, de Broglie and Pauli; and The Holographic Paradigm (1982). A stage map: The Atman Project (1980), Up from Eden (1981), Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995), with stages up the Great Chain toward non-dual unity and substitute goals on the way. A method: in Eye to Eye (1983) valid knowledge in any field has an injunction (do this), an apprehension (see what comes) and a confirmation (compare with others who did it); later, Integral Spirituality (2006) splits a religion's translative function (meaning at your stage) from its transformative one (moving you on).
Who: the books named; contents [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for titles and gist.
Use for the essay: Quantum Questions is where many readers met Schrodinger as a mystic, so Alex's Schrodinger paragraph can point to it. The injunction scheme matches "an experiment you can run on yourself". The translative and transformative split explains why a religion's public face and inner teaching differ. Critics call Wilber's stages loose science and say he assumes a perennialist metaphysics.

14. Joseph Campbell.
Claim: Campbell compares stories. The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949) finds one shape in hero myths: departure, initiation, return. He often cited the Rigveda sentence that the real is one and the sages name it variously.
Who: Campbell 1949; Masks of God (1959 to 1968); Portable Jung 1971. Where he quotes Rigveda 1.164.46: [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for titles and gist.
Use for the essay: return as a shape stories keep supports the third clause only as a pattern, since stories do not prove metaphysics. Jung, in his commentary on the Tibetan Book of the Dead, warned against transplanting Eastern ideas.

## 4. How they compare: the method

Six moves recur.

1. Theme first. The anthologist picks a heading (the One, charity, silence) and collects passages by what they say, not where they come from. Huxley's chapter 1 puts a Chandogya line, an Eckhart sermon and a Taoist passage together and lets the commentary say they match. Each excerpt was chosen for fit, so the agreement looks strong.

2. A translation key. One vocabulary becomes the key and the others are translated into it: Huxley's Ground and Godhead, Smith's four levels, Perry's Traditionalist set (Principle, manifestation, Intellect). The equation is made at the translation stage, before the reader sees it.

3. Witnesses and experience. Huxley, Stace and Underhill treat mystics as reporters and ask whether the reports agree. This is closest to Alex's "witnesses and not authorities". It needs a further step, showing the reports are of the same thing, which Zaehner and Katz dispute.

4. Outer and inner layers. Perry, Smith and Schuon sort every religion into exoteric and esoteric, so disagreement at the surface does not count against agreement at the centre. Powerful and hard to test: a text that disagrees can always be assigned to the outer layer.

5. Structure over content. Campbell and Wilber compare shapes (departure, initiation, return; stages from body to non-dual unity). The risk is that a shape fits almost anything.

6. Many hands. Wilson, Novak, Hastings and Muller let specialists speak for their own tradition and give agreement and disagreement equal room.

How the movement explains differences: temperament and path (Huxley, Ramakrishna); idolatry, the hardening of a symbol (Huxley); the necessary exclusivity of each form (Schuon); local costume over an elementary idea (Campbell); different stages of development (Wilber).

A worked example in the anthologists' manner, for the essay's karma step. Heading: the other is oneself. Passages: Isha Upanishad 6 and 7, Gita 6.29, Leviticus 19:18, Matthew 22:39, Shantideva on exchanging self and other, the hadith of the one body. Commentary: the ethic follows from the metaphysic. What the row hides is that Leviticus and Matthew ground love in a shared status under God, Shantideva in the lack of a fixed self, the Isha in identity. The essay already says this ("Most of these speak first of a community"), which is good practice.

## 5. What scholars say

Short, from memory, [M].

Steven Katz ("Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", in Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis, 1978) argues that no mystical experience is unmediated: a tradition's concepts shape what is experienced, so cross-tradition agreement is less than it looks. Robert Forman (The Problem of Pure Consciousness, 1990) replied that some experiences are contentless. The debate is unsettled. Zaehner argued against Huxley that mystical types differ.

Stephen Prothero (God Is Not One, 2010) is the best-known popular case against "all religions are the same": they ask different questions and offer different cures. Jonathan Z. Smith wrote that comparison always involves choosing what to compare and why, and that the resemblance is made by the comparer. Richard King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) argues that the Western image of a mystical East was partly made by anthologists of this kind. Wouter Hanegraaff (1996) and Mark Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) give the historians' account: a modern esoteric current with its own rivalries, and Traditionalism as a distinct, often anti-modern school.

Textual scholars add a quieter charge: the anthology removes a passage from its argument. A line of Eckhart read alone sounds like Advaita and, read in its Dominican sermon, belongs to a Christian theology of the soul.

In fairness, the movement's best authors (Coomaraswamy, Schuon, Smith, Shah-Kazemi) know the specialist objections and answer them, usually by appeal to the inner and outer layers. An anthology has no room for that argument.
