# 1. The picture in brief

Schuon's metaphysics is the most developed statement of Alex's axiom that I know in the perennial movement. It is an argument and not an anthology. Huxley collects sentences; Schuon builds a ladder of reality and places every tradition's words on a rung.

The core is a pair. The Absolute is what alone is real and depends on nothing. Because nothing outside it can limit it, it is also the Infinite, and the Infinite holds every possibility, including the possibility of something that looks like not-the-Absolute. That something is Maya, the world of appearances: real in its own order, unreal when taken as separate from the Reality it shows. Atma, the Self, is the Absolute seen from inside a person. The ego is a refraction of it that takes itself for a centre. The "fall" is that forgetting; the "return" is remembrance and deliverance, which is identity and not arrival. The faculty that sees this is the Intellect, seated in the Heart.

He is a strong witness for the illusion that feels real, for why separation exists at all, for its end, and for an ethic that follows from oneness. He is a poor witness for lifetimes: he and his school reject reincarnation as the West understands it, and they fought Theosophy by name. His cosmos also declines while individuals climb. Alex can lean on him for the three clauses of the axiom and the ethic, and must be careful about lifetimes and the word "arrow".

Spiritual Perspectives and Human Facts, Light on the Ancient Worlds, To Have a Center (1990), The Play of Masks (1992). The Essential Frithjof Schuon, ed. Seyyed Hossein Nasr (1986), is a reader. Chapter titles and locators unknown.
Tier: [M] for titles, [U] for exact years of the minor books.
Use for the essay: if Alex checks one book, Survey is the shortest complete statement. The Play of Masks has the essay's picture in its title (programs as masks of one actor).

2. The Absolute and the Infinite.
Claim: Divine Reality has two inseparable aspects. The Absolute cannot not be and depends on nothing. The Infinite cannot be limited, so it holds all possibility and tends to give itself. A third term, Perfection or Goodness, names the quality of that overflow.
Who: Logic and Transcendence, Esoterism as Principle and as Way, Survey [M for the books; locators unknown].
The words: paraphrase. The argument as I remember it has four steps. The Absolute has nothing outside it to limit it. So it is Infinite. The Infinite includes the possibility of a relative, a reflection and not a rival. That possibility is realised, and the realisation is Maya. The world is therefore not a mistake in the Absolute and not an addition to it; it is what infinity includes. He maps the pair onto Hindu terms, Atma for the Absolute and Maya for the Infinite as it shows up in the relative, and uses Sat, Chit and Ananda (being, consciousness, bliss) for what Atma contains [U on how he divides them].
Traditions cited: Hindu (Atma and Maya, Brahman and Shakti), Platonic and Neoplatonic (the Good that gives itself), Islamic (the infinite mercy, the Names), Christian (the Trinity read as the same aspects [U]).
Tier: [M] for the argument, [U] for the Sat-Chit-Ananda mapping and the Trinity link.
Use for the essay: the cleanest answer to why separation at all: a limitless whole has to include the possibility of looking limited. It matches the machine, where one computer can run any number of isolated programs.

3. Three degrees: Beyond-Being, Being, Existence.
Claim: The Divine itself has levels. The Essence is Beyond-Being (no qualities, no person). Being is the same Reality as personal God and Creator, with the Names. Existence is the manifested world.
Who: Schuon in several books, with Beyond-Being chosen in place of Guenon's Non-Being [M; P04 agrees].
The words: paraphrase. The point is to stop two camps quarrelling. The Advaitin who says there is only the impersonal Self and the monotheist who says there is a Lord are, in his reading, speaking of different degrees of one Reality. His usual pairs are Nirguna and Saguna Brahman, Dhat and Allah the Lord, the Godhead and God, Ein Sof and the sefirot.
Traditions cited: Hindu, Sufi, Eckhart, Kabbalah, Plato (the Good beyond being, Republic 509b) [M].
Tier: [M] for the scheme, [U] for which pairs appear in which book.
Use for the essay: it explains "sometimes different gods" without saying the gods are the same. Write that one comparer reads the personal God and the impersonal Absolute as two degrees of one thing, and that Buddhists and many theologians would not accept the equation.

4. Maya: neither real nor unreal, a veil and a mirror.
Claim: The world of forms is real relative to the beings in it and unreal relative to the Absolute. It veils the Principle and it reveals it, since every form is a symbol.
Who: Language of the Self (the Atma and Maya essays are there, I believe [U]); Survey; The Play of Masks (title [M]).
The words: paraphrase. The key idea is degrees of reality: nothing is simply real or simply illusory. A dream is illusion relative to waking and reality relative to the dreamer. Maya is neither being nor non-being, which he links to the Sufi formula that the world is He and not He. Lila, the play, is the joyful side of the same doctrine. Illusion is not a lie told by God; it is the taking of the part for the whole.
Traditions cited: Hindu (maya, lila), Sufi (He and not He, tied to Ibn Arabi), Platonic (the cave), Buddhist (samsara, emptiness [U as his pairing]), Christian (Eckhart on creatures having no being of their own [U as his use]).
Tier: [M] for the logic and the Sufi link, [U] for the Buddhist and Eckhart pairings.
Use for the essay: the source for "the wall is real inside the system". Alex's paragraph on what illusion means already follows this logic and can credit the comparer.

5. Atma and the ego: Self and self.
Claim: The deepest subject in each person is Atma, the one Self. The ego is a reflection that has forgotten it is one.
Who: Language of the Self, Esoterism as Principle and as Way [M]; the same pairing in Coomaraswamy (P03 reports).
The words: paraphrase. Atma is the Subject that can never become an object. The individual "I" is a ray that thinks it is a source. Deliverance is therefore not the ego growing, it is finding that the ego was never the owner. The ego is to be transcended and not crushed. Pride is the ego claiming to be an absolute.
Traditions cited: Hindu (Atman, jiva, ahamkara), Sufi (the saying that whoever knows himself knows his Lord, which hadith critics do not accept as Prophetic [M on status; U on his use]), Christian (Eckhart's ground of the soul), Buddhist (anatta read as denying only the ego; the comparer's reading).
Tier: [M] gist, [U] on the Sufi saying as his.
Use for the essay: it fits "my private memory is the self I feel from inside". The processor, not the process, is the Self.

6. The Heart-Intellect.
Claim: There is a faculty that knows the Real directly. He calls it the Intellect, holds that at its summit it is uncreated, and seats it in the Heart. Reason is its shadow.
Who: Eye of the Heart (French 1950, English collection 1997), Logic and Transcendence, Esoterism as Principle and as Way [M]; P04 reports the same.
The words: paraphrase. Intellection is the immediate seeing of an evident truth, as a mathematician sees an axiom. Metaphysical principles are axiomatic in that sense and not conclusions of logic. He sets it against reason, which works step by step, and against visions or psychic experience, which he distrusts as proof. The Heart is its bodily symbol and spiritual centre, where in the Hindu image the Self dwells in a small cave.
Traditions cited: Greek (nous), Scholastic (intellectus), Eckhart (something uncreated in the soul, which the 1329 papal bull listed among condemned propositions [M]), Sufi (qalb, ruh, aql), Hindu (buddhi, hridaya), Orthodox hesychasm (the mind in the heart), the Gospels (the pure in heart will see God) [M for parallels; U for which he cites where].
Tier: [M] doctrine, [U] his own citations.
Use for the essay: a principled reading of "an axiom, not a proof" and "check it in yourself". Caution: for Schuon metaphysical certainty is immediate, which is more than Alex claims. Alex's modest posture should stay.

7. The five divine presences.
Claim: Reality is arranged as five presences from the Essence down to the sensory world, and man, a microcosm, contains all five.
Who: Sufi doctrine (Ibn Arabi's school, systematised by Qunawi and later writers [M]). Schuon uses it in Esoterism as Principle and as Way and nearby books, as I recall [U].
The words: paraphrase. One standard list: the Essence (Hahut), divine Being and Names (Lahut), the angelic or spiritual world (Jabarut), the subtle or psychic world (Malakut), the corporeal world (Nasut) [M as one standard list; U that Schuon follows this exact one]. The point is that "God, soul, body" is too short a ladder.
Traditions cited: Sufi; Hindu (the four states in the Mandukya Upanishad: waking, dream, deep sleep, the Fourth); Kabbalah (the four worlds, Atzilut, Beriah, Yetzirah, Assiah) [the last two M as standard parallels, U as his].
Tier: [M] for origin and outline, [U] for his list.
Use for the essay: a better ladder than Huxley's three parts; Huston Smith simplified the same grid into four levels of reality in Forgotten Truth [M]. Caution: it is a ladder of worlds and not of personal stages, so it does not map onto the arrow from minus four to plus four.

8. The fall as separation, and the return.
Claim: The "fall" has two senses: the cosmic going-out of Maya, which is the world, and the human loss of the Centre, which is forgetfulness of the Self. The return is remembrance and interiorisation.
Who: Light on the Ancient Worlds, Esoterism as Principle and as Way, Form and Substance [U on which book carries which point].
The words: paraphrase. Adam's expulsion is, in this reading, an inward event as well as a story: man leaves the central state in which the Intellect is open. It follows from finding 2. The return runs backward from the periphery to the Centre; the method is in P04 finding 15. He also accepts that ages decline from a golden age, and calls ours the Kali Yuga [M].
Traditions cited: Genesis 3, the Yugas, Hesiod's ages, Plato (the soul shedding its wings in the Phaedrus; anamnesis), Neoplatonic procession and return, Sufi (the primordial covenant, Quran 7:172; "to Him we return", Quran 2:156) [M that the texts exist; U that he cites each].
Tier: [M] two senses, [U] locations and individual citations.
Use for the essay: the two senses separate why the whole separates from why I forget. Alex's second and third clauses map onto them.

9. Deliverance, and salvation versus deliverance.
Claim: Schuon separates exoteric salvation (saved from damnation, admitted to Paradise) from esoteric deliverance (the end of the illusion of separation, which is identity with the Self). The goals the traditions name are one event.
Who: Transcendent Unity, Esoterism as Principle and as Way, Survey [M; locators unknown].
The words: paraphrase. Moksha is not travelling to a place; the Self never left, and deliverance removes the veil. The model is the person delivered in life (jivanmukta). He pairs it with Sufi fana (passing away of the self) and baqa (abiding in God), and with deification in the Christian East.
Traditions cited: Hindu (moksha, "that thou art"), Islamic (fana, baqa), Christian (theosis, Eckhart's birth of God in the soul), Buddhist (nirvana as the Unconditioned [U as his pairing]), Taoist (return to the root [U]).
Tier: [M] for the exoteric/esoteric line and the Hindu, Sufi, Christian pairs; [U] for the Buddhist and Taoist pairs.
Use for the essay: the third clause of the axiom in the traditions' own words, and Alex's public and inner faces: Paradise is the public face, union the inner one. Do not cite Schuon for "birth" after plus four.

10. Karma.
Claim: Karma is the cosmic law that actions return to the actor, valid within the relative and dissolved by knowledge.
Who: scattered; locators unknown [U].
The words: paraphrase, and weak. I recall that he treats karma as the Hindu and Buddhist form of divine justice, a law of equilibrium in the world of Maya that does not touch Atma, and rejects the Western moral bank account paid out by reincarnation.
Tier: [U].
Use for the essay: do not credit Schuon for the "fingers" version. That is Alex's reading of one-Self doctrine, better supported by Coomaraswamy's one-transmigrant paper (P03).

11. Transmigration, reincarnation, and what becomes of the soul.
Claim: Schuon rejects reincarnation as popular Western reincarnationism (the same person comes back to improve). He accepts rebirth as a traditional doctrine of Hinduism and Buddhism, but reads it as the fate of a psychic residue or a change of state, and holds that a human individuality is not repeated. At death the body dissolves, the soul passes through intermediate states, and the Spirit, which is the Self, returns to the Principle.
Who: likely places are Spiritual Perspectives and Human Facts, Language of the Self and Form and Substance; locators unknown [U]. The school's position is documented in P02: Guenon rejected reincarnation in favour of transmigration as change of state, called reincarnation a modern idea, and wrote The Spiritist Fallacy against it [V in P02].
The words: paraphrase only; his wording is unverified. I recall with some confidence that he links reincarnationism to evolutionism and a progressive attitude to the soul, and treats the human state as exceptional. Less firmly, I recall that he reads the Abrahamic one life and the Indic many lives as different perspectives on the same seriousness of the human state [U].
Traditions cited: Hindu (the two Upanishadic paths, to the gods and to the fathers, Chandogya 5.10 and Brihadaranyaka 6.2 [M for the texts, U that he uses them]; Gita 2.22 and 9.21), Buddhist (rebirth without a fixed soul), Platonic (metempsychosis as myth [U]), Islamic (barzakh, Paradise), Christian (purgatory).
Tier: [M] that he rejects Western reincarnationism and that the school teaches survival and states of being; [U] for his positive account of the soul after death.
Use for the essay: be explicit. Schuon is not a witness for "the soul returns to this world". He is a witness for "something continues after death", for "the traditions disagree on the form", and for "the popular form is not the inner doctrine". The step to lifetimes comes from the axiom and not from the school.

12. Ethics founded on the One.
Claim: Charity and humility are ontological and not merely moral. The neighbour is the Self, so compassion is recognition, and pride is the ego's false absoluteness.
Who: Esoterism as Principle and as Way, Spiritual Perspectives and Human Facts, To Have a Center [M that virtue is a theme; locators unknown].
The words: paraphrase. Truth needs Virtue to be safe: without humility and charity the knowledge of the Absolute turns into spiritual pride. To love the neighbour is to recognise in him the Self you are. The Golden Rule is the moral corollary of "that thou art".
Traditions cited: Hindu (tat tvam asi, Isha 6 and 7), Christian (Matthew 22:39 and 25:40), Buddhist (karuna, metta), Islamic (the hadith that none believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself) [M for parallels, U for his citations].
Tier: [M] logic, [U] citations.
Use for the essay: Alex's fingers section already makes this move. Credit one comparer for Self-as-neighbour and for the warning on spiritual pride.

14. Where Schuon agrees with Guenon, and where he differs.
Claim: He accepts Guenon's frame and changes its emphasis.
Who: P02 on the split [V in P02]; my memory of Schuon [M, some U].
Agreement [M]: one primordial tradition; exoteric and esoteric layers; the Intellect; Advaita as the clearest statement of the metaphysic; rejection of Theosophy, spiritism and reincarnationism; cyclical decline; hostility to modernity.
Differences:
(a) Guenon's Non-Being above Being becomes Beyond-Being, and the pair Absolute and Infinite is Schuon's own [M].
(b) Christianity: Guenon held its sacraments had sunk to the exoteric level; Schuon repudiated this, and P02 reports it was the central point of their split in 1948 to 1950 [V in P02].
(c) Buddhism: Guenon first held it heterodox and later amended his references [V in P02]; Schuon treats it as an authentic revelation [M].
(d) Emphasis: Schuon gives devotion, beauty and virtue a place beside knowledge, and puts the prayer of the Name at the centre; Guenon is more austere [M].
Use for the essay: if Alex names the two together, one line should say they split.

15. Where Schuon agrees with Coomaraswamy, and where he differs.
Claim: They share the doctrine of one Self and many selves; they differ in method and temperament.
Who: P03 on Coomaraswamy; my memory of Schuon [M, some U].
Agreement [M]: the Self and the self; Platonic recollection as the Western form of the Indian doctrine; Eckhart as a Western Vedantin; metaphysics before psychology.
Difference: Coomaraswamy read Buddhism as essentially Hindu, which P03 notes Theravada scholars reject, while Schuon insists on its own revelation [M; P03 and P04 agree]. Coomaraswamy was a philologist; Schuon was a shaykh who held that unity is seen from inside one form [M, U]. I know of no Schuon text that criticises the one-transmigrant doctrine.
Use for the essay: for "who carries over", use Coomaraswamy (P03 finding 7, [V in P03]) and not Schuon.

16. Schuon on the Theosophists.
Claim: Schuon, like Guenon, counts Theosophy as pseudo-esoterism: Hindu and Buddhist words mixed with Western evolutionism, psychic phenomena and claimed Masters, with no lineage and no sound doctrine.
Who: Guenon's Theosophy: History of a Pseudo-Religion (1921) and The Spiritist Fallacy (1923) are the school's base texts [M; P02 covers]. Schuon's own remarks are in several books; locators unknown [U].
The words: paraphrase. Four objections as I recall them. Psychism: occult phenomena belong to the subtle plane and are not realisation. Evolutionism: reincarnation as progress is a modern myth. Syncretism: borrowing from many religions without belonging to any is not transcendent unity. No lineage: the Mahatmas have no regular chain. I recall no long Schuon chapter on it and cannot confirm he names Blavatsky [U].
Tier: [M] for the school's position, [U] for Schuon's own wording.
Use for the essay: "perennial philosophy and Theosophy" mixes two camps that disliked each other (P04 says the same). One sentence is enough. They were also rivals, each claiming a primordial wisdom behind all religions.

17. Cosmic decline, anti-evolutionism, and the word "arrow".
Claim: The Traditionalist cosmos is not progressive. Time runs from a golden age downward; evolution as a world-process is rejected, and so is the progress of the soul through repeated lives. Individuals can rise against the current.
Who: Schuon and Guenon in many books; Schuon's remarks on evolutionism and on Teilhard de Chardin, as I recall, in Logic and Transcendence and Form and Substance [U].
Tier: [M] cosmic view, [U] the Teilhard remark.
Use for the essay: Alex's arrow is a personal scale and does not claim that history improves. A reader who knows this school will hear "evolution", so one sentence ("the scale is for a person, I'm not claiming the world is getting better") removes the clash.

# 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

Schuon's method for these doctrines is not a list of overlapping quotations. It has five steps.

One: fix the level before comparing. A term is equated with another only at the same degree of reality. Nirguna Brahman, Dhat and the Godhead sit at Beyond-Being. Ishvara, Allah the Lord and God the Father sit at Being. Setting Ishvara beside Dhat is a category error in his system. This is what separates him from Huxley's anthology, which lays passages side by side without the ladder.

Two: classify each statement as exoteric or esoteric. Paradise is the outer side of deliverance and union the inner. When traditions seem to disagree (one life against many, eternal hell against final restoration), the disagreement is read as one between outer forms, and the inner teaching is sought behind it.

Three: explain differences by perspective. Each tradition stresses one aspect of the Absolute: knowledge, love, unity, emptiness. The Infinite admits many ways of looking, and a perspective that admitted all the others would stop working as one.

Four: the criterion is recognition by the Intellect, with comparison as a help.

Five: exclude counterfeits. Theosophy, spiritism and eclectic syncretism fail by lineage and doctrine. Here the school is least open to Alex's method of witnesses.

Examples: Dhat, Nirguna Brahman and the Godhead as one rung [M]. Moksha, fana and theosis as one event, with Paradise as its outer form [M]. Transmigration as change of state, Guenon's line [V in P02], accepted by Schuon in outline [U]. Maya and "He and not He" as one relative reality [M].

# 5. What scholars say

From memory, [M] unless marked.

Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (Oxford, 2004), is the standard history of Traditionalism. He treats Schuon as the most influential founder after Guenon and the school as an esoteric current opposed to modernity, and not as a neutral comparison of religions.

Wouter Hanegraaff and other historians of esotericism describe perennialism as a modern position making a theological claim (one truth behind all religions) and not an empirical finding [general line M, specific work U].

Steven Katz and the contextualists argue that mystical experience is shaped by tradition, so common cores are partly an artefact; Robert Forman and others reply with reports of pure consciousness. Schuon's stance (unity at the summit, difference below) is a main target.

Defenders: Huston Smith, Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Harry Oldmeadow, and Reza Shah-Kazemi, whose Paths to Transcendence (2006) sets Shankara, Ibn Arabi and Eckhart in Schuon's grid.

Critics inside religions: some Catholic writers, among them Rama Coomaraswamy, a son of A. K. Coomaraswamy, say the religio perennis relativises Christianity [U on names]. Buddhism and Advaita specialists note that perennialists read everything through Vedanta.

For Schuon's person, circle and the allegations around it, see P04. The doctrine can be used with care; the name carries baggage, so the essay need not rest on him.
