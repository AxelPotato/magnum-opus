## 1. The picture in brief

These passages do four jobs.

The one and the many. Brihadaranyaka 2.4.14, Katha 2.1.10 and Gita 13.16 each say the duality holds "as it were" (iva). Chandogya 6.2.3 has Being decide to become many; Brihadaranyaka 1.4 has the self alone, afraid, wanting a second. Plotinus V.1.1 and Marcus 8.34 add self-will and discontent as causes of separation, and Marcus adds that the cut-off part can grow back.

Lives. Gita 6.40 to 45 and 7.19 say that one life is not enough and progress carries over; Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 5 has the caterpillar and the goldsmith.

The wall as an obstacle that is removed. Yoga Sutra 4.3, Mundaka 2.2.8, Tao Te Ching 48, Zhuangzi 6, Plotinus I.6.9, Tattvartha Sutra 10.7 and Wang Yangming all describe liberation by subtraction. This is the strongest cross-tradition structure here, and it is Part one's letting go.

The ethic of the fingers: Marcus 2.1, Gita 13.29, Dhammapada 129, Mencius 2A6, Wang Yangming, Zhang Zai.

Where a tradition does not say it. SN 12.48 names "all is a unity" an extreme; MN 22 mocks one version of "the self is the world"; DN 16 denies a closed fist; Gaudapada 2.32 says nobody is bound or freed; the Sutrakritanga refutes the single soul; Zhang Zai ends in death as rest.

One error in the draft: in the Myth of Er the souls choose their lives before they drink from Lethe, not after (finding 38).

## 2. Findings

### A. Upanishads

Translators: Olivelle (OUP 1998), Hume (1921), Muller (SBE 1, 15).

1. Chandogya 6.2.1 to 3, 6.3.2. Uddalaka to Svetaketu. In the beginning Being alone, one without a second. It "thought" it would become many, and produced heat, water, food; in 6.3.2 it enters them as "this living self" and unfolds name and form. Search strings: "Being alone, one only, without a second"; "may I be many". Cf. Taittiriya 2.6, Rigveda 10.129. Tier [M]. Use: the safest ancient anchor for clause two.

2. Chandogya 6.8.7 and the refrain to 6.16. After the sleep teaching (6.8.1 to 6: in deep sleep one has gone to Being), Uddalaka says the finest essence is the self of all this, and "that you are" (tat tvam asi; Hume: "That art thou"). Advaita reads identity; Ramanuja and Madhva read it differently [M]. Tier [M]. Use: say the Chandogya teaches the self is that essence, and that Advaita is one reading.

3. Chandogya 6.9 to 6.11, 6.1.4, 8.7 to 12. Creatures that merge into Being (sleep, death) "do not know they have merged" (6.9.2), and whatever they were, tiger or gnat, they become again (6.9.3). 6.11.3: the body dies when the living leaves; the living does not. 8.7 to 12: Indra returns to Prajapati three times, finding each answer flawed. Tier [M]. Use: forgetting at the merge; a staged path. 6.9.3 is "same again", not growth.

4. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.1 to 4. The world was the self in the form of a person. It saw only itself, said "I am", feared, reasoned that fear comes from a second, and was not afraid. It did not enjoy, wanted a second, and split into husband and wife, then every pair of beings. Tier [M]. Use: a reason for separation (no joy alone) and a mechanism (fear needs a second), nearer "knowing needs a gap" than lila. It is myth, and Advaita calls it provisional.

5. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10. In the beginning this was Brahman. It knew only itself as "I am Brahman", and therefore became all this. Gods, seers and humans who awoke to this became that, and so now. One who worships another deity thinking "he is one, I another" does not know. Tier [M]. Use: the essay's sentence is right in outline. Add that knowing is what "becomes all", and that the end is polemic against dualist worship. Shankara's gloss [U].

6. Brihadaranyaka 2.4.14 (4.5.15 in the other recension). Yajnavalkya to Maitreyi: where there is duality, as it were (dvaitam iva), one sees another, smells another; where all has become one's own self, by what and whom would one see? By what would one know the knower? Search string: "where there is duality, as it were". Tier [M]. Use: best single line for "illusion is a wrong reading of something real", since the text itself says "as it were".

7. Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 7. The caterpillar draws itself to the next blade; the goldsmith makes a newer, more beautiful form (of fathers, gods, Brahma). 4.4.5: as it acts, so it becomes; as the desire, so the deed and fruit. 4.4.6 to 7: free of desire, it goes to Brahman; when all desires in the heart are cast off, a mortal becomes immortal. Tier [M]. Use: lives, growth, karma as becoming, and letting go of desire as the exit, in one chapter. Rebirth and karma appear as guarded new teaching (BU 3.2.13; BU 6.2 = Chandogya 5.10, taught by a king) [M].

8. Mundaka 2.1.1, 3.2.8 to 9; Taittiriya 3.1. As sparks fly from a fire, so from the Imperishable beings come forth and return. As rivers lose name and form in the sea, the knower goes to the divine Person; who knows Brahman becomes Brahman. Taittiriya: Brahman is that from which beings are born, live and enter. Tier [M]. Use: the axiom's three clauses in one numbered formula.

9. Mundaka 2.2.8 (cf. Katha 2.3.14 to 15). The knot of the heart is cut, all doubts split, one's karmas wear away when That, higher and lower, is seen. Search string: "knot of the heart". Tier [M]. Use: the Upanishads' wall is a knot undone by seeing. "Tension" is Alex's gloss.

10. Mundaka 3.1.1 to 2 (also Rigveda 1.164.20): two birds on one tree, one eating, one looking on. Advaita reads one self apparently two; theistic schools read two. Tier [M]. Use: the whole and the part in one body, no reading chosen.

11. Katha 2.1.10 to 11 (4.10 to 11 in Olivelle's continuous numbering [U]). What is here is there. He goes from death to death who sees here as if different (naneva). There is no diversity here at all. Tier [M]. Use: ties seeing difference to repeated death, the essay's own order.

12. Isha 6 and 7. One who sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings does not shrink from anything (na vijugupsate). For the seer of oneness (ekatvam), what delusion, what sorrow? "Recoils from nothing" is defensible; some write "feels no hatred". Kanva verse order differs [U]. Tier [M]. Use: the ethic that follows from oneness.

### B. Bhagavad Gita

Translators: B. S. Miller (1986), Sargeant (1984), Arnold (1885), Besant (1905), Radhakrishnan (1948).

13. Gita 2.13, 2.22, 15.8. 2.13: as the embodied one passes through childhood, youth and age in this body, so it passes to another; the wise are not confused. 2.22: worn clothes, new ones. 15.8: leaving a body, the lord takes the senses and mind along, as wind carries scent. Tier [M]. Use: 2.13 (identity through change in one life as the model for identity across lives) is sharper than the clothes; 15.8 is the "saved state" in a verse.

14. Gita 6.37 to 45, 7.19. Arjuna asks about the person whose practice fails. 6.40: no one who does good comes to a bad end. 6.43: he regains the understanding of his former body. 6.44: old practice carries him on. 6.45: the yogi perfected "through many births" (aneka-janma-samsiddhah) reaches the highest goal. 7.19: at the end of many births the knower comes to me, "Vasudeva is all". Tier [M]. Use: the best Indic text for "not enough time in one life" and progress carried over.

15. Gita 2.47, 2.48, 2.50, 12.12. Right to action, not to its fruits; do not cling to inaction. Equanimity is yoga (samatvam yoga ucyate). Yoga is skill in action (yogah karmasu kausalam). Tier [M]. Use: Part one's thesis, DO with BE, in Sanskrit; an anchor for Part three.

16. Gita 4.11, 7.21, 9.23 to 25. 4.11: as people approach me, so I meet them. 7.21: I make steady the faith of whatever form a devotee worships. But 7.23 calls that fruit finite, and 9.23 says worshippers of other gods worship me alone, "not by the right method". Tier [M]. Use: the Gita is inclusivist with a ranking, not pluralist. Say so.

17. Gita 6.5 to 6, 6.29 to 32, 13.28 to 29. 6.29: the yogi sees the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self. 6.32: one who sees the same everywhere by comparison with himself (atmaupamyena), in pleasure or pain, is the highest yogi. 6.5: the self is its own friend and enemy. 13.29: seeing the same Lord everywhere, he does not injure the Self by the self. Tier [M]. Use: 13.29 and 6.5 to 6 are the Gita's fingers.

18. Gita 13.16 (13.17 where Arjuna's opening verse is counted [U]), 15.7, 18.20. 13.16: undivided in beings, yet standing as if divided (vibhaktam iva). 15.7: an eternal fragment of me (mamaivamsah) becomes the individual self in the world of life. 18.20: the knowledge that sees one imperishable being in all, undivided in the divided, is the pure kind; seeing separate things by separateness is the passionate kind. Shankara reads amsa "as if a part", Ramanuja a real part [M]. Tier [M]. Use: 15.7 is the Gita's clause two; 18.20 ranks the vision of unity.

### C. Vedanta and Yoga

19. Brahma Sutra 2.1.32 to 35 (Thibaut, SBE 34). 2.1.32 objects that creation needs a motive; 2.1.33 answers that it is play (lila), as in the world. Shankara: a fulfilled Lord has no motive, and the question belongs to the empirical standpoint, since at the highest level there is no creation. 2.1.34 to 35 answers the charge of partiality by beginningless karma. Tier [M]. Use: lila is an answer within a standpoint, and karma enters as the answer to inequality.

20. Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 2.32 (repeated at 3.48). No dissolution, no origination, no one bound, no practitioner, no seeker, no one freed: this is the highest truth. Translators: Nikhilananda, Gambhirananda, King (SUNY 1995; he argues for Buddhist influence, contested). Tier [M]. Use: clause three is stated in the empirical register. At the highest level Advaita says nothing began and nothing ends.

21. Yoga Sutra 1.12, 2.3 to 9, 4.3 (Bryant 2009; Woods 1914). 1.12: the mind's fluctuations are stilled by practice (abhyasa) and non-attachment (vairagya). 2.3: five afflictions (ignorance, I-am-ness, attachment, aversion, clinging to life). 2.6: I-am-ness is seer and instrument seeming one (ekatmata iva). 4.3: the cause does not move nature but removes the obstruction, as a farmer breaks a dam and the water runs (Vyasa's example). Tier [M]. Use: 1.12 is Alex's two axes; 4.3 says letting go removes and does not add. Patanjali's goal is the seer's isolation from nature, so he is no witness for "we are one".

### D. Pali and Mahayana

Translators: Bodhi (SN, MN, AN), Thanissaro (Access to Insight), Walshe (DN), Rhys Davids (SBE), Fronsdal and Muller (Dhammapada).

22. Anguttara 6.63; Samyutta 15.14 to 19. "It is intention that I call karma." Samsara has no discoverable beginning; it is not easy to find a being who has not been your mother, father, brother, sister, son or daughter. Tier [M]; sub-numbers [S/M]. Use: karma as intention, no administrator; the Buddhist route to "everyone is family" is beginningless rebirth, not oneness.

23. Milindapanha, Book II (Rhys Davids, SBE 35). The reborn is "neither the same nor another". The lamp through the night watches is not the same flame, yet not another lamp. Tier [M], locator unknown. Use: safe for "something continues without a fixed soul".

24. Samyutta 12.48 (Lokayatika; Thanissaro, Bodhi). A cosmologist asks whether all exists, does not exist, is a unity, is a plurality. The Buddha calls these four cosmologies and teaches the middle: with ignorance as condition, formations arise. Tier [M]. Use: he treats "all is a unity" as an extreme. Say so wherever Buddhism is quoted as a witness.

25. Samyutta 22.59, 22.89. 22.59: the five aggregates are not self; "this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self". 22.89 (Khemaka): he sees no self in the aggregates, yet a residue of "I am" remains, like lye in a washed cloth. Tier [M]. Use: the border of the self comes off in stages.

26. Samyutta 36.6; Majjhima 118. 36.6: the untrained feel two pains, bodily and mental, like two arrows; the trained feel the first. MN 118: sixteen steps; the last is breathing in and out contemplating relinquishment (patinissagga). Tier [M]. Use: the second arrow is non-acceptance; step sixteen is letting go in the Buddha's words.

27. Majjhima 22. The Dhamma is a raft for crossing, not for holding. It calls the view "the self is the world, after death I shall be permanent" wholly a fool's teaching; Gombrich reads this as an answer to Upanishadic identification (cf. Chandogya 3.14.4) [M]. Tier [M]; paragraph unknown. Use: a Buddhist reader will cite it against "the self is the world".

28. Digha 16. The Buddha has taught without inner and outer; the Tathagata has no "closed fist of a teacher" (acariya-mutthi). Walshe and Thanissaro 2.25, Rhys Davids 2.32 [U]. Tier [M], numbering [U]. Use: any esoteric layer in Buddhism must face this; say "later schools held".

29. Udana 6.4, 8.3; Udana 5.5 with Anguttara 8.19. 6.4: sectarians quarrel, each seeing one side; the point is non-clinging to views, and "religions as parts of one elephant" is a later reading (Jain syadvada, Rumi). 8.3: there is an unborn, unmade, unconditioned. This Dhamma has one taste, release. Tier [M]. Use: "one taste" is said of this Dhamma, not of religions.

30. Dhammapada 129 to 130; Sutta Nipata 705 (Nalaka Sutta [U]). All tremble at violence, all fear death; putting oneself in another's place (attanam upamam katva) one should not kill. Sn 705: as I am, so are they. Tier [M]. Use: likeness, not identity. File it with Gita 6.32 and the golden rule.

31. Heart Sutra (Conze 1958; Red Pine 2004; Nattier, JIABS 15.2, 1992). Form is emptiness, emptiness is form. Nattier argues the Sanskrit is a back-translation from the Chinese (Xuanzang's version, 649); widely accepted, details contested [U]. Tier [M]. Use: emptiness means lacking own-being (svabhava), not a substance; "the walls are not solid" is Alex's reading. Equating sunyata with the Absolute equates what Madhyamaka keeps apart.

32. Bodhicaryavatara 3.25, 8.90 to 91, 8.120 (Padmakara 1997, Wallace 1997, Crosby and Skilton 1995). 3.25: today I am born in the Buddhas' family. 8.91: as the body, divided into limbs, is guarded as one, so the divided world, undivided in suffering and happiness (abhinna-duhkha-sukhatmakam). 8.120: exchange of self and other. Tier [M] content, [U] numbering: Padmakara and Wallace follow the Tibetan, Crosby and Skilton the Sanskrit, so numbers may differ by a few verses. Use: 8.91 is a body image, not monism; "one in wanting happiness" is a gloss.

33. Mahayana additions [M]. Awakening of Faith (Hakeda 1967): One Mind, ignorance as wind on water; Critical Buddhism (Hubbard and Swanson 1997) denies that this strand is Buddhism. Nagarjuna, MMK 25.19: no difference between samsara and nirvana. Use: the Awakening of Faith is nearest the axiom, and some Buddhists reject it.

### E. Jain

34. Acharanga 1.5.5 (Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; Mahaprajna's Bhasyam). "tumam si nama sacceva" is said of the one you think to kill or command. Whether Jacobi writes "you are he" or "like you", I cannot recall; Mahaprajna reads oneness of killer and killed, the older commentary "like you" [U]. Tier [U]. Use: do not write "none other than you" until a named translation is read.

35. Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, 10.5 to 8 (Tatia 1994). 5.21: parasparopagraho jivanam, souls support one another. 10.5: the liberated soul rises to the end of the cosmos. 10.7 gives the images: potter's wheel, a gourd freed of clay, a castor seed, a flame; 10.8: it stops for want of a medium of motion. Tier [M]. Use: 5.21 is interdependence of many souls. The gourd rising when the clay is gone is the Jain picture of letting go. The souls stay many.

36. Sutrakritanga 1.1.1, about verse 9 (Jacobi, SBE 45): the one-soul view (an earth-lump appearing as many things) is refuted [U]. I could not tie 1.11.33 to content [U]. Use: Jainism rejects clause one; do not cite it for that.

### F. Greek

Translators: Jowett (1892), Grube and Reeve (Hackett); Plotinus: MacKenna (1917 to 1930), Armstrong (Loeb), Gerson et al. (2018).

37. Republic 509b and 514a to 520e. The Good is not being (ousia) but beyond being (epekeina tes ousias), exceeding it in rank and power (search string, Jowett: "far exceeds essence in dignity and power"); scholars debate above existence or above essence; Plotinus read it as the One. Cave: prisoners take shadows for the real (515c); education is turning the whole soul around (periagoge, 518c to d); the philosopher must go down again (520c). Tier [M]. Use: periagoge sits beside teshuvah, tawba and metanoia.

38. Republic 614 to 621, esp. 617e, 620 to 621. Souls draw lots and choose lives; the blame is the chooser's, God is blameless (617e); choices follow habits of the former life (619c to d). After choosing, each receives a guardian, passes to the plain of Lethe, drinks from the river Ameles, then is born (620e to 621b). Tier [M], high confidence on the order. Correction: the draft says souls choose "after drinking from the river of forgetfulness". Plato has them choose first and drink after. Fix before release.

39. Phaedo 64a, 67c to e, 72c to d; Phaedrus 246a to 249d. Phaedo: philosophers practise dying; purification gathers the soul into itself from the body; if the living came from the dead and never returned, all would end dead. Phaedrus: a winged soul loses its wings and sinks until it takes a body (246c to d); wings regrow in ten thousand years, or in three lives of philosophy (249a). Cf. Symposium 210a to 212a (the ladder). Tier [M]. Use: practice of dying as a Platonic letting go; fall, return and long time are all in Plato.

40. Plotinus IV.9 (Armstrong: "On the Question of Whether All Souls Are One"), IV.8.8. Soul is both one and many, and the unity explains sympathy between souls; individual souls are not numerically identical [M, details U]. IV.8.8: our soul does not descend entire. Tier [M]. Use: the Neoplatonic "one and many", souls also distinct.

41. Plotinus V.1.1. What made the souls forget the father, God, and ignore themselves and him? The beginning of evil was audacity (tolma), birth, the first otherness and the wish to belong to themselves (to boulethenai heauton einai). Pleased with their freedom they ran the other way. Tier [M]. Use: separation by the part's own will, a different answer from lila.

42. Plotinus I.6.9, VI.9.11. I.6.9: if you do not yet see yourself beautiful, like a sculptor cut away the excess, straighten the crooked, clear the dark, and never stop working on your statue ("never cease working on your statue"). VI.9.11 (the last lines): release from the alien, the flight of the alone to the Alone (phuge monou pros monon). Cf. V.3.17, "take away everything" (aphele panta). Tier [M]. Use: letting go as sculpture by subtraction. VI.9.11 is union with the One, and Gerson and Rist dispute whether the individual survives.

43. Marcus Aurelius 2.1, 4.40, 8.34, 11.8 (Long 1862; Haines, Loeb 1916; Hays 2002). 2.1: the meddler and the ungrateful are kin, by shared mind and a portion of the divine, "made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth". 4.40: the world as one living being. 8.34: a hand, foot or head cut off is what a man makes of himself who is not content with what happens and separates from others; yet it is in his power to rejoin. 11.8 repeats it. Search string: "in your power to unite yourself again". Tier [M]. Use: Marcus is Part one's high-agency exemplar, and here he gives the fingers, the cause of the cut (not accepting what happens, Gennady's non-acceptance) and the way back: the strongest bridge between Parts one and two.

### G. Chinese

Translators: Lau (1963), Legge (SBE 39 to 40); Watson (1968), Graham (1981), Ziporyn (2009); Chan, Source Book (1963).

44. Tao Te Ching 1, 25, 42. 1: the nameless is the beginning of heaven and earth, the named the mother of the ten thousand things. 25: something undifferentiated and complete before heaven and earth; great, it passes on, goes far, returns (fan). 42: the Way gave birth to one, one to two, two to three, three to the ten thousand things. Tier [M]. The Way is prior to the One, so Dao is not "the One" (commentators differ, [U]). Use: a Daoist cosmogony with return; do not equate Dao with Brahman.

45. Tao Te Ching 16, 48. 16: attain utmost emptiness, hold to stillness; the ten thousand things arise together and I watch them return, each to its root, which is stillness. 48: in pursuit of learning one gains daily; in pursuit of the Way one loses daily, until non-action; with non-action nothing is left undone (wu wei er wu bu wei). Tier [M]. Use: 48 is letting go as method; "nothing left undone" is DO from BE.

46. Zhuangzi 2 (Qiwulun). The butterfly dream ends: between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction, called the transformation of things (Watson, Graham, Ziporyn agree on the gist). Also: heaven and earth were born with me and the ten thousand things are one with me; and "one" plus the saying "one" makes two, without end. Tier [M]. Graham and Hansen read ch. 2 as perspectival skepticism, not an ontological One [M]. Use: the draft's butterfly sentence is accurate. If "one with me" is added, note that the chapter doubts "one" can be said.

47. Zhuangzi 4, 6, 7. 4: the fasting of the mind. 6: Yan Hui sits and forgets (zuo wang): limbs and body fall away, perception and intellect are dismissed, he merges with the Great Thoroughfare. 7: the perfect person uses the mind like a mirror, welcoming nothing, storing nothing; Hundun (Chaos) dies when seven holes are bored in him. Tier [M]. Use: "sitting and forgetting" and "no storing" are Daoist letting go. Hundun is a story against over-interference, not a fall.

48. Mencius 2A6, 7A4 (Lau; Legge 2.1.6). Anyone suddenly seeing a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion, not to win the parents' favour or praise. Compassion is the sprout (duan) of benevolence. 7A4: all things are complete in me; treat others as you wish to be treated. Tier [M]. Use: feeling before reasons, a test for the first view's closed self.

49. Wang Yangming, Inquiry on the Great Learning (1527; Chan, Instructions for Practical Living, 1963). The great person regards heaven, earth and the ten thousand things as one body, from the humanity of the mind and not by deliberate choice. A child about to fall in a well, crying birds, broken plants, shattered tiles each draw a feeling of "unbearableness" (buren). The small person has the same oneness but is obscured by selfish desire. The Great Learning removes the obscuration and restores the original one-body. Tier [M]. Scholars read the one-body as moral and ontological together (Tu Weiming, Ivanhoe) [M]. Use: the best Chinese witness for the whole axiom: one body, obscured by desire, restored by removal.

50. Zhang Zai, Western Inscription (Ximing, c. 1076; Chan, Source Book). Heaven is my father and earth my mother; what fills the universe I regard as my body, what directs it as my nature. All people are my siblings (tongbao), all things my companions. It ends: in life I serve with obedience, in death I will be at peace. Cheng Yi defended it by "principle is one, manifestations many" (li yi fen shu). Tier [M]. Use: the fingers in Confucian dress. The ending is no rebirth: Zhang Zai holds that qi condenses and scatters like ice and water, which fits the essay's "keep the growth and drop the return".

## 4. How they compare

The anthologist sets short passages under a theme and lets the echo work (Huxley 1945, Huston Smith, Radhakrishnan's Gita commentary). The equated terms are the anthologist's: Brahman, Godhead, the One, the Tao. Translators have often done the equating already: translators such as MacKenna and Waley render terms as "the One" and "the Way" with other traditions in their ears [U].

The Traditionalist builds a metaphysical grid (Absolute, degrees of reality, form and essence) and reads each text through it; Coomaraswamy's "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (1944) traces one doctrine through Indic, Platonic and Christian sources [details in G1-3]. Differences become differences of form or level.

The Theosophists assume an older Wisdom-Religion, concealed or corrupted. They cite Gita 2.22 as the standard reincarnation text (Judge, Besant) [M]. Differences are loss and concealment.

Native comparers exist too: Cheng Yi's li yi fen shu (one and many), Ramakrishna's landing steps on one lake (grounded in Gita 4.11, against 9.23), Buddhist skilful means, Advaita's two standpoints.

Weak points recur: a metaphor lifted out of context (the elephant); terms equated that the traditions keep apart (Brahman, Dao, the One, sunyata; atman and anatta); texts read for what they do not claim (SN 12.48, MN 22, DN 16, Sutrakritanga 1).

A method that suits Alex: find the word that carries the claim in each passage (iva, tolma, amsa, svabhava, periagoge, buren), say what role it plays in its own argument, then compare roles. Plotinus says tolma, Brihadaranyaka fear, Shankara lila, Marcus discontent.

## 5. What scholars say

Katz (1978) argues mystical experience is shaped by tradition; Forman (1990) argues for a "pure consciousness event" across traditions; Prothero (2010) argues the religions answer different questions. Olivelle treats rebirth and karma as late Upanishadic developments, Bronkhorst as non-Vedic in origin.

Gombrich (1996, 2009) reads the Buddha as answering Upanishadic ideas; Collins (1982) reads anatta as denying a permanent self. Dodds (1951) and Burkert (1972) trace Plato's soul lore to Orphic and Pythagorean sources. Graham, Hansen and Ziporyn differ on whether Zhuangzi 2 teaches one reality or perspectival freedom.
