## 1. The picture in brief

"Theosophy" names three different things, and Alex's essay needs to keep them apart.

First, an old word. Theosophia is Greek for divine wisdom. It turns up in late antiquity. From the seventeenth century it named a school of speculative Christian thought around Jakob Boehme, carried on by William Law, Oetinger, Saint-Martin and Swedenborg's followers. Scholars still call Kabbalah and the Shi'a hikma tradition "theosophies" in that technical sense.

Second, a society. In 1875 in New York, Helena Blavatsky, Henry Olcott, William Judge and others founded the Theosophical Society. It drew on Spiritualism, Mesmerism, Freemasonry, Hermetic and Rosicrucian literature, Kabbalah, Emerson's circle and then Indian texts. Blavatsky said her Theosophy was the Wisdom-Religion, the one source of all religions, and said the name came from the third century school of Ammonius Saccas. Historians treat that genealogy as a nineteenth century reconstruction.

Third, a way of knowing: teaching said to come from living Masters.

The perennial philosophy shares the family idea (one core under many religions) and uses another method: anthology, metaphysical exposition and initiation inside an existing tradition. Theosophy was the largest organised Western carrier of the common-core idea and taught millions of readers karma, rebirth and the unity of life. The Traditionalists (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) were cousins who disowned it: Guenon wrote a whole book against it in 1921, and Coomaraswamy had passed through it as a young man.

The two agree in outline on one Principle, a soul identical with it, many levels, the world as veil, religions as forms and liberation as return. They diverge on reincarnation, initiation, orthodoxy, Masters, and progress against decline. For Alex: Blavatsky's 1888 third proposition is a close forerunner of his three-clause axiom (finding 7).

## 2. Findings

1. The word, and Blavatsky's story of it
Claim: theosophia is a Greek compound (theos, sophia), divine wisdom or wisdom about the divine. Late antique use is real but I cannot give a locator; the Tubingen Theosophy (a late fifth century anthology of pagan oracles) is a later example [U]. Lutheran mystical writers adopted the word, and Gichtel's Amsterdam edition of Boehme (1682) was titled Theosophia revelata [M].
Who: Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy (1889), opening section. Gist: Theosophy is divine knowledge, not a new religion. The word goes back to the third century and the Alexandrian school of Ammonius Saccas, whose followers were called lovers of truth (Philaletheians), Analogeticists and Eclectic Theosophists [M for the gist, [U] for the section number].
Her further claim: Diogenes Laertius traces a theosophic system to an Egyptian priest called Pot-Amun [U]. What Diogenes Laertius, Lives, Prologue 21, does say is that Potamo of Alexandria introduced an eclectic school [M]. Scholars read Potamo as a Greek name, so the Egyptian etymology is hers.
Parallels: Neoplatonic, Egyptian, Hindu.
Tier: [M] and [U]. Use: "an old Greek word for divine wisdom" is safe. Do not repeat Pot-Amun.

2. Ammonius Saccas
Claim: Ammonius (died about 242, Alexandria, nicknamed Saccas) taught Plotinus for about eleven years from 232 [M]. He wrote nothing. Porphyry reports that Plotinus, Herennius and an Origen agreed not to divulge his teachings, which later readers took as a sign of a secret doctrine [M].
Who: Porphyry, Life of Plotinus, section 3 [M]. Hierocles of Alexandria, through Photius, calls him theodidaktos (God-taught) and says he reconciled Plato and Aristotle [M].
Blavatsky's picture of an eclectic reconciler of all religions comes from eighteenth century church history (Mosheim) and from her friend Alexander Wilder, who edited Isis Unveiled [M]. Historians say little is known of his teaching and that "Neoplatonism" is a modern label.
Parallels: Greek, Egyptian, Christian, reconciled by one man.
Tier: [M]. Use: if Ammonius appears, say "Blavatsky's story of him". For a verified Neoplatonic witness use Plotinus himself (finding 20).

3. Boehme and Christian theosophy
Claim: Boehme (1575 to 1624, a shoemaker of Gorlitz) wrote Aurora (1612), Mysterium Magnum (1623), De signatura rerum and The Way to Christ. His God is an abyss (the Ungrund, the "unground") that becomes manifest to itself through will, opposition and nature. The soul's task is to return [M for the gist; wording and locators [U]].
Followers: the Philadelphian Society (Jane Leade, London, 1694), William Law (the Law translation of Boehme, 1764 to 1781), Oetinger, Baader, Saint-Martin; Hegel and Schelling read him [M].
Scholarly frame: Antoine Faivre calls this "Christian theosophy", speculative knowledge of God, nature and the human being in one scheme [M].
Parallels: Christian, Kabbalistic, Paracelsian.
Tier: [M]. Use: a Western precedent for a whole that reveals itself through opposition. Check a source before any sentence about Boehme goes in.

4. Swedenborg
Claim: Swedenborg (1688 to 1772) taught correspondences between the visible and spiritual worlds, which is a way of reading scripture and nature as symbol, close to Blavatsky's "Analogeticists". He described heaven as a Grand Man (Homo Maximus) in which each soul and community has a place like an organ [M for the idea, locators [U]].
He rejected reincarnation: one earthly life, then development in the afterlife [M].
His followers formed a "Theosophical Society" in London in 1783 [U].
Emerson wrote on him (Representative Men, 1850) [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: the Grand Man is a Western picture very near Alex's fingers of one hand. His denial of rebirth shows the Christian theosophical stream did not supply the reincarnation in the Theosophical package.

5. Saint-Martin and Pasqually: fall and reintegration
Claim: Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin (1743 to 1803), "the Unknown Philosopher", wrote Des erreurs et de la verite (1775), Le Tableau naturel (1782), L'Homme de desir (1790), and translated Boehme into French [M]. His teacher Martines de Pasqually (died 1774) left a Traite de la reintegration des etres (written about 1770, printed 1899), about spirits fallen into matter and their return [M]. The Elus Coens and later Martinism came from this.
Parallels: Christian, Kabbalistic, Hermetic.
Tier: [M]. Use: "reintegration" is an old Western word for the axiom's third clause (the separation ends).

6. The Society's sources: Masonry, Rosicrucians, Hermeticism, Mesmerism, Spiritualism
Claim: the 1875 founders came out of that milieu. Olcott met Blavatsky at the Eddy farmhouse in Vermont in 1874 while reporting on mediums [M]. Her Isis Unveiled (1877) defends the phenomena and attacks the spirit theory. Mesmerism and Eliphas Levi supplied the "astral light" idea [M]. Masonry supplied lodge form, degrees and the "Unknown Superiors" trope, ancestor of the Masters [M]. Rosicrucian texts (Fama Fraternitatis, 1614) and the Kabbala Denudata (1677 to 1684) fed the Hermetic and Kabbalistic parts [M]. A parallel body: Kingsford and Maitland's The Perfect Way (1882) [M].
Who: Joscelyn Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment (1994), argues Theosophy continues eighteenth century esotericism [M]. Bruce Campbell, Ancient Wisdom Revived (1980), is the standard history [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: a short sentence "Theosophy grew from Spiritualism, Freemasonry and the older Hermetic and Christian mystical traditions" is safe.

7. Emerson's Over-Soul and Blavatsky's third proposition
Claim: Emerson's essay "The Over-Soul" (Essays, First Series, 1841) pictures one soul of the whole in which each person takes part, and says we live in division, in parts [M]. A sibling slice (P01) reports a Standard Ebooks snippet of the sentence; I did not see it.
Blavatsky's The Secret Doctrine (1888), Proem, "three fundamental propositions": (1) an omnipresent, eternal, boundless and immutable Principle; (2) the periodic manifestation of the universe; (3) the fundamental identity of all souls with the Universal Over-Soul, and the obligatory pilgrimage of every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law [M for content and the use of "Over-Soul"; locator about p. 17 of the 1888 edition, [U]].
Parallels: Transcendentalist, Hindu, Theosophical. Emerson's essay "Compensation" says in effect that the thief steals from himself [M, wording unverified].
Tier: [M]. Use: the third proposition is the clearest historical forerunner of Alex's axiom: one whole, souls identical with it, a pilgrimage through separate lives, return. Offer it as "an 1888 statement of the same shape" after checking the wording against the book (public domain).

8. The Wisdom-Religion and its names
Claim: Blavatsky and her circle used a set of names for the one old teaching. "Wisdom-Religion" (Isis Unveiled onward), "Gupta Vidya" (Sanskrit, secret knowledge), "Brahma Vidya" (knowledge of Brahman, an Upanishadic term), "Bodhism" or Bodhi Dharma (wisdom-teaching, which she preferred to "Buddhism"; not the monk Bodhidharma), "Archaic Doctrine", "Esoteric Philosophy" [M for the names, [U] for exact sources]. Besant's The Ancient Wisdom (1897) made it a textbook [M].
The claim of transmission: the teaching was kept by an initiated brotherhood (the Masters) from remote ages, with Atlantean and Lemurian "root races" in the story [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: the names show Theosophy borrowed Sanskrit terms to say what Boehmian and Neoplatonic texts already said. Alex can name them as vocabulary, not as authority.

9. Theosophy as carrier of the common-core idea
Claim: the idea has a long genealogy (Plethon, Ficino, Steuco, Leibniz; sibling slice P01). Wouter Hanegraaff (New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996; Esotericism and the Academy, 2012) treats the Theosophical Society as the main nineteenth century channel that turned it into a mass idea and brought Asian texts into the Western esoteric tradition, and traces the New Age to it [M gist, locators unknown].
Antoine Faivre (Access to Western Esotericism, 1994) names "concordance" (the search for common ground between traditions) as a typical mark of Western esotericism [M].
Other carriers: Orientalists (Jones, Mueller), Transcendentalists, Vivekananda and Ramakrishna, the Baha'i faith, Spiritualism [M]. Mueller's Gifford lectures "Theosophy or Psychological Religion" (1892) reclaim the word for Vedantic religion [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: "one of the main routes" is accurate; "the" main route is not.

10. Guenon: the hostile cousin
Claim: Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951) was in the Paris occult milieu (Gnostic Church, Martinism, Masonry), left it, and wrote Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923) [M]. Gist of the attack [M, details unchecked]: Theosophy mixes Western occultism with a distorted Orientalism; it lacks regular initiatory transmission; its Masters are unverifiable; it rests on a modern belief in progress and evolution; its reincarnation is a misreading. He also attacked Besant's Krishnamurti project.
Who: Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), on Guenon's break with the milieu [M].
Parallels: Guenon's own doctrine uses Hindu terms (Atma, Maya, "multiple states of being" in Les Etats multiples de l'etre, 1932), says all authentic traditions are branches of a primordial tradition, and also has a hidden centre (Agarttha, Le Roi du monde, 1927), which looks like Theosophy's hidden Masters [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: say they compared, and did not agree. His rejection is about method and authority, and leaves the shared core alone.

11. Coomaraswamy: a Theosophical youth, and the one transmigrant
Claim: Ananda Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947) joined the Theosophical Society around 1907 and left it later; a sibling slice (P03) reports this from several web pages [M for me]. His mature writing followed his correspondence with Guenon in the 1930s [M]. The Theosophical Forum (Pasadena, March 1944) reviewed his Hinduism and Buddhism as showing Hinduism as one face of the philosophia perennis (reported by P03).
His essay "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3, 1944) argues from the Upanishads that the one thing that passes through all lives is the one Self, not a separate personal soul [M for the title and gist, locator [U]].
Tier: [M]. Use: this essay fits Alex's axiom closely (one Self goes through every life) and separates him from the Theosophical individual-ego model. Eliade's 1979 essay "Some Notes on Theosophia Perennis" pairs Coomaraswamy with Corbin (P03 reports the citation).

12. Schuon: unity above the forms
Claim: Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998), De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English 1953), holds that religions differ in outer forms (the exoteric) and converge in an inner, formless reality (the esoteric); "religio perennis" is his name for that [M]. Initiation and orthodoxy within one tradition are the way to it.
His view of Theosophy: the school treated it as pseudo-esoterism, like Guenon [U for Schuon's own wording and place].
Publishing detail: a Quest Books edition of The Transcendent Unity of Religions came from the Theosophical Publishing House, with an introduction by Huston Smith [U].
Tier: [M] for the thesis, [U] for the rest. Use: the exoteric and esoteric split is the cleanest answer to why religions differ, and it is a Traditionalist answer, not a Theosophical one.

14. India and Ceylon: the Theosophical encounter
Claim: Blavatsky and Olcott moved to India in 1878 and set the headquarters at Adyar in 1882 [M]. They allied with the Arya Samaj of Dayananda Saraswati, then broke with him in 1882 [M]. In Ceylon in 1880 they took the Buddhist five precepts publicly. Olcott wrote the Buddhist Catechism (1881) and, in 1891, a list of fourteen Buddhist propositions accepted by Buddhists of several lands, which is a "common core" in practice [M]. Anagarika Dharmapala joined as a youth and later broke with them [M].
Scholars: Stephen Prothero, The White Buddhist (1996); Gananath Obeyesekere and Richard Gombrich, Buddhism Transformed (1988), on Theosophy's part in "Protestant Buddhism" [M]. Mark Bevir ("The West Turns Eastward", JAAR 1994) argues Blavatsky joined the occult tradition to Asian ideas [M]. Tim Rudbog and Erik Sand, Imagining the East (2020), is the recent study [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: the movement was a two-way exchange, not Indian teaching passed on neutrally.

15. Besant, Nehru, Gandhi
Annie Besant (1847 to 1933) joined in 1889 after reading The Secret Doctrine, led the society from 1907, wrote The Ancient Wisdom (1897) and a Gita translation (1895), founded the Central Hindu College (1898, source of Banaras Hindu University, 1916), led the Home Rule campaign and was president of the Indian National Congress in 1917 [M]. Allan Octavian Hume, founder of the Congress, was a Theosophist [M]. Jawaharlal Nehru was introduced to Theosophy by a tutor and joined as a boy (his Autobiography) [M].
Gandhi: in London (1889) two Theosophist brothers had him read the Gita (in Arnold's translation); he met Blavatsky and Besant, read the Key to Theosophy, which made him want to read Hindu books and cured him of the missionary view that Hinduism was superstition; and he declined to join, saying he knew too little of his own religion to belong to any body (Autobiography, Part I, ch. 20) [M gist; paraphrase only].
Tier: [M]. Use: the Gandhi anecdote is an honest, small, checkable use of Theosophy in the essay: a book of comparison that sent a reader back to his own tradition.

16. Where the two movements agree
In outline [M]: (a) one ultimate Principle beyond the world (Parabrahman for Blavatsky; the Absolute, "Beyond-Being" for the Traditionalists); (b) the human core is not other than it (third proposition; the Atman in Huxley's second part and in Schuon); (c) reality has levels (planes; states of being); (d) the world is veil or maya; (e) religions are forms of one truth, with an inner and an outer side; (f) liberation is return, by knowledge and conduct; (g) modern materialism misses all of it; (h) both take the Hindu cyclic time scheme (yugas, a present dark age) seriously. Both claim a hidden spiritual centre.
Tier: [M]. Use: (a), (b), (d), (e), (f) are the shared core Alex can say "both movements point to" with little risk, if the wording is kept general. These are my mapping of the two sets of ideas and not the authors' own claim of agreement.

17. Where they diverge
(a) Reincarnation. Theosophy teaches repeated rebirth of the higher individuality (Blavatsky's Isis Unveiled, 1877, rejected the Spiritist version; by the 1880s she taught it; scholars note the shift) [M]. Guenon rejected it as an error, and the school generally rejects individual return, though Coomaraswamy and Schuon treat Hindu and Buddhist rebirth as the teaching of those traditions [M for Guenon, [U] for the others]. Swedenborg also rejected it (finding 4).
(b) Initiation. The Society had open membership with a later Esoteric Section (1888) and graded "initiations" under Besant and Leadbeater; the Traditionalists require regular transmission in an existing tradition [M].
(c) Orthodoxy. Theosophy: "no religion higher than truth", with no need to belong to any religion. Traditionalists: a person needs a real tradition and its forms [M].
(d) Masters against lineages. Theosophy claims living Mahatmas as source; Traditionalists claim authority from actual lineages (Guenon's Shadhili initiation, Schuon's Maryamiyya) and from intellectual intuition [M].
(e) Direction of history. Theosophy: evolution of souls and races; Traditionalists: cyclic decline, and Perry's The Widening Breach (1995) attacks evolutionism [M].
(f) Attitude to modernity. Theosophy sought a synthesis of science, religion and philosophy (the subtitle of The Secret Doctrine); Traditionalism is anti-modern [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: where Alex says "perennial philosophy and Theosophy," he is joining two groups whose members would object to each other. A one-line note will do.

## 4. How they compare

Blavatsky. She claims one archaic source and then looks for its traces. Method: (a) parallel cosmogonies (The Secret Doctrine sets "stanzas" beside commentary drawn from the Puranas, Kabbalah, Chaldean, Egyptian, Greek, Gnostic and Christian texts); (b) analogy and correspondence, reading myths as the soul's events (the "Analogeticists" idea); (c) etymology and symbol; (d) citation of Orientalist scholarship when it helps and attack when it does not [M]. She explains differences as corruption by priests and literalists and as deliberate "blinds" (veils) by the initiates [M]. Authority rests on the Masters and her own inner knowledge. Olav Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, 2001) names three strategies in this literature: appeal to tradition, appeal to experience, and scientism [M].

Besant's Four Great Religions and Esoteric Christianity read each creed as a veiled form of one plan [M].

The Traditionalists. Start from a metaphysical principle, then show that each authentic tradition states it in its own terms. Coomaraswamy's way is dense equations across Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and German terms with footnotes (for example, Eckhart's Godhead beside Brahman) [M]. Differences are explained as different forms revealed for different peoples, each valid and each aiming at the same summit; the unity is at the formless level, not in history [M]. No claim of a single historical source.

Huxley's way (one line, for contrast): anthology by theme, read side by side, a "highest common factor"; differences put down to temperament and the ineffability of the Reality [M, phrase unverified].

Campbell: comparative mythology, with Jungian archetypes and Bastian's elementary ideas as the cause of sameness [M]. Wilber: a map of levels (the Great Nest) and a theory of knowing that tries to make the claim testable [M]. Smith: a phenomenology of each religion, then a primordial scheme of levels of reality and of selfhood [M].

The contrast: Blavatsky's explanation is historical (one source, then corruption); Schuon's is metaphysical (one Reality, many forms); Campbell's is psychological. Each is a claim by the comparer, and not by the traditions.

## 5. What scholars say

Hanegraaff, Godwin, Bevir and Campbell (the historian) place Theosophy in a long line of Western esotericism and note that it tied that line to Asian texts for the first time at scale. They treat the Masters as the modern form of the old "Unknown Superiors" and the "Stanzas of Dzyan" as unverifiable [M].
Richard Hodgson's 1885 report to the Society for Psychical Research judged Blavatsky's phenomena fraudulent; Vernon Harrison's 1986 paper in the SPR Journal argued the report was flawed (both are described in the repo's notes) [M]. William Emmette Coleman (1895) catalogued sources she drew on [M]. K. Paul Johnson argues the Masters had historical models; this is contested [M].
Sedgwick (2004) shows Traditionalism as a modern movement with an anti-modern message, and shows how much it owed to the occult milieu it condemned [M]. On the common-core idea itself, Steven Katz (1978) and the constructivists say mystical experience is shaped by its tradition; Forman, Hick and Smith defend modest versions (as the repo notes already record) [M].
On India: Theosophy is credited with stirring pride in Hindu and Buddhist heritage, and criticised for helping shape a "Protestant Buddhism" and a Hinduism fitted to Western taste [M].
Overall: influential, with a mixed reputation.
