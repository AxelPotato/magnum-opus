# 1. The picture in brief

On lives and karma the perennial camp is split, and the split is the useful fact. There are five camps.

The strict Traditionalists (Guenon, and after him Schuon, Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt) deny reincarnation in the Western sense, the return of the same individual to an earthly life. They call it a nineteenth century error born in Spiritism and Theosophy. They teach instead that a being has many states, that no state is repeated, and that the Hindu and Buddhist talk of rebirth is a lower or popular way of speaking about this.

Coomaraswamy keeps transmigration but changes the question. The one who goes from life to life is not the individual but the one Self, "the one and only transmigrant". This is the nearest thing in the whole literature to Alex's axiom.

The Theosophists (Blavatsky, Olcott, Sinnett, Besant) put reincarnation and karma at the centre, with a soul that carries the harvest of each life into the next.

Huxley, Smith and Wilber do not make rebirth part of the common core. Jung translates it into psychology.

They disagree for five reasons: a metaphysical argument (nothing is repeated), loyalty to an Abrahamic home religion, a polemic against modern ideas of progress, a quarrel over which layer of a tradition counts as the real teaching, and a quarrel over who or what is the subject that continues.

They agree on more than they admit: the ego is not the final self, the being has more room than one short life, action has consequences that outlast the act, and the aim is the same return. Alex's axiom does not need to take a side on reincarnation, because on his axiom the one who lives every life is the whole.

# 2. Findings

1. The map of positions. Claim: no single perennial position on rebirth exists. Who: synthesis of Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon, Blavatsky, Huxley, Jung (works below). Words: paraphrase. Parallels cited: all the traditions below. Tier [M]. Use: state it in one plain paragraph, with three positions: the same individual returns (popular Theosophy, Hindu and Buddhist folk belief), no return to the same state but a continuing passage through other states (Guenon, mainstream Islam and Christianity), the one subject lives all lives (Coomaraswamy, Advaita, the axiom).

2. Guenon rejects reincarnation. Who: Rene Guenon, Le Theosophisme (1921), L'Erreur spirite (1923, English The Spiritist Fallacy), L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta (1925), Les Etats multiples de l'etre (1932). Locator for the specific chapters: unknown. The words, paraphrased: the human bodily state is one state among an indefinite number open to a being; possibility is unlimited and a state once run through is not run through again, because repetition would contradict the infinity of possibility; what a being does is pass to other states. He traced Western reincarnationism to Allan Kardec and the Theosophists and tied it to the modern faith in progress. Parallels he points to: the Vedantic doctrine of states and of liberation beyond all conditioned states. A matching idea exists in Ibn Arabi (no repetition in the divine self-disclosure), and Guenon was a Sufi in later life. Tier [M] for the position and the titles, [U] for the claim that he went as far as saying the Hindu and Buddhist traditions themselves do not teach reincarnation, which I recall but cannot place. Use: he is the strongest voice against "I come back". He does not deny continuation, only return to the same state, and the axiom needs neither. Say that he does not count a Spiritist or Theosophist as a tradition's voice.

3. Coomaraswamy and "the one and only transmigrant". Who: Ananda K. Coomaraswamy, "On the One and Only Transmigrant", printed with "Recollection, Indian and Platonic", Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3 (1944); reprinted in Selected Papers vol. 2, Metaphysics (ed. Roger Lipsey, Bollingen 1977). Tier [M] for the title and year. Words, paraphrased: the Upanishads say the only seer, hearer, thinker and knower is the one Self (Brihadaranyaka 3.7.23 and 3.8.11), so the only one who travels from life to life is that Self, wearing each individual as a vehicle; the personalities do not come back. Parallels he cites: the Self as the thread on which all beings are strung (Brihadaranyaka 3.7.1 to 2, Gita 7.7, the sutratman), the Knower of the field in all fields (Gita 13.2), the two birds on one tree (Rigveda 1.164.20, Mundaka 3.1.1, Katha 1.3.1), Plato's recollection, Eckhart's ground of the soul, and the Gospel command to deny oneself (Matthew 16:24). Tier [M]. Use: this is the axiom in older words, and the thread in Alex's "lives and the thread" picture is the sutratman. Check the exact argument in the paper before quoting.

4. The Buddhist test of Coomaraswamy's reading. Claim: he read the Buddha's anatta as a denial that the aggregates are the Self, not a denial of the Self. Texts at stake: the monk Sati who says one consciousness runs on through rebirths and is rebuked (Majjhima 38); the Buddha's silence when asked if there is a self (Samyutta 44.10); his refusal of the question "who feels?" (Samyutta 12.12). Tier [M] for the texts. Scholars: the mainstream reading (Rahula 1959, Collins 1982, Gombrich) is that early Buddhism denies any abiding self, so Coomaraswamy's reading is a minority one, though a few modern scholars and Thanissaro Bhikkhu argue the silence is strategic [U on details]. Use: the essay's line "no fixed soul, yet something continues" is the safe one. Say that the comparer reads the Buddha's silence as agreement with the Upanishads, and specialists keep the two apart.

5. Coomaraswamy on karma and second birth. Claim: karma is the Sanskrit word for action, and he insisted it is a law of consequence and not fate or reward and punishment; action done without grasping at results frees (Gita 3.9, 4.18). He also read "rebirth" as a second birth inside this life: the twice-born (dvija), initiation, Eckhart's birth of God in the soul and John 3:3. Tier [M] for karma as action, [U] for the second birth claim being a sustained thesis of his. Use: supports the essay's treatment of John 3:3 and the door as birth inside a life. Check Hinduism and Buddhism (1943).

6. Schuon: forms differ, the truth does not. Who: Frithjof Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953), Esoterism as Principle and as Way (1981), In the Tracks of Buddhism (1968), and many other books. Claim, paraphrased: religions disagree at the level of form and agree at the level of essence, and the formal contradictions are providential, each tradition stressing an aspect. The Eastern long cycle and the Western single decisive life can both be read this way: one stresses the gravity of this life, the other the length of the road. Tier [M] for the general principle, [U] for the rebirth application, locator unknown. He agreed with Guenon that modern reincarnationism is wrong. On law and grace he was a close student of Pure Land Buddhism and set it beside Christian and Hindu devotion. Use: Schuon is the best source for how a comparer explains why traditions differ on rebirth. Open his Transcendent Unity before citing.

7. Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt, Pallis, Perry. Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, 1981), Lings (What is Sufism?, 1975; The Book of Certainty, 1952) and Burckhardt (Introduction to Sufi Doctrine, 1959) are Muslims in the Guenon and Schuon line, so they hold the Islamic position: one earthly life, barzakh, resurrection, and rejection of tanasukh. Marco Pallis (A Buddhist Spectrum, 1980) is a Traditionalist and a Buddhist, so he treats rebirth as part of his tradition. Whitall Perry's A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971) is a very large topical anthology from this circle. Tier [M] for the people and titles, [U] for what each says on rebirth and for any entry in Perry on it. Use: this shows that the Traditionalist stance on rebirth follows the author's own religion, which is itself a fair point to make.

8. Huxley. Who: The Perennial Philosophy (1945), the chapter titled Immortality and Survival (number unknown to me, possibly 14), and Time and Eternity. Claim: rebirth is not in his three-part core (a divine Reality in the world, something in the soul like it, and the aim of knowing it). He treats posthumous states as depending on the state of the soul, with the Tibetan bardo, Christian purgatory and Hindu and Buddhist rebirth set side by side. Tier [M] for the core, [U] for his stance in that chapter. The movement is much larger than Huxley, so cite him as one anthologist. Use: Huxley shows that the common core can omit rebirth, which supports saying the traditions are not unanimous.

9. Smith and Wilber. Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976): four levels of reality and of selfhood, from body to Spirit, which gives a vertical map of what continues. Ken Wilber, The Atman Project (1980), Up from Eden (1981), Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995): development as a movement from union through separation back to union, involution then evolution. Tier [M] for the models, [U] for each man's own stand on reincarnation (I think both are cautious and I cannot place a passage). Use: Wilber's Atman project is the nearest modern developmental twin of "we are one, experiencing separation, returning"; his involution and evolution scheme owes much to Aurobindo and to Hegel.

10. The evolutionary reincarnationists. Sri Aurobindo, The Life Divine (1939 to 1940) and The Problem of Rebirth (1952): a psychic being, the evolving soul, takes on a new mind, life and body each time; the personality does not return; there is no fall back into animals; karma is a working of nature that the soul can outgrow. Rudolf Steiner, Theosophy (1904) and later karma lectures, teaches rebirth and a Christ-centred karma. Tier [M] for the broad lines. Use: Aurobindo is the best Indian model for a staged ascent across lives, so he is a parallel for the arrow of consciousness.

11. Theosophy. Who: H. P. Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (1877), The Secret Doctrine (1888), The Key to Theosophy (1889), The Voice of the Silence (1889); A. P. Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (1883); Annie Besant, Reincarnation and Karma (both 1890s). The words, paraphrased: in Isis Unveiled she rejected Kardec's reincarnation and allowed return only in exceptional cases; from the later 1870s and 1880s she and her circle taught reincarnation as the rule. The reincarnating Ego (the individuality) returns, the personality does not; between lives come a purgatory-like kamaloka and a long heaven-state, devachan; karma is the law of cause and effect and of restored harmony, with no judge. The third of the three Proem propositions in The Secret Doctrine reads very close to Alex's axiom: all souls are fundamentally identical with the Universal Over-soul, and every soul must make a pilgrimage through cycles of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law (wording from memory, so paraphrase only). The thread-self (sutratman) is used here too. They also teach that the unity of humanity is the basis of ethics and of karma, and "separateness" is called the great heresy [U on the exact phrase and place]. Like Guenon, they hold that the popular belief in rebirth as animals is a distortion; unlike him, they hold the true teaching is reincarnation. Tier [M] except where marked. Use: quote the third proposition after checking the text; it is public domain.

12. Theosophy's critics. Max Mueller said in 1893 that there is no "esoteric Buddhism" (the Buddha says he kept no closed fist, Digha 16, 2.32). William Emmette Coleman (1895) traced much of The Secret Doctrine to nineteenth century books. The Hodgson Report (1885) judged her phenomena fraudulent, a verdict Harrison's 1986 paper reopened (the notes already list both). Guenon's Le Theosophisme calls it a pseudo-religion. Campbell (1980) and Godwin (1994) are the standard histories. Tier [M]. Use: Alex can cite Theosophy for the comparative project and admit that specialists dispute its sources.

13. Jung. Who: "Concerning Rebirth" (Eranos 1939, in Collected Works 9 part 1); "Psychological Commentary" on Evans-Wentz's Tibetan Book of the Dead (1935, CW 11); Memories, Dreams, Reflections (1962), the chapter on life after death. Claim: the word rebirth covers five different things: metempsychosis, reincarnation proper, resurrection, rebirth as renewal inside one life, and taking part in a rite of transformation. He treats them as psychic facts and does not rule on metaphysics. He read the bardo as a reversed initiation and said the book was a constant companion. In his memoir he wrote, as I recall, that he sometimes felt he was answering questions left open by earlier lives, without claiming it. Tier [M] for the five senses and the commentary, [U] for the memoir passage. Use: Jung gives Alex a clean way to separate the senses of "rebirth" before claiming one.

14. The Greek record. Pythagoras and the soul of the beaten dog (Xenophanes, Diels-Kranz 21 B7); Empedocles' "I have been a boy and a girl, a bush, a bird" (DK 31 B117); Herodotus 2.123 on Egyptian teaching (Egyptologists do not accept it); Pindar, Olympian 2 (three lives in each world to reach the Isles of the Blessed); Orphic gold tablets with the cycle of births and the waters of Memory against Lethe; Plato in Meno 81, Phaedo, Phaedrus 246 to 249, Republic 10, Timaeus 41 to 42, Laws 10 (903 to 905). Plato's key point for karma: in the Myth of Er the blame lies with the chooser and God is blameless (Republic 617e). Caesar, Gallic War 6.14, says the Druids taught that souls pass to other bodies. Whether Greek rebirth came from India is disputed; many scholars say it arose independently. Tier [M]. Use: Myth of Er is the best Western statement that responsibility, not punishment, is the point.

15. Plotinus and the Neoplatonists. Enneads 4.9 asks whether all souls are one, and answers that they are one and many; 3.2 compares souls to actors who change roles; 3.4 covers the allotted spirit; 6.4 and 6.5 treat the one Being present everywhere whole. Plotinus accepted transmigration, including punitive falls, while Porphyry and Iamblichus rejected rebirth into animals, a dispute that looks like the later Theosophist and Guenonian dislike of animal rebirth. Tier [M] on the lines, [U] on exact passages. Use: the oneness of souls and many lives are already together in Plotinus, which is the axiom's pairing.

16. Christianity beyond the essay. Origen (De Principiis; Commentary on Matthew 13.1) taught pre-existence and final restoration and called transmigration foreign to the church; the notes say that the claim that the Council of 553 banned reincarnation is not supported, and Theosophical and anthology writers (Head and Cranston, Reincarnation: The Phoenix Fire Mystery, 1977) repeat it. John 9:2 is quoted by rebirth advocates (was the man blind through his own sin?) and read by scholars as a reflection of Jewish views on prenatal sin. Geddes MacGregor (Reincarnation in Christianity, 1978) argues a Christian case; John Hick (Death and Eternal Life, 1976; Evil and the God of Love, 1966) argues that if the world is a place of soul-making then the task is longer than one life and so continues in other lives or worlds. Tier [M]. Use: Hick makes Alex's "otherwise the whole is a bad designer" argument in academic form.

17. Judaism, gilgul. Appears in the Bahir (12th century), the Zohar, and above all Isaac Luria (1534 to 1572) and Hayyim Vital (Sha'ar ha-Gilgulim): the soul returns to repair a flaw (tikkun), souls have roots in the limbs of the first Adam, and there are special forms (ibbur, dibbuk). Saadia Gaon (Emunot ve-De'ot, 933) argued against transmigration; Menasseh ben Israel wrote on it (1651). Scholem, "Gilgul" in the Encyclopaedia Judaica and in On the Mystical Shape of the Godhead (1991). Tier [M]. Use: the picture of souls as organs of Adam and repair as the purpose of return is the fingers and the thread in Jewish form.

18. Islam. Mainstream theology rejects tanasukh (Avicenna's argument that each soul arises with a fitted body; Quran 23:99 to 100), while the Druze (taqammus: death is followed directly by rebirth as a human), Nusayris, Yarsan and some extremist Shia groups teach it. Stevenson drew many cases from Druze Lebanon. Barzakh (Quran 23:100) is the in-between, often set beside the Tibetan bardo, though one ends in resurrection and the other in rebirth. Ibn Arabi teaches that creation is renewed at every moment and that nothing repeats. Mulla Sadra teaches the soul's substantial movement and development after death without rebirth in this world. Rumi's lines on dying as mineral, plant, animal and man (Masnavi 3, around 3901 to 3906) describe the soul's rise through forms; Chittick and others read them as the arc of ascent and not reincarnation. Tier [M]. Use: the Islamic position is a strong case of "growth continues, the return is dropped".

19. Hindu texts that answer "not enough time". Gita 6.45 says the yogi perfected through many births reaches the goal; 7.19 says the wise one comes to Me at the end of many births; 4.5 has Krishna remembering many births; 8.6 says the last thought at death directs the passage. Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 5 (the caterpillar and the goldsmith: the Self fashions a newer form; as one acts so one becomes), Chandogya 5.10.7 (conduct decides the womb), Yoga Sutra 2.13 (the store of karma bears fruit as birth, lifespan and experience). Tier [M]. Use: Gita 6.45 and 7.19 are direct witnesses for "the run is longer than one life".

20. Hindu texts for the axiom. Gita 13.16 and 18.20: the Reality is undivided in beings and yet appears divided. Gita 15.7: the living soul is an eternal part of Me. Brahma Sutra 2.3.43: the individual is a part (the schools dispute whether a real part, as Ramanuja, or "as if" a part, as Shankara). Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36 faces the objection that a creator who makes unequal lives is cruel and answers with karma and a beginningless cycle. Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 2.32: from the highest standpoint no one is bound, no one is liberated. Ramana Maharshi's habit, as I recall, was to turn the question "who is reborn?" back to "who are you now?" [U on place]. Tier [M] except Ramana. Use: 13.16 is the nearest line in any scripture to "one whole experiencing separation", and 2.32 is the honest answer to the essay's "who carries over?".

21. Buddhist teaching on karma. Intention is karma (Anguttara 6.63). Beings are heirs of their actions (Anguttara 5.57, the five recollections). Short and long analyses of action (Majjhima 135, 136). The Buddha rejects "everything I feel comes from past karma" and lists other causes (Samyutta 36.21), and later tradition gives five orders of natural law, karma being one. At the awakening he gains three knowledges, recall of past lives, the passing of beings by their deeds, and the end of the taints (Majjhima 4, 36). Dhammapada 1 and 2 (the wheel follows the ox), 127 (no spot to escape the result), 165 (purity and impurity depend on oneself). Yogacara adds the store consciousness carrying seeds; Tibetan tradition has recognised lines of rebirth (tulku) from the 13th century. Tier [M]. Use: Samyutta 36.21 supports the essay's warning that blame is the cheap karma.

22. Jain and Sikh. Jain: karma is fine matter that binds the soul through passion; eight kinds; souls are many, eternal and uncreated; liberation is the shedding of karma (Tattvartha Sutra chapter 8, 8.1 on the causes of bondage; 5.21 on souls helping one another). Sikh: rebirth through a long cycle of births, the human birth a rare chance, liberation while living; Japji pauri 4 (Guru Granth Sahib page 2) says the body comes by karma and liberation by grace. Tier [M], page unsure. Use: Jain and Sikh are strong on rebirth and karma but not on one whole (Jain souls are many), so cite them for the lives, not the oneness.

23. Karma in the West and the fingers. Hosea 8:7 (sow the wind, reap the whirlwind), Psalm 7:15 to 16, Matthew 7:2 (measure for measure), Galatians 6:7, Aeschylus (the doer must suffer, Choephori 313), Anaximander (things pay penalty to one another, DK 12 B1), Heraclitus B94, Emerson's essay "Compensation" (1841). The closest match to the fingers is Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Representation vol. 1, section 63 ("eternal justice"): since one will lives in all, tormentor and tormented are the same, and the tormentor suffers what he inflicts. He drew on the Upanishads through Anquetil-Duperron's Latin Oupnek'hat and put the doctrine of rebirth as palingenesis (the will returns, not the memory) against metempsychosis (World as Will vol. 2, chapter 41). Tier [M] on works, [U] on exact section numbers beyond 63. Use: Schopenhauer is public domain in old translations and is the best precedent for the fingers.

24. Law and grace. The traditions that teach consequence also teach a way out that is not payment: Japji 4, Pure Land other-power, Paul's grace (Ephesians 2:8), the Islamic mercy that outruns wrath, the Jain verse of forgiveness. Tier [M]. Use: Alex says repair undoes karma; add that forgiveness is the partner of repair.

# 4. How they compare

The Traditionalists use the exoteric and esoteric split. Differences between religions sit in the outer forms and doctrines; unity sits in the inner or metaphysical level. Guenon tests a doctrine against his own picture of "Tradition", so reincarnation fails and the multiple states pass. Schuon refines it: formal contradictions are intended, each form a refracted whole. The risk is circular: the inner layer is whatever the comparer needs.

Coomaraswamy uses philology. He lines up Sanskrit, Pali, Greek, Latin and Eckhart's German in a single paragraph and treats matching terms as evidence: smrti and anamnesis (recollection), sutratman and the thread, the two birds and the two selves. His strength is real learning, his risk is that translated words differ in context.

The anthologists (Huxley, Perry) cut quotations into topics and set them side by side, which shows resemblance and hides context.

The Theosophists use the wisdom-religion model: one ancient teaching, Senzar stanzas and hidden masters, spreading into all religions, with garbled outer forms. Blavatsky and Guenon share the move of "popular belief is a distortion" and disagree about what the original says.

Smith and Wilber use levels: one map of reality and selfhood with each tradition mapping a part.

Jung uses types of experience: he sorts the senses of rebirth and does not say which is metaphysically true.

Concrete examples of equation: bardo and barzakh (both "between"), atman with Intellect with Spirit with the ruh, karma with measure for measure with sowing and reaping, Lethe with the Myth of Er's river. How they explain disagreement on rebirth: Guenon, deformation plus modern error; Theosophy, garbling of one teaching; Schuon, providential perspectives; Coomaraswamy, a confusion about who the transmigrant is; Hick, a plurality of responses to one Real; Jung, different psychological meanings.

# 5. What scholars say

Mark Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) is the standard history of Traditionalism and notes its sources, its Muslim and anti-modern commitments, the Evola strand with fascist politics, and the later controversies around Schuon. Guenon read Sanskrit sources through informants and never went to India. Indologists reject any claim that Hinduism and Buddhism do not teach rebirth. Katz, Proudfoot and Sharf (already in the notes) reject strong perennialism as hiding the role of language and doctrine; Forman and Hick answer. Coomaraswamy's reading of anatta is a minority view. Donald Lopez (The Tibetan Book of the Dead: A Biography, 2011) shows how the title and framing came from Evans-Wentz. Paul Edwards (1996) is the main critic of reincarnation; Stevenson's cases did not convince the scientific community. Scholem treated gilgul as a late medieval Kabbalistic doctrine with disputed origins. Chittick reads Rumi and Ibn Arabi as ascent and renewal, not reincarnation. Historians of the Origen controversy reject the claim of a council ban on reincarnation.
