## 1. The picture in brief

Both weak points have long Western histories, and the histories are better than the essay's current one-line answers.

On the opening view, the older reply is a logical point and not a counter-fact. That mind and brain rise and fall together shows dependence. It does not show production. William James made this exact in 1898: a brain can produce thought, release it, or transmit it. Schiller (1891), Myers (1903), Bergson (1896 to 1913) and Broad (1925, 1953) restated it, Huxley's reducing valve (1954) made it famous, and Kelly and colleagues (2007) and Kastrup (2014 to 2019) revived it. Neuroscience grants that the brain restricts and denies that a Mind at Large sits behind it. The theory is logically open and has no decisive positive evidence, and that is the honest sentence for Alex.

On plural lives, the Western case is a set of reasons: the unequal start, the unfinished task, the prodigy, love, soul-making, and the symmetry of before and after. Plato supplies most of them. Origen adds pre-existence with a just God. Hume, Lessing, Kant, Goethe and Schopenhauer carry them through the Enlightenment. French Spiritists and the Theosophists turn them into a rule between 1857 and the 1880s. McTaggart, Ducasse, Price, Weatherhead, MacGregor and Hick give the twentieth century versions.

S2 is half right that the dilemma is Lessing's and Hick's. It has earlier homes: Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36, Plato's blameless god, Gita 6.37 to 45 and Kant 1788. It also has a third horn that Alex half states himself: growth continues without return (purgatory, Gregory of Nyssa, Hick's other worlds). The safe form is "the run is longer than one life, and the traditions differ on where the rest happens".

## 2. Findings

### A. Filter and transmission theories

1. James's three functions. Claim: the brain's dependence on mind can be productive (a kettle makes steam), permissive or releasing (a trigger frees a stored force), or transmissive (a lens, prism, stained glass or organ key lets something through). Only the first gives the materialist conclusion. Who: William James, Human Immortality: Two Supposed Objections to the Doctrine, Ingersoll Lecture, Harvard, given 1897, published 1898, second edition with preface 1899; public domain; chapter locator unknown. Words: paraphrase; the images are recalled and need checking. He also cites Shelley's dome of many-coloured glass staining the white radiance of eternity [M, wording unchecked]. Parallels: little religious comparison here; Kant's transcendental self as ancestor; Fechner later in A Pluralistic Universe (1909). Tier: [M]. Use: the cheapest honest fix for the opening view. Quote after checking the text on a public-domain host. It restores logical room and adds no evidence, and the essay should say so.

2. Schiller and Myers, the first English statements. Claim, Schiller: matter limits and confines consciousness and does not generate it, so the body is an instrument of restriction. Who: F. C. S. Schiller, Riddles of the Sphinx (1891), published anonymously as by "a Troglodyte" and revised under his name in 1910 [M]; I do not trust my wording of the limiting sentence, and I think James credited it in his 1899 preface [U]. Claim, Myers: waking life is a selection from a larger "subliminal self" shown by genius, hypnosis, automatism, dreams and mediumship. Who: F. W. H. Myers, Human Personality and Its Survival of Bodily Death, two volumes, 1903, posthumous [M]. Tier: [M]; [U] for what Myers said about reincarnation (I think he left it open). Use: Schiller's "limitation" is close to Alex's "wall". The lineage runs James, Schiller, Myers, Bergson, Broad, then Huxley.

3. Bergson. Claim: the brain selects from a memory that lies beyond it and is the organ of attention to life, so damage blocks recall without destroying memory; the mind overflows the brain; survival is at least possible. Who: Matter and Memory (1896); "Brain and Thought: A Philosophical Illusion" (1904, in Mind-Energy, tr. Carr, 1920); "Soul and Body" (1912). Words: paraphrase; the coat and nail image (pull the nail and the coat falls, yet the nail is not the coat) and the brain as an organ of pantomime are recalled, and which essay holds each is [U]. Tier: [M]. Use: Bergson is the philosopher behind the valve. His claim that the brain stores no memories sits badly with engram research (finding 7), so cite the idea and not that claim.

4. Broad and Huxley's valve. Claim: the brain, nerves and senses mainly eliminate and do not produce; each person could in principle remember everything and perceive everything, and the system cuts this to what is useful for survival. Who: C. D. Broad, "Some Elementary Reflexions on Sense-Perception", Philosophy 27 (1952), reprinted in Religion, Philosophy and Psychical Research (1953); earlier the compound theory (a mind as brain plus a "psychic factor") in The Mind and Its Place in Nature (1925). Aldous Huxley, The Doors of Perception (1954), quotes Broad, takes "Mind at Large" from him, and adds the reducing valve; Broad took the idea from Bergson [M]. Whether Huxley also names Schiller or James is [U]. Huxley equates the mescaline experience with Eckhart's Istigkeit and the Buddhist Dharma-Body [M]. Use: the valve is a Broad-Bergson idea in Huxley's prose.

5. Modern restatements. Claim: Kelly and colleagues present Myers and James as the basis for a mind-first psychology. Who: Edward Kelly and others, Irreducible Mind (2007); Beyond Physicalism (2015) [M]. Claim, Kastrup: one universal mind; individual minds are dissociated parts, like alters in dissociative identity disorder; the brain is what dissociation looks like from outside, closer to an image of a mind than to a filter. Who: Bernardo Kastrup, Why Materialism Is Baloney (2014), Brief Peeks Beyond (2015), The Idea of the World (2019) [M]. Use: Kastrup's picture is the nearest academic neighbour of Alex's axiom. The standard objection is how one mind divides into many, the boundary or decombination problem (Chalmers, "Idealism and the Mind-Body Problem", 2019 [M]). Priority monism (Schaffer, Philosophical Review, 2010 [M]) and cosmopsychism (Shani 2015 [U]) are the analytic names for the axiom.

6. The older forms of the same picture. Plato: the body as tomb or prison (Phaedo 62b, Cratylus 400c). Plotinus: the soul does not wholly descend, a higher part stays above (Enneads IV.8.8). Advaita: limiting adjuncts (upadhi) make the one seem many, as the space in a pot seems cut off from all space (ghatakasa, Gaudapada's Karika, chapter 3). Averroes: one shared intellect for all humans, attacked by Aquinas in De unitate intellectus (1270). Fechner: life before birth, on earth and after death as three stages, as the womb is to the world (Das Büchlein vom Leben nach dem Tode, 1836). All [M]. Use: the pot-space image is the old Indian filter theory and fits the essay's machine. Fechner's stages echo the child in the dark.

7. Objections from neuroscience and philosophy. (a) Dependence is fine-grained: lesions, stimulation, anaesthesia, dementia. Filter theorists reply that a damaged transmitter looks the same, and critics answer that then no brain result could ever count against the theory (Edwards 1996; Augustine, in Martin and Augustine, eds., The Myth of an Afterlife, 2015). (b) Memory: engram work (Liu et al., Nature 2012; Ryan et al., Science 2015) finds cell groups whose activation recalls a memory, even one lost to amnesia, and both sides use it. (c) Dying brains: a gamma surge after cardiac arrest in rats (Borjigin, PNAS 2013), with a human report later [U]. (d) Terminal lucidity in dementia (Nahm and Greyson, 2009) is the filter side's best anomaly, and the cases are retrospective. (e) Physics: Sean Carroll argues that any signal into the brain must come through known fields, which leave no room (2011 blog; journal version U). (f) The radio analogy has a transmitter we can find, and for mind there is none. (g) Disembodied persons are hard to make sense of (Strawson 1959; Flew 1987). Tier [M] unless marked.

8. What neuroscience already grants. Thalamic gating, attention and predictive processing all describe the brain as a reducer of input. Psilocybin lowers activity in some regions (Carhart-Harris and others, PNAS 2012), which filter writers cite, and its own authors read in naturalistic terms (the REBUS model, Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019) [M]. So the valve metaphor holds as a description of the brain, and the extra claim is only that what is reduced is a pre-existing Mind at Large. Tier: [M]. Use: give the reader the two claims separately. The first is easy to grant and the second carries the weight.

### B. The Western case for plural lives

9. Plato. Meno 81: the soul has been born many times and has seen everything, so learning is recollection. Phaedo 70 to 77: opposites come from opposites (the living from the dead), and knowledge before experience shows pre-existence. Phaedrus 245 to 256: ten thousand years for the soul to regain its wings (shorter for the philosopher), and love as recognition of a beauty seen before birth. Timaeus 41e to 42d: the maker gave every soul the same first birth so that none would be disfavoured, and so is blameless for later evil. Republic 617e and 621: the chooser is responsible and god is blameless, and the souls drink forgetfulness. Tier [M]. Use: the oldest "not a bad designer" in the West. The designer is cleared by an equal start, and the unequal present is explained by earlier choices.

10. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa. Origen, De Principiis (c. 220 to 230), argues that the diversity of birth conditions (Greek or barbarian, rich or poor) would make God unjust unless souls pre-existed and earned their places (I.5 to 6, II.9) [M, locators U]. He also speaks of worlds before and after this one (II.3) and rejects transmigration. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380) argues that souls unpurified at death are purified after it, because God's purpose cannot fail (On the Soul and the Resurrection), and taught endless progress, epektasis [M]. The Church defined purgatory at Lyon II (1274) and Florence (1439) [M]. Use: the Western orthodox solution is purification and more time without return, which is the third horn. Do not repeat the Council of 553 story (P16).

11. The Indian receipts for the dilemma. Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36 (the section on partiality and cruelty): God is not partial or cruel, because creation is regulated by the souls' own earlier deeds, and since creation has no beginning the regress is no objection. Shankara's image is rain, a common cause of grain whose kinds differ by seed [M; numbering to within one sutra]. The essay already cites 2.1.33 (lila), so the next three sutras are adjacent. Gita 6.37 to 45: Arjuna asks what becomes of the seeker who fails, and Krishna answers that he is not lost, is born again, regains his earlier practice, and is perfected through many births [M]. Majjhima 135, the Culakammavibhanga Sutta: a young man asks why humans differ in health, looks, wealth and wisdom, and the Buddha answers by action [M]. Weber called the karma doctrine the most consistent theodicy in history [M]. Use: these are the best receipts for the unfinished process and the unequal start. Check the sutra numbers in Thibaut or Gambhirananda.

12. Hume. "Of the Immortality of the Soul" (written about 1755, published 1777) argues against survival from the dependence of mind on body, and says that if the soul is immortal it must have existed before birth, so that metempsychosis is the only scheme of its kind that philosophy could listen to (my paraphrase; check the wording) [M]. Use: a sceptic grants the symmetry argument, so a rational reader may take pre-existence seriously without taking it as proved.

13. Lessing, Education of the Human Race (1780), paragraphs 93 to 100. Claim: revelation educates the species in stages, each person must travel the same road, one life is too short, so each individual may have lived more than once. He calls the idea the oldest and the first that human understanding finds, asks why he should not come back as often as he can gain new knowledge, says forgetting is a mercy, and asks whether all of eternity is not his [M; P01 reported the lines as [V] from education-uk.org]. English: Henry Chadwick, Lessing's Theological Writings (1956) [M]. He published the work as editor, so authorship is sometimes discussed, and most treat it as his thought [M]. Jacobi (1785) reported him saying hen kai pan, the One and All [M]. Use: Lessing gives "time is not wasted" and "forgetting is useful". I do not recall him framing a bad designer, so the negative horn is not his.

14. Kant. In the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), the second part, immortality is a postulate: holiness, the full fit of will to moral law, cannot be reached at any moment of a life in the world, so it needs endless progress and an endless existence of the same person [M]. In Universal Natural History (1755) he speculated that beings on farther planets are more perfect and that human souls might later move through the planetary system [M, details U]. Tier [M]. Use: the "no one finishes in one life, so the process continues" argument, in 1788. The planets of 1755 are a precedent for Alex's arrow mapped on space travel, as are Swedenborg's Earths in the Universe (1758) and Flammarion [M].

15. Herder, Goethe, Schopenhauer, T. H. Huxley. Herder wrote on palingenesis, the ascent of souls (Zerstreute Blatter) [U]. Goethe wrote to Charlotte von Stein in 1776 that she was in earlier times his sister or his wife ("Warum gabst du uns die tiefen Blicke"), told Falk in 1813 that he had been here a thousand times and hoped to come a thousand more, and argued from entelechy and activity to continuance (Eckermann) [M]. Schopenhauer separates metempsychosis (a soul migrates) from palingenesis (the same will appears in a new individual, with no memory and a new intellect), and takes the second as the Buddha's meaning (World as Will, vol. 2, ch. 41; Parerga II, ch. 15) [M; P01 reported the 1844 edition as [V]]. T. H. Huxley (Aldous's grandfather) wrote in Evolution and Ethics (1893) that transmigration, like evolution, has roots in the real world, meaning heredity [M]. Use: Goethe's poem is the best receipt for love and continuity. Schopenhauer's "no memory, same will" is close to the essay's "account and not the session".

16. The French rule and its popularisers. Jean Reynaud (Terre et ciel, 1854) and Pierre Leroux (De l'humanite, 1840) taught progress through many lives on many worlds. Allan Kardec, Le Livre des Esprits (1857; enlarged 1860), made reincarnation the centre of Spiritism and argued from justice, unequal starts, child prodigies and the need for more time; his tomb carries a motto about being born, dying, being reborn and progressing without end. His worlds run from primitive and trial worlds through regeneration to happy and celestial ones (Gospel According to Spiritism, 1864, ch. 3, on John 14:2) [classification U]. Edwin Arnold, The Light of Asia (1879), gave English readers karma as moral law and rebirth as its consequence. Tier [M]. Use: Kardec's ladder of worlds is the nearest nineteenth century match to the arrow mapped on orbit and escape. Never describe it as Eastern.

17. How the Theosophical rule arose. Julie Chajes, Recycled Lives: A History of Reincarnation in Blavatsky's Theosophy (Oxford, 2019), argues that Blavatsky moved from an Isis Unveiled (1877) that rejected Kardec's reincarnation, allowing return only as an exception, to a rule by the early 1880s, in dialogue with Spiritualist and Spiritist progressive reincarnationism, Kingsford and Maitland (The Perfect Way, 1882), Sinnett (Esoteric Buddhism, 1883) and Arnold, and not as an import from India [M for the thesis; details U]. Edward Walker, Reincarnation: A Study of Forgotten Truth (1888), and Head and Cranston (1961, 1967, 1977) gathered the witnesses, with weak history (P16, T04) [M]. Use: say Theosophists adopted reincarnation and argued for it by reason. Do not say they brought it from the East.

18. McTaggart. Some Dogmas of Religion (1906), Human Immortality and Pre-Existence (1915, posthumous), The Nature of Existence (vol. 1, 1921; vol. 2, 1927, edited by Broad) [M]. Claim: if selves are immortal they are pre-existent; one life cannot hold the growth of a self; memory is not needed; love between selves is the bond across lives and the highest value in the universe; love at first sight may mark an earlier tie [M for shape, U for the last point and all locators]. Use: the best philosophical receipt for "love and continuity", from an atheist Hegelian. Check chapter numbers.

19. Ducasse, Price, Broad. C. J. Ducasse, A Critical Examination of the Belief in a Life After Death (1961): no a priori objection, mind as cause and not product, reincarnation as one form of survival [M]. H. H. Price, "Survival and the Idea of 'Another World'" (Proceedings of the SPR, 1953): an after-death world of mental images shared by telepathy [M]; his remarks on reincarnation [U]. Use: mid century philosophers treated survival as a coherent open question.

20. Weatherhead and MacGregor. Leslie Weatherhead (Methodist minister), The Case for Reincarnation (1958) and a chapter in The Christian Agnostic (1965): unequal starts, early death, the unfinished soul, the love of God. Geddes MacGregor (Episcopal priest and philosopher), Reincarnation in Christianity (1978): reincarnation as a purification that fits sanctification better than the one-life view. Tier [M]. Use: Christian reincarnationists make the essay's argument from the Church side.

21. Hick. Evil and the God of Love (1966; revised 1977) and Death and Eternal Life (1976). Claim: a world meant for "soul-making" (the phrase is Keats's, from a letter of 1819, and the type is "Irenaean", after Irenaeus, Against Heresies IV.38) must see most people die unfinished, so a just God needs a continuation; in 1976 he proposed a series of lives in many worlds with a character carried over and memory set aside, ending beyond the individual self [M for the argument; U for chapter locators and the final stage]. Use: the academic form of the bad-designer argument, and the source of "other worlds", the third horn. Open the book before quoting.

22. Stevenson, Tucker, Matlock. Ian Stevenson, Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966), Cases of the Reincarnation Type (four volumes, 1975 to 1983), Reincarnation and Biology (1997); Jim Tucker, Life Before Life (2005); James Matlock, Signs of Reincarnation (2019) [M]. Keil and Tucker (2005) argued for cases with records written before the earlier person was traced [M, journal U]. Stevenson called the carrier a "psychophore" [M]. Use: the essay is right to keep these out. If cited, name the objections (finding 23).

23. The critics. Paul Edwards, Reincarnation: A Critical Examination (1996), and four articles in Free Inquiry (1986 to 1987). His lines: reincarnation needs a separable soul that brain-dependence rules out; memory and character make a person and neither carries; the population explosion (where do the new souls come from); the karma mechanism is arbitrary; the cases are cultural, suggested, selected and checked late. Others: Keith Augustine; Michael Sudduth, A Philosophical Critique of Empirical Arguments for Postmortem Survival (2016); Stephen Braude's "super-psi" (living people's psi as the rival explanation); hypnotic past-life regression is shaped by suggestion (Spanos, 1991). All [M]. Use: the population objection loses force under the axiom, because the number of windows is not the number of souls. That is my reasoning and not a citation.

24. Personal identity. Locke made memory the test of identity, and Edwards uses it. Parfit, Reasons and Persons (1984), argued that what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness and not a further fact of identity, and wrote an appendix on the Buddha's view [M]. Kant's second paralogism (A363) has consciousness handed on between substances like motion between elastic balls [M]. Use: this answers "who carries over" in Western terms and matches the Milindapanha flame.

## 4. How they compare: the method

The filter authors rarely compare religions. They argue a gap in logic with a family of functional images (kettle, trigger, lens, valve, organ key, nail and coat), press with anomalies (mediumship, hypnosis, genius, dying), and then call mystics as witnesses to a wider self: James in the Varieties (the "More"), Huxley on Eckhart and the Buddha, Bergson in 1932 with a ranking that puts Christian action and love on top. The comparing comes last.

The lives authors use six moves. (1) Reason first: justice, development, love, the unfinished task, with traditions as later confirmation (Lessing, Kant, McTaggart, Hick). (2) The oldest-idea argument: Lessing and Hume treat the idea's age and spread as a sign that it fits the human understanding, Schopenhauer calls it the natural belief of Asia, T. H. Huxley ties it to heredity. (3) The catalogue: Walker and Head and Cranston sort short quotations by thinker and religion, which shows spread and hides context. (4) Home reinterpretation: Weatherhead and MacGregor read it into Christian terms (purgatory, sanctification, Elijah and John). (5) The word-equation: Theosophists match devachan with a Pure Land, karma with Nemesis. (6) Stages: Lessing explains the religions as stages in the education of the species, Kardec as grades of worlds, Schopenhauer explains rebirth against migration as truth against myth, and Hick as one Real met through different filters.

## 5. What scholars say

Filter theory: philosophers call it coherent and not forced by any evidence, neuroscientists treat dependence as production, physicists such as Carroll find no room for the extra channel, and a minority (Kelly, Kastrup, Braude) keep it alive. Lessing: read mostly as philosophy of history, with the rebirth paragraphs tentative. The Theosophical rule: Chajes shows a Western and Spiritist history, which cuts against the story of an Eastern import. Stevenson: respected for care, criticised for retrospective testimony and cultural setting; the strongest cases have records made before verification (Keil and Tucker). Hick: the soul-making theodicy is the standard Irenaean version, criticised for the amount of suffering. Edwards: the main sceptical book, itself accused of a hostile reading of Stevenson. For history, Bremmer, The Rise and Fall of the Afterlife (2002), Segal, Life After Death (2004), and Obeyesekere, Imagining Karma (2002), which finds a shared ethical rebirth pattern in Greek and Indian cases [all M].
