# 1. The picture in brief

Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998) is treated by most scholars as the second founder of the Traditionalist school, after Rene Guenon, and the one who turned its comparative method into a body of books. He was a Swiss-born metaphysician and a Sufi sheikh. The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953) is his best known book and sets his method.

The method is top down. He does not gather quotations and look for overlap, which is how Huxley and most anthologists work. He starts from a metaphysical grid (the Absolute and the relative, the Self and its veil), sorts each tradition into an outer layer (exoterism) and an inner layer (esoterism), and says the traditions disagree at the outer layer and converge at the inner one. The unity is "transcendent" because it lies above the forms and cannot be found by averaging them. He rejects the lowest common denominator, and he rejects mixing religions.

His answer to "why do the forms differ" is that the difference is willed. Each form is a relatively absolute window on one Reality, and a window that admitted all the others would stop working as a window.

For Alex he is useful in four ways: the exoteric and esoteric split (Alex's own correction about popular and inner teaching), a worked vocabulary of equivalent terms (Atma, Maya, Beyond-Being, Godhead, Nirguna and Saguna, Tao, Sunyata), an answer to why separation arises at all, and practices that recur across traditions (the Name, discernment, humility). He is a weak witness for lifetimes and karma, his school opposed Theosophy, his order has a record of serious allegations, and critics call his method circular.

6. The Absolute and the relative, Atma and Maya.
Claim: His metaphysics rests on one pair. The Absolute alone is Real. The relative is real only through the Absolute, and is "not it" taken by itself.
Who: Schuon, Language of the Self (1959); Logic and Transcendence; Esoterism as Principle and as Way; Survey of Metaphysics and Esoterism (English 1986). Locators unknown.
The words: paraphrase. In Hindu terms the Absolute is Atma, the Self, and the relative is Maya, read as veil, divine magic and play, neither fully real nor a mere nothing. In his later books he pairs the Absolute with the Infinite and presents Atma and Maya as their Hindu names. Maya has a veiling side and a radiating side. In Sufi terms the world is neither God nor other than God ("He and not He"). He also sets three degrees: Beyond-Being (the Absolute in itself), Being (the Absolute as personal Creator), Existence (the manifested world). Beyond-Being replaces Guenon's Non-Being [U].
Traditions: Hindu (Atma, Maya, Brahman), Islamic (Dhat, "He and not He"), Christian (Eckhart's Godhead), Platonic (the Good beyond being, Republic 509b).
Tier: [M] Atma and Maya, the three degrees, the Sufi formula. [U] the Absolute and Infinite scheme in detail.
Use: the best source in the movement for "the illusion that feels real". Maya is real and not it, which fits the computer picture: a process is real inside the machine and is the machine seen from one corner.

# 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

Schuon deduces and does not gather. The seven steps are my reconstruction from memory, and the order is mine.

One, fix the grid. The Absolute and the relative; Beyond-Being, Being, Existence; discernment and concentration. It comes from Vedanta and Sufi vocabulary and is stated before any tradition is examined.

Two, separate layers inside each tradition: doctrine, law and ritual for the crowd on one side, metaphysical doctrine and method on the other. Compare like with like. Eckhart goes with Shankara and Ibn Arabi, the catechism with the temple cult and the Sunni law. An esoteric text is never set against an exoteric one.

Three, translate. Each doctrinal term is moved to the universal word. Atma, Dhat, Gottheit and Tao are four names of one thing at the inner level. Ishvara, Allah the Lord and God the Father are three names of another.

Four, explain differences by emphasis. Each form brings out one face of the Real and closes the others on purpose. As I recall, Islam stresses the Absolute and the Intellect, Christianity the Infinite as love and Incarnation, Buddhism the Void and compassion. [U] on where he says so.

Five, explain exclusivism as function. A form claims to be the only truth because it must save a whole people. The inner layer relaxes the claim.

Six, exclude counterfeits (syncretism, Theosophy, New Age eclecticism). Only forms with a revealed origin, a lineage and a method count.

Seven, forbid mixing. The unity is seen from inside one form, never from a collage.

Three worked examples, [M] in gist.

The Shahada. "There is no god but God" has two halves: discernment (nothing is real except the Real) and concentration on the Real. It parallels Vedanta's neti neti followed by identity with Atma.

Self-naming. "I am that I am" is set beside "I am Brahman" (Aham Brahmasmi) and beside Eckhart's saying that the eye with which God sees me is the eye with which I see him. [U] whether Schuon puts all three together; the pairing is a typical move of his school.

The Name. The Hindu mantra, the Sufi dhikr, the hesychast Jesus Prayer and the Pure Land nembutsu are set side by side as one operation: concentration on the Real through its Name.

Contrast with the anthologists. Huxley collects short passages by theme, and the unity shows in how alike they sound. Schuon treats resemblance as the result of one Reality and one Intellect, and explains why it is hidden at the level of form. Huxley's method is closer to Alex's essay of examples. Schuon supplies the reason the examples differ.

# 5. What scholars say, short

Admirers (Nasr, Smith, Oldmeadow, Laude, Shah-Kazemi) say Schuon gave the clearest account of how a person can stay loyal to one religion and understand the others. They say the unity is one of the Intellect and not of experience, which shields it from the usual objection to "common mystical experience" theories.

Critics make four points. Circularity: the grid decides what counts as inner. Bias: the grid is Vedanta and Sufism, and traditions that do not share it (Theravada, Confucian, most Jewish and Christian practice) are read through it. Ahistory: scholarship on origins and context is treated as beside the point. Politics and person: the school distrusts modernity, and the order has faced serious allegations (Sedgwick covers the record). Katz's 1978 argument that mystical experience is shaped by its tradition is aimed at experience-based perennialism and is cited against the whole family. Hick's pluralism has a distinction near Schuon's (the Real and its personal and impersonal forms) without the esoteric hierarchy. [M] outline, [U] who said exactly what.

Inside the school the sharpest dispute was Guenon against Schuon, about 1948 to 1950, over Christianity, the Alawi order and Schuon's authority (P02 reports it).
