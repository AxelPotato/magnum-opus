## 1. The picture in brief

Theosophy built the largest numbered map of stages in the modern West, and it scales outward in three layers. The first is cosmic time: a Great Breath, with universes that appear and vanish through manvantara and pralaya, so that even a universe is an episode. The second is a stack of scales of being: person, planet, sun, cosmos, each with its own conscious Life (a planetary Logos, a solar Logos, a cosmic level above), each body made of the lives of the level below. The third is the Path: a person climbs from probation and discipleship through initiations to the Master, who faces a choice between private bliss and staying to help.

The sources layer, and the layering matters. Blavatsky (1888 to 1891) supplies the Breath, the cycles, the chain of globes and rounds, the Dhyani hierarchy, three Logoi, the Voice of the Silence and the Great Renunciation, building on Sinnett and the Mahatma Letters (1880 to 1885). Besant and Leadbeater (1895 to 1925) supply the named seven planes, the planetary and solar Logos hierarchy, the five initiations with Pali, Hindu and Christian labels, and the clairvoyant root-race history. Bailey (1922 to 1949) supplies nine initiations, the cosmic planes and the paths beyond the Master.

The logic fits Alex's axiom closely: the whole goes down into separation, turns, and returns with something gained. The numbers do not fit his arrow. Theosophy has no speed, no galaxy rung and no minus four to plus four. The mapping to run, bike, car, fly, orbit and escape velocity is his, and the essay should say so. The system also carries root races, which scholars treat as its most problematic part. The essay should leave them out.

## 2. Findings

A. Blavatsky's cosmos, and what was added

1. The frame: three propositions and the pilgrimage
Claim: The Proem of The Secret Doctrine sets out one boundless Principle, universes that appear and disappear in cycles, and the identity of every soul with the Over-Soul, with an obligatory pilgrimage through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. She adds that the divine spark must pass through every form of a manvantara and win individuality by its own effort, rising from mineral and plant to the highest archangel (Dhyani-Buddha), with no privileges.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, Proem (1888). Locator of the ascent passage: early Proem, page unknown. T02 covers the Proem.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels she names: Brahmanical (Days and Nights of Brahma), Buddhist, Kabbalistic.
Tier: [M] for content; exact wording of the ascent sentence [U].
Use: one growth path from the lowest form to the highest being, no outside grace. It sits well beside "the whole experiencing itself through individual lives."

2. The Great Breath, manvantara and pralaya
Claim: She calls the periodic out-breath and in-breath of the unmanifest the Great Breath. A manvantara is a period of manifestation, a pralaya a period of rest, and she applies both at every scale: a round, a chain, a solar system, a universe. The numbers are Puranic. A day of Brahma (kalpa) is 1,000 mahayugas, 4.32 billion years. A manvantara is 71 mahayugas, about 306.7 million years; fourteen plus their junctions fill a kalpa (14 x 71 + 6 = 1,000). The life of Brahma is 100 divine years, 311.04 trillion years. The Puranas list four dissolutions: occasional (naimittika), elemental (prakritika), final (atyantika, individual liberation) and continual (nitya).
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, Proem and Stanzas I to III (the Eternal Parent sleeping seven eternities, the lotus bud swelling). The Puranic scheme: Vishnu Purana and Bhagavata Purana, which she read in Wilson's English version. Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu cosmology chiefly; Buddhist kalpas.
Tier: [M] for numbers and the four dissolutions; Stanza wording [U].
Use: a safe source for "even plus four may be a stage." A whole universe is one breath, followed by another. Alex's multiverse sentence has an older precedent, in time rather than space.

3. The Stanzas of Dzyan and the book's structure
Claim: Volume 1 (Cosmogenesis) comments on seven Stanzas she says are translated from the Book of Dzyan in a priestly language, Senzar. Volume 2 (Anthropogenesis) comments on twelve more. The first Stanzas cover the sleep and waking of the One and the swelling of the lotus; later ones bring in the Sons of Dhyan, Fohat (cosmic energy), the Seven and the building of the planet and its chain. A third volume (1897) was assembled by Besant from her papers.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine (London, 1888, two volumes).
Words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for structure; [U] for Stanza titles and the 1897 detail.
Use: say "the book Blavatsky says she translated, which no one has found."

4. Descent and ascent: the chain of globes and the Rounds (the axiom in cosmic form)
Claim: The human Monad (Atma with Buddhi) travels a chain of seven globes (A to G); one circuit is a Round. Globes A, B, C are the arc of descent (spirit into matter), D, our Earth, is the lowest point, and E, F, G the arc of ascent. There are seven Rounds, and we are in the fourth, past its midpoint, the turning point. The return is not a repeat of the start: the being comes back a conscious god where it began an unconscious one. Atma is universal and not anyone's possession, so the separateness is in the vehicles.
Who: Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (1883), drawing on the Mahatma Letters (1880 to 1884, published 1923), first published the scheme. Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, widens it. Atma as universal: The Key to Theosophy (1889), locator unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu going out and returning (pravritti, nivritti). Neoplatonic procession and return (Plotinus, Proclus) is a comparer's pairing, not hers.
Tier: [M] for scheme and attribution.
Use: the nearest nineteenth-century cosmic version of Alex's three clauses: one whole, separation, return. Return with consciousness gained answers "why separate at all."

5. Planes and principles: hers and the later list
Claim: The septenary man and seven planes entered Theosophy in 1881 to 1883 through Sinnett and the Mahatma Letters. Isis Unveiled (1877) had a threefold man. In The Secret Doctrine she uses seven principles and planes with cautions that they are states, not places. The standard named list (physical, astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic or anupadaka, logoic or adi) is Besant's and Leadbeater's systematisation. The buddhic plane is called the plane of unity, where the sense of separation drops away.
Who: Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897); Leadbeater, Man Visible and Invisible (1902) and A Textbook of Theosophy (1912). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels the comparers draw: Vedantic sheaths (five koshas), lokas, the four worlds of Kabbalah and of Sufism, Neoplatonic hypostases. Guenon (1921, 1925) argued Theosophy misread the sheaths.
Tier: [M] for the list and attribution; "cautions" [U].
Use: say "the Theosophical list of planes," not "Blavatsky's." In Theosophical terms the plane of unity is where Alex's axiom is lived: the sense of "another" is what goes.

6. Root races and sub-races
Claim: Volume 2 teaches seven root races per globe period, each with seven sub-races. The first two are ethereal, the Third (Lemurian) gets mind and solid bodies, the Fourth (Atlantean) is the peak of materiality, the Fifth (Aryan) is ours, two more follow. Later writers made it concrete: Scott-Elliot (The Story of Atlantis, 1896; The Lost Lemuria, 1904) mapped continents; Besant and Leadbeater (Man: Whence, How and Whither, 1913) gave a clairvoyant history and a coming sixth sub-race.
Problem: the scheme ranks peoples. Blavatsky treats some living peoples as degenerate remnants fated to die out and puts the Aryan highest among the living. Defenders say root races are stages every soul passes through, and note the Society's first object, brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour. Critics say the hierarchy stands in the text regardless.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 2; Scott-Elliot; Besant and Leadbeater. Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu yugas, Hesiod's ages (loosely).
Tier: [M] for content and debate; every locator [U].
Use: leave it out. If Theosophy is introduced as a stage-mapper, one sentence naming this as the part scholars find hardest to defend would be honest.

7. Hierarchies and Logoi: Blavatsky, then Besant and Leadbeater
Claim: Between the Absolute and humanity Blavatsky places Dhyan-Chohans, planetary spirits and builders, who work by law and are not personal gods; some are said to be perfected humanity of earlier cycles. She uses "Logos" at three levels: unmanifest, the Spirit-Matter pair, and manifested universal Mind. The Vedantic scaffolding (Parabrahman, Mulaprakriti, Logos, Pranava) came from T. Subba Row's 1886 Bhagavad Gita lectures. Besant and Leadbeater then built tiers: a Solar Logos (the conscious life of a solar system); seven Planetary Logoi, read into the "seven spirits before the throne" of Revelation; for Earth, a Lord of the World (Sanat Kumara, the Ancient of Days) heading a Hierarchy of Manu, Bodhisattva or World Teacher, Mahachohan, the ray Chohans and the Masters; and a cosmic level left in shadow.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1; Subba Row (1886); Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897); Leadbeater, A Textbook of Theosophy (1912) and The Masters and the Path (1925). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Greek Logos (Heraclitus, Stoics, Philo, John 1), Hindu Ishvara and Om, Daniel 7, Revelation 1:4, Hindu Sanat Kumara (Chandogya Upanishad 7, number [U]).
Tier: [M] for structure and sources; "perfected humanity of earlier cycles" as her own wording [U]; whether Sanat Kumara equals the Planetary Logos [U] (the authors differ).
Use: this is the planetary, solar, cosmic ladder in plain form, and it is mostly the later layer. Do not write "she says" for the tiers.

8. Bailey: the Heavenly Man and the cosmic planes
Claim: Alice Bailey (1880 to 1949), writing for "the Tibetan" (Djwhal Khul), held that a planet's Logos is a "Heavenly Man" whose cells are humans, and that our seven planes are the seven sub-planes of one cosmic physical plane, with six higher cosmic planes above (cosmic astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic, logoic). The pattern repeats at each scale. The Solar Logos draws on stars such as Sirius, the Pleiades and the Great Bear.
Who: Bailey, A Treatise on Cosmic Fire (1925); Initiation, Human and Solar (1922). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Kabbalah (Adam Kadmon, also in The Secret Doctrine), Hermetic "as above, so below."
Tier: [M] for structure; star details [U]. Critics have flagged passages about Jews in her texts [U]: check before any citation.
Use: the strongest statement I know of a self-similar ladder, each scale's whole being one plane of a larger scale. It resembles Alex's small square inside a 1,000 by 1,000 map. The likeness is structural only.

B. The Path

9. Probation, chelas and the four qualifications
Claim: The 1880s teaching spoke of probationary, accepted and lay chelas and warned that a pupil is tested long and there are no shortcuts (Blavatsky, "Chelas and Lay Chelas," 1883). Besant formalised a Probationary Path and a Path of Discipleship. At the Feet of the Master (1910) gave four qualifications: discrimination, desirelessness, good conduct (six sub-qualities) and love. The first three adapt the Vedantic four means (viveka, vairagya, shatsampatti, mumukshutva, from the Vivekachudamani), with love replacing the desire for release.
Who: as named; Besant, The Path of Discipleship (c. 1896). At the Feet of the Master is credited to "Alcyone" (the young J. Krishnamurti) with Leadbeater's help; authorship is disputed.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Advaita (viveka, vairagya).
Tier: [M] for books and qualifications; Besant's date and the authorship [U].
Use: "desirelessness" at the probationary stage is Theosophy's own name for what Alex calls letting go.

10. The Voice of the Silence: Three Halls and Seven Portals
Claim: The Voice (1889), which she says she translated from The Book of the Golden Precepts, has three fragments. In the first the pupil crosses three Halls: Ignorance (avidya), Learning (a serpent under every flower), and Wisdom, beyond which lies the shoreless ocean of the imperishable; the text speaks of passing through three states into a fourth and thence into seven worlds. In the third he enters seven portals, each a virtue: dana (giving), shila (harmony in word and act), kshanti (patience), viraga (indifference to pleasure and pain), virya (dauntless energy), dhyana (meditation), prajna (wisdom, which makes a Bodhisattva). The Mahayana has six perfections; the Voice inserts viraga as the fourth.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (1889), Fragments I and III. T03 treats the text.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Mahayana paramitas.
Tier: [M] for the portals and the Halls' order; exact wording [U].
Use: a seven-step map whose fourth step is indifference to pleasure and pain is the nearest older text to letting go as the hinge of the path.

11. The Two Paths: Great Renunciation and Nirmanakaya
Claim: Fragment II sets a choice at the end. One way is private liberation into nirvana (Dharmakaya), tied to the Pratyeka-Buddha, whom she paints as self-regarding. The other is the "Great Renunciation": the adept who could enter nirvana declines, keeps a Nirmanakaya ("transformation body") and stays near humanity, adding to a protective "Guardian Wall." The voice of compassion asks whether there can be bliss while all that lives must suffer. The Theosophical Glossary (1892, ed. Mead) defines the Nirmanakaya this way.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence, Fragment II; The Theosophical Glossary.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels she cites: the Mahayana bodhisattva vow (Shantideva), and the Buddha's "Great Renunciation" (leaving the palace) reused with a new meaning.
Tier: [M].
Scholars: in Buddhist usage nirmanakaya is a Buddha's visible emanation body (Shakyamuni is one), not an adept who stays on, and the Pali pratyekabuddha is a solitary awakened one, not a villain. Her usage filters Mahayana polemic through Victorian writing such as Edwin Arnold's The Light of Asia (1879).
Use: a fork at the top, not an end. It fits the hand-back in Alex's voice and answers "what comes after the last rung" with return. Do not call it "birth."

12. The five initiations: Besant and Leadbeater
Claim: The Path proper has a Probationary stage, Accepted Discipleship under a Master, then five initiations to the Adept (Asekha, the Master). Labels come from three traditions. Buddhist: Sotapanna, Sakadagamin, Anagamin, Arhat, Asekha ("beyond training"). Hindu, as I recall: Parivrajaka, Kutichaka, Bahudaka, Hamsa, Paramahamsa. Christian: Birth, Baptism, Transfiguration, Crucifixion (renunciation), Resurrection and Ascension. Reading the Gospels as an initiation map goes back at least to Kingsford and Maitland (The Perfect Way, 1881) and was taken up by Besant (Esoteric Christianity, 1901). The Buddhist fetters supply content: the first initiation drops the belief in a permanent separate self, doubt and ritualism; the second weakens desire and aversion. The first initiation is said to touch buddhic consciousness, the plane of unity.
Who: Besant, Initiation: The Perfecting of Man (1923); Leadbeater, The Masters and the Path (1925). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: as listed. In the Pali Nikayas an arahant is an asekha, so splitting "arhat" and "asekha" into two stages is a Theosophical addition.
Tier: [M] for structure, Pali list and Christian pairing; Hindu names and the early dates [U].
Use: Alex's essay says early Buddhism has four stages. The Theosophical five is an edit of those four. If he cites five, he should say who made them five.

13. Bailey's nine initiations and "birth"
Claim: Bailey keeps the Christian pattern and extends it: Birth, Baptism, Transfiguration, Renunciation (crucifixion), Revelation (resurrection, the Master); then I recall Decision (sixth), Resurrection (seventh), Transition (eighth), Refusal (ninth). After the fifth the Master chooses among seven Paths of Higher Evolution: Earth service, magnetic work, training for Planetary Logos, the path to Sirius, the Ray path, the path of our Logos, absolute sonship. I also recall Leadbeater listing seven choices for the Adept, including merging into the Logos and staying in the Nirmanakaya state.
Who: Bailey, Initiation, Human and Solar (1922); A Treatise on Cosmic Fire (1925); The Rays and the Initiations (1960). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for the first four names and the idea of seven paths; [U] for labels five to nine and the exact path titles.
Collision: in Bailey and in Besant and Leadbeater "Birth" is the first initiation, at the start of the Path proper. In Alex's school "birth" is the stage after plus four. Both use the second-birth image for a threshold, but not the same one. Do not say Theosophy "calls it birth."
Use: the fork after the Master (Decision, seven Paths) is a second precedent for "what comes after the top is a choice."

14. Planetary to solar to cosmic as a path
Claim: In Blavatsky the pilgrimage ends, over later cycles, at the Dhyani level, and what lies above is unknowable. In Besant and Leadbeater the human Monad grows into a Master, then superhuman ranks, and in far cycles toward Planetary and Solar Logos. In Bailey the Path is explicit: human initiations (one to five), planetary and higher (six on), solar, cosmic. A planet is a Logos's body, a solar system a larger one's.
Who: as above; Bailey is the explicit source.
Words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for the shape; [U] for how Bailey divides initiations between planetary and solar.
Use: the same size order as Alex's, at a different place. In his arrow the Earth is the small square and escape velocity at plus one leaves it. In Theosophy the Earth's Logos stands above the Master. They do not map one to one, and the essay should not claim they do.

15. Krishnamurti's refusal
Claim: Leadbeater found Krishnamurti in 1909, and Besant and Leadbeater proclaimed him vehicle of the coming World Teacher. On 3 August 1929 at Ommen he dissolved the Order of the Star in the East built around him, saying truth is a pathless land that no organisation, creed or gradual stage can reach. He left the Theosophical Society in 1930.
Words: paraphrase; the "pathless land" wording is well known but unverified here.
Tier: [M].
Use: the best internal objection to stage maps, from the person they were built for. It fits Alex's "treat the labels as labels."

## 4. How they compare: the method

Blavatsky uses five moves. All are [M].

First, genealogy. All religions descend from one archaic teaching kept by initiates. Agreement is inheritance, difference is loss: symbols read literally, keys lost, priestcraft. Her Preface says she brings only the string that ties a nosegay of flowers gathered by others (a line she takes from Montaigne). Alex can borrow the stance: he gathers and does not claim to discover.

Second, the vocabulary list. She sets terms side by side across languages: Atma, Buddhi, Manas with Greek and Hebrew counterparts; Logos with Ishvara; Nirvana with Moksha. The Glossary is that table alphabetised.

Third, the template. She and her heirs impose a sevenfold shape on every source: five Hindu sheaths become seven principles, six Mahayana perfections become seven portals, Puranic cycles become seven Rounds. Here comparers and scholars object most: the fit comes from the template, and the sources are bent to it.

Fourth, one label per tradition for each rung. Besant and Leadbeater write one initiation as Sotapanna, Parivrajaka and Birth at once. The reader feels three religions agree, though the three lists differ in length and meaning and the Theosophists cut and joined them.

Fifth, exoteric against esoteric: the popular form hides the inner form, so apparent contradictions are veils. Later writers add claimed observation, clairvoyant investigation (Besant and Leadbeater, Occult Chemistry), which turns a claim into a report that cannot be checked from outside.

For Alex the contrast is plain. He claims no original, no template and no clairvoyant source. He starts from one axiom and shows which parts of each tradition follow. That is closer to the Perennialists who argue from metaphysics than to the later Theosophists who report from observation.

## 5. What scholars say

- Sources of The Secret Doctrine: William Emmette Coleman (1895) traced large parts to printed works of her day (Wilson's Vishnu Purana, Winchell's World-Life, Donnelly's Atlantis, Jacolliot). No Book of Dzyan has been found. Godwin (1994) and Hanegraaff (1996) read her system as a nineteenth-century creation from Western esotericism, Romantic nature philosophy, evolutionism and Orientalist readings of India, not a transmission. [M]
- Historical weight: Campbell (1980) and Hammer (2001) take Theosophy as a main source of modern ideas of karma, reincarnation, ascended masters and the New Age, whatever one judges true. Chajes (2019) shows Blavatsky's own view of reincarnation shifting between 1877 and the 1880s. [M]
- Buddhism: Prothero (1996) and Lopez (1998) show Theosophical Buddhism as a Victorian composite, with Tibet and the Mahatmas partly a Western fantasy. [M]
- Root races: Goodrick-Clarke (1985) shows the scheme taken into racist Ariosophy (List, Lanz von Liebenfels). Historians debate how far this implicates Theosophy itself; none says Theosophy is Nazi. Ramaswamy (2004) follows Lemuria from a zoologist's land bridge of 1864 (Sclater) through Haeckel to Theosophy; plate tectonics removed the geological basis. [M]
- Traditionalists: Sedgwick (2004) reports that Guenon attacked Theosophy (Le Theosophisme, 1921; L'Erreur spirite, 1923) as a pseudo-religion mixing the psychic with the spiritual. The perennialists most respected in the field were Theosophy's sharpest critics. [M]
- Splits: Theosophists loyal to Blavatsky and Judge (the United Lodge, Pasadena) call the Besant and Leadbeater layer "Neo-Theosophy." De Zirkoff's Collected Writings (1950 to 1991) is the standard text for separating her words from later ones. Tillett (1982) is the standard Leadbeater biography, including the 1906 scandal; Hanegraaff treats Bailey as the main bridge to the New Age. [M]
