## 1. The picture in brief

After Blavatsky died in 1891 her movement split into four kinds of writer, and they compare religions in four different ways.

The colleagues who kept her words (Judge, then Tingley at Point Loma, Crosbie's United Lodge, Purucker) wrote plain handbooks of her system. They compare by repeating her equivalences and by stressing karma and rebirth.

The Adyar school (Besant, Leadbeater, Jinarajadasa, Hodson) added claimed clairvoyant observation as a second witness, and ranked maps of the path (probation, five initiations). They lay one list of terms over each religion and read each religion as the public face of an inner teaching.

The scholars and translators (Olcott on Buddhism, Subba Row on Vedanta, Mead on Gnostic and Hermetic texts) compare most checkably, because they work from named texts.

The heirs and dissenters (Steiner, Bailey, Krishnamurti) kept the frame and changed the centre: Christ, the Hierarchy, or nothing at all.

All of them share one frame. There is one boundless source. Each soul is a pilgrim who descends into matter, lives many lives under a law of consequence, and returns. That is close to the three clauses of Alex's axiom (one whole, it experiences separation, the separation ends). It is the nearest precedent in English for his structure.

Three cautions. Much of the Adyar layer rests on observation nobody can repeat. Specialists keep apart what these writers equate. The perennialist school (Guenon) attacked Theosophy, Besant's wing above all, so presenting both as one family would be wrong.

The later writers treat these as the creed.
Who: Blavatsky, Secret Doctrine, Proem; followers' restatements, locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: the comparers say every religion carries this pattern.

2. Judge, The Ocean of Theosophy (1893)
Claim: William Quan Judge (1851 to 1896), Irish-born New York lawyer and founder-member of 1875, wrote the briefest orderly summary of Blavatsky's system. It runs from the one source, through the seven principles of man, to death, a period of shedding desire (kama-loka), a long rest of assimilation (devachan), and rebirth. Karma is ethical causation and adjustment, not a verdict. What carries over is the reincarnating ego, not the personality. He argues for rebirth from unequal births, child prodigies and continuity of character.
Who: Judge, Ocean of Theosophy, 1893. Chapter order unknown to me.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Pythagoras, Plato, Origen, the Gospels' Elijah and John the Baptist, Hindu texts (his exact list is [U]).
Tier: [M] for author, year, outline. [U] for chapter order.
Use: a clear answer to who carries over. The devachan rest is the nearest nineteenth century picture to Alex's saved state between runs.

3. Judge as editor and letter-writer

His Letters That Have Helped Me (first part 1891) give students personal advice on inner work: act from duty, drop the grip on results, work on the lower self.

4. Olcott: the Buddhist Catechism (1881) and the Fourteen Propositions (1891)
Claim: Henry Steel Olcott (1832 to 1907) wrote the Buddhist Catechism in question-and-answer form in Colombo in 1881, as an answer to missionary catechisms. The monk Sumangala endorsed it, and it went through many editions and languages. It presents rebirth by karma without a permanent soul. In 1891 Olcott drafted fourteen propositions meant to be common to Southern and Northern Buddhists and had Sinhalese and Japanese authorities accept them. This is the only comparison in this slice that was shown to the people compared.
Who: Olcott, Buddhist Catechism, 1881; Old Diary Leaves (six series, 1895 to 1935).
Parallels: Theravada and Mahayana, set side by side by negotiation.
Tier: [M]. [U] for the number of editions and which edition added the propositions.
Use: a model of honest common ground: a short list each side would sign. A good test for the wording of "the separation ends".

5. Olcott's Buddhism against Theosophy on the soul
Claim: Buddhist teaching denies a permanent self. Theosophy teaches a reincarnating individuality. Olcott said the two agree, because what continues is a set of consequences. Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism (1883), a Theosophical system under a Buddhist name, was disliked by Buddhists and by Orientalists (Rhys Davids and Max Mueller wrote against it, I believe, in the 1880s and 1893).
Tier: [M] for the tension. [U] for the critics.
Use: the comparer equates rebirth with a soul and without one. Part two already keeps them apart. Keep doing so.

6. Subba Row: Parabrahman, the veil, the thread
Claim: T. Subba Row (1856 to 1890), a Madras lawyer and Advaitin close to Blavatsky, wrote the most Indian account in the circle. He set out Parabrahman as the ground, Mulaprakriti ("root nature") as its veil, and the Logos as the first manifest. He held that the "principles" are limiting conditions (upadhis), as in Advaita, and preferred a smaller count than seven. He lectured on the Gita in 1885 and 1886 as a manual of the soul's growth.
Who: Subba Row, articles in The Theosophist (1881 to 1887); Notes on the Bhagavad Gita (book date unknown); Esoteric Writings (1895).
Parallels: Advaita (upadhi); the Logos of John 1, which he equates.
Tier: [M] for the man and the veil idea. [U] for details, and for his role in bringing in the "thread-soul" (sutratman, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7, as I recall).
Use: the one and its veil in an Indian voice. The thread fits Alex's lives and the thread figure. Check the Upanishad locator in Olivelle.

7. Besant, Four Great Religions (1897)

Her explanation of difference: each Great Teacher gives the same truth in the form suited to a people and an age.
Who: Besant, Four Great Religions, 1897. The venue of the lectures is [U] (an Adyar convention, 1895 or 1896).

8. Besant, Esoteric Christianity, or the Lesser Mysteries (1901)
Claim: Christianity has an outer creed and an inner teaching, the lesser mysteries. The Trinity, Incarnation, Atonement and sacraments are read as statements about the Logos, who limits himself in matter, and about the Christ born inside the soul. The gospel life is read as the path of a soul. Clement and Origen knew the inner meaning. Early Christianity taught rebirth and lost it.
Who: Besant, 1901. Anna Kingsford and Edward Maitland's The Perfect Way (1882) is the earlier model.
Parallels: Hindu trimurti, Egyptian triads, Greek mysteries, Paul's wisdom for the "perfect" (1 Cor 2).
Tier: [M] for the thesis. [U] for the chapter list and for which gospel episode maps to which stage.
Use: her reading of the Atonement as the one limiting itself in form is close to Alex's reason why the whole would separate. Name it as Besant's reading.

9. Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897)
Claim: The long manual of the Adyar school: planes of nature, the constitution of man, after-death states, rebirth, karma, the path of discipleship. Spirit descends into matter, reaches a low point of density, and climbs back through the human kingdom to higher ranks. Part of her detail rests on Leadbeater's observation.
Tier: [M] for the shape. [U] for chapter order.

Who: A Study in Consciousness (1904, subtitle "A Contribution to the Science of Psychology"); The Self and Its Sheaths (1895, short lectures).
Parallels: Advaita koshas.
Tier: [M] for titles. [U] for the second's date and for what the first says on the sense of separateness.

11. Besant on karma, and who administers it
Claim: Besant's Karma (1895) probably uses the Hindu three-way division (accumulated, ripe, now being made). Blavatsky's karma has no judge. In later Adyar writing the Lords of Karma (earlier "Lipika", recorders) appear as beings who allot a person's lot.
Tier: [M] that the Lords of Karma exist in later Theosophy. [U] for the three-way division in Besant's book.

12. Leadbeater: clairvoyant report in place of comparison
Claim: Charles Webster Leadbeater (1854 to 1934), former Anglican curate, wrote The Astral Plane (1895), The Devachanic Plane (about 1896), Man Visible and Invisible (1902), The Other Side of Death (1904), A Textbook of Theosophy (1912), The Masters and the Path (1925). His method is observation by trained clairvoyance. He describes after-death states in detail and labels them with the world's terms: devachan is his heaven-world, matched with the Pure Land and Christian heaven; the lower astral regions are matched with hell and purgatory.
Parallels: devachan with Sukhavati (the Tibetan name bde-ba-can translates it).
Tier: [M] for titles and method. [U] for dates and specific equations.
Use: not as evidence. As a witness to the equation habit.

13. At the Feet of the Master (1910) and the stage maps
Claim: Credited to "Alcyone" (the teenage Krishnamurti), with Leadbeater's hand disputed. It sets four qualifications for the path: discrimination, desirelessness, good conduct (six sub-qualities such as control of mind and action, tolerance, one-pointedness and confidence) and love. The first three match the Advaita list of four means (viveka, vairagya, the six disciplines, longing for freedom), with love in the last place. Besant and Leadbeater also equate the four Buddhist stages (stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arhat) with the first four initiations.
Who: At the Feet of the Master, 1910; Besant, Initiation: The Perfecting of Man (1923); Leadbeater, The Masters and the Path (1925).
Tier: [M] for the four qualifications and the general equation. [U] for authorship and for which book makes which mapping.
Use: "desirelessness" is a Theosophical name for letting go. The comparers equate. Buddhist scholars define the four stages by which fetters are cut, not by initiations. Say so if used.

14. Mead: Fragments of a Faith Forgotten (1900), Pistis Sophia (1896), the subtle body (1919)
Claim: George Robert Stow Mead (1863 to 1933), Blavatsky's secretary, argued that the Gnostics were not heretics but the thinking half of early Christianity, a school of Gnosis (wisdom). He sets out the Valentinian myth: the Fullness (pleroma) is complete, its last aeon Sophia falls through wanting to know the Father, a Limit (horos) fixes the boundary, and a redeemer gathers what fell and restores it. Pistis Sophia (his 1896 translation) has souls reborn in new bodies until they know. In The Doctrine of the Subtle Body in Western Tradition (1919) he traces the soul's "vehicle" (Greek ochema) in Platonism and the Fathers. He left the Society in 1909 over the Leadbeater affair and founded the Quest Society and its quarterly.
Parallels: Gnostic, Platonic.
Tier: [M] for books and the broad myth. [U] for his text base (I believe a nineteenth century Latin rendering).
Use: the Valentinian myth is an ancient order of one, wall, return. The Apocryphon of John (found 1945, Nag Hammadi, section [U]) also has souls cast into another body until they awaken, which backs the Theosophical claim that some Gnostic texts teach rebirth.

15. Mead, Thrice-Greatest Hermes (1906): the descent and the second birth
Claim: Three volumes, "Studies in Hellenistic Theosophy and Gnosis". The Poimandres (Corpus Hermeticum I) tells how Man, born of Mind, leans down, loves his reflection in Nature and is joined to her; so man is mortal in body and immortal in essence. After death the soul climbs through seven planetary spheres, dropping one fault at each (growth, scheming, desire, arrogance, daring, greed, falsehood), reaches the eighth sphere and becomes God. Corpus Hermeticum XIII is a talk on rebirth: Tat sheds twelve torments, receives ten powers, and sees himself everywhere, in heaven, in earth, in animals, in the womb and before it.
Who: Mead 1906; CH I (paragraphs about 12 to 26) and CH XIII. Exact numbers [U].
Parallels: an ancient arrow of stages; a rebirth after them.
Tier: [M] for the content. [U] for section numbers.
Use: the strongest Western text for one, separation, climb, return, then a new birth. See lead 1.

16. Purucker: the esoteric tradition and the Glossary
Claim: Gottfried de Purucker (1874 to 1942) led the Point Loma and later Pasadena society from 1929. His books: Fundamentals of the Esoteric Philosophy (1932), Occult Glossary (1933), The Esoteric Tradition (1935, two volumes). The universe is a living hierarchy in which every being is a ray of a greater being. He prefers "reimbodiment" to "reincarnation", because the soul re-embodies on many planes. His Glossary sets Sanskrit, Hebrew, Greek and Tibetan terms side by side.
Parallels: Kabbalah (he treats the Zohar as a form of the same tradition), Pythagoras, Hindu, Buddhist.
Tier: [M] for dates and titles. [U] for the organism emphasis and the Kabbalah matches.
Use: the organism picture supports karma as one part of a body harming another, the fingers. A reference book, not a source to quote.

18. Steiner: from the German section to Anthroposophy
Claim: Rudolf Steiner (1861 to 1925) led the German section (1902 to 1912) and left or was expelled in 1913, partly over Besant's claim that Krishnamurti was the coming World Teacher. He wrote Christianity as Mystical Fact (1902), Theosophy (1904), Knowledge of the Higher Worlds (1904 to 1905), Occult Science (1910) and Karmic Relationships (lectures, 1924). He keeps rebirth and karma but makes Christ the central event of earth's development, so he reads the Greek, Egyptian and Persian mysteries as a run-up to Christ. His three higher knowings are Imagination, Inspiration, Intuition. His six supplementary exercises (control of thought, of will, equanimity, positivity, open-mindedness, balance of the five) are an early Western practice of letting go.
Tier: [M] for dates, titles, the Christ centre and the exercises. [U] for the intervals between lives he gives.
Use: a comparer who does not say all religions are equal, and a good counter-voice to "all say the same". Equanimity is the swing-damping exercise. Critics have pointed to racial passages in his lectures [U]. Check before use.

19. Bailey: Hierarchy, rays and the three veils
Claim: Alice Bailey (1880 to 1949), a Theosophist until about 1920, founded the Arcane School and Lucis Trust and wrote books she ascribed to a Tibetan teacher, Djwhal Khul. She names three veils over the mind: maya, glamour and illusion, linked to the physical, emotional and mental levels. In The Reappearance of the Christ (1948) she says all religions await the same World Teacher under different names (Christ, Messiah, Maitreya, Imam Mahdi, Krishna).
Parallels: the World Teacher equation is a classic comparer move.
Tier: [M] for the broad teaching. [U] for the assignment of the three veils and the list of names.
Use: three kinds of wall at three levels, as an example of structure only. The "dictated" source is unverifiable.

20. Krishnamurti: the break (1929)
Claim: Found by Leadbeater in 1909 and trained as World Teacher, Jiddu Krishnamurti dissolved the Order of the Star at Ommen on 3 August 1929 and left the Society. He said truth is a pathless land that no organisation, creed or stage can reach. His later teaching (the observer is the observed; choiceless awareness) says the division of self and world is the problem and no method undoes it. Huxley wrote the foreword to The First and Last Freedom (1954).

Use: the honest counterweight to stages. One paragraph in the arrow section could say that the person Theosophy raised to lead the path rejected the path. "The observer is the observed" is a plain-English version of the veil.

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

One. Start from a master list and find each item in each religion. Besant's Seven Principles of Man (1892) gives Sanskrit names (Atma, Buddhi, Manas, Kama, Prana and two bodies) and lines them up with Christian spirit, soul and body and the Kabbalah's soul names. Specialists note that Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold scheme and that Buddhi is the discriminating faculty, not a "spiritual soul".

Two. Match by position in a sequence. Four Buddhist stages become the first four initiations. The gospel life becomes five. The Advaita fourfold means become four qualifications. Order is taken to prove identity.

Three. Match by number: Trinity, Trimurti and Osiris, Isis and Horus; seven principles with seven rays, holy immortals, planes, heavens. This is the weakest step.

Four. Treat the public religion as the veil of an inner one. Besant's Esoteric Christianity and Mead's Gnosis both say the creed is the outer face of the mysteries. This lets any difference count as agreement, since the outer is never the real. It cannot be refuted, which is its strength and its flaw.

Five. Explain differences by four devices. Each Great Teacher adapts to a people and an age (Besant). Priests and councils corrupted the teaching (Judge, Besant, with the 553 and 869 stories). Different temperaments need different religions (Hodson, Bailey's rays). Different words for the same item (the Glossaries of Blavatsky, edited by Mead, and of Purucker).

Six. Add observation. Leadbeater, Besant and Hodson say trained clairvoyants confirm what scriptures hint at (Occult Chemistry, 1908, is the famous case). This turns a textual comparison into a report that only the reporters can check.

Seven. Negotiate common ground. Olcott's Fourteen Propositions (1891) are the one case where authorities of two schools were asked to sign.

Eight. Translate and edit the sources. Mead on the Hermetic and Gnostic texts, Subba Row on Vedanta, Judge on the Gita and Olcott's Catechism work from named texts. They are the most checkable and the most useful to Alex.

What Alex's axiom does differently: it begins with a premise, asks what follows, shows each tradition's own wording, and does not claim identity. Seven and eight are worth copying. Four, used as a rule, is the one to avoid.

## 5. What scholars say

From memory, [M] unless marked.

- Campbell (Ancient Wisdom Revived, 1980) is the standard history. Washington (Madame Blavatsky's Baboon, 1993) is sceptical. Hanegraaff (1996, 2012) places Theosophy in Western esotericism and the New Age. Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, 2004) studies its claim to knowledge by direct observation.
- Olcott: Prothero (The White Buddhist, 1996). Gombrich and Obeyesekere (Buddhism Transformed, 1988) place his catechisms and schools in a "Protestant Buddhism".
- Besant: Nethercot (1960, 1963) and Taylor (1992) are the biographies.
- Leadbeater: Tillett, The Elder Brother (1982). Historians treat his reports as unverifiable.
- Mead: respected as an editor, dated as a translator. The Hermetica now rests on Nock and Festugiere (1945 to 1954), Fowden (1986) and Copenhaver (1992), who treat the texts as eclectic Greek philosophy, not a secret doctrine. Nag Hammadi (found 1945) transformed Gnostic studies, and the label "Gnosticism" is now contested (Williams 1996, King 2003).
- Mystery religions: Burkert (Ancient Mystery Cults, 1987) shows they were rites and experience without systematic doctrine, which weakens the claim that they taught rebirth and oneness as a creed.
- Perennialists: Guenon's Le Theosophisme (1921) called Theosophy a pseudo-religion and found Besant's wing a modern invention. Part two names both families, so one clause of honesty would help.
- A common judgement: they gave Asian religions a fair hearing in the West, while the equations are often wrong in detail.
