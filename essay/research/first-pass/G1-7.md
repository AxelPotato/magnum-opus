## 1. The picture in brief

The Pratyabhijna ("Recognition") school of Kashmir Shaivism is the closest technical match I know to Alex's three clauses. One consciousness, free by nature, shows the world on its own surface, contracts into limited knowers, and recovers itself by recognising what it never stopped being. It names the veil exactly (three malas, five cloaks), it names the contraction and its opposite (sankoca and vikasa), and it counts hiding as one of five things the whole does, with grace as another. Its way home is recognition, not escape, and the world stays real throughout.

The perennial anthologists and the Traditionalists mostly leaned on Advaita Vedanta, Sufism and Christian mysticism, and I know of no sign that they used this school [U]. It reached English readers late: first in the 1910s, strongly from the 1960s.

Next to it stand systems that keep world and souls real inside one whole. Ramanuja makes them God's body. The bhedabheda schools and Chaitanya hold oneness and difference together. For clause three, "the separation ends", they answer weakly: bondage ends and individuality stays. The Yoga Vasistha is the nearest Indian story of one consciousness living many lives and worlds with the memory switched off, and it adds a ladder of stages and a teaching of effort over fate.

The contraction vocabulary is widespread: sankoca and vikasa, qabd and bast, tzimtzum with katnut and gadlut, sankhitta and vikkhitta, song. These are cousins and not twins. One names the scope of knowing, one a mood sent by God, one a cosmic withdrawal, one a sluggish mind, one a bodily quality. Alex can use the family resemblance if he says which is which.

## 2. Findings

### A. Kashmir Shaivism

1. The school and its texts [M]
Claim: "Kashmir Shaivism" is a modern umbrella for Shaiva traditions of Kashmir from about the 9th to the 11th century: Trika (the triad of Shiva, Shakti and the soul), Pratyabhijna (the philosophy of recognition), Spanda (pulsation), and the more ritual Kula and Krama. The phrase itself dates from the early 1900s (J. C. Chatterji, 1914 [U]).
Who: Vasugupta (Shiva Sutras; the Spanda Karikas are attributed to him or to his pupil Kallata); Somananda (Shivadrishti); Utpaladeva (Isvara-pratyabhijna-karika with his own vrtti, early 10th century); Abhinavagupta (c. 950 to 1016: Tantraloka, Tantrasara, Paramarthasara, Isvara-pratyabhijna-vimarsini); Kshemaraja (Pratyabhijna-hrdayam, Spanda-nirnaya).
Parallels the school itself cites: none for comparison's sake. It argues against Buddhists, Nyaya, Mimamsa, Samkhya and Advaita, and ranks them (section 4).
Use for the essay: call it "the Recognition school", about a thousand years old. Dates need checking.

2. The core story: free consciousness shows the world on its own screen [M for the gist, U for numbering]
Claim: The ultimate is consciousness (citi, also called Shiva or Bhairava) whose nature is light (prakasha) joined to self-awareness (vimarsha), and whose essence is freedom (svatantrya). Out of that freedom it manifests the universe. The world is the shining-forth (abhasa) of consciousness in itself. It is not illusion and not a second thing.
Who: Kshemaraja, Pratyabhijna-hrdayam, sutras 1 and 2. Gist of 1: consciousness, being free, is the cause of the universe coming about. Gist of 2: by its own will it unfolds (the verb is unmilayati, literally "opens like an eye or a petal") the universe on its own surface (svabhitti: one's own wall, canvas or screen). Translations: Jaideva Singh (1963), Christopher Wallis (2017).
Mapping to Alex: one whole is citi; separation is its own free self-limitation; the separation ends in recognition.
Use for the essay: "on its own screen" has the same structure as his two windows on one machine. Say the match is structural, and check the wording in Singh or Wallis before quoting.

3. Why would the whole do it: freedom, play, delight [M, with one U]
Claim: The school gives no outside reason. The whole manifests because it is free, and the manifestation is play (krida, also lila) and delight (ananda; Abhinavagupta's wonder, camatkara). Abhinavagupta and Utpaladeva use the mirror: a city appears in a mirror as if outside it, though nothing is in the mirror but the mirror. The school objects to Advaita on one point: a light without self-awareness would be inert, like a crystal, so the Absolute must have vimarsha and freedom. For that reason it will not call the world maya in the sense of illusion.
Who: Utpaladeva, Isvara-pratyabhijna-karika; Abhinavagupta, Isvara-pratyabhijna-vimarsini and Tantraloka. Locators unknown. Parallel: Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 (lila), already in the essay.
Use for the essay: a stronger answer than "knowing needs a gap". The school says the whole never actually loses itself; the hiding is part of the play. That sits slightly against Alex's clause that the whole forgets for a while, so flag it. [U] whether Abhinavagupta ever names self-knowing as the purpose; I think he says no purpose is needed.

4. The five acts, with concealment and grace [M; the mapping of Kshemaraja's five functions is U]
Claim: Shiva's activity is fivefold (panchakritya): srishti (emission), sthiti (maintenance), samhara (withdrawal or dissolution), a fourth act of concealment, and anugraha (grace). The fourth is named tirodhana or tirobhava in some texts and vilaya or pidhana in others. In the Trika, concealment and grace are one freedom seen twice: the power that veils is the power that unveils. Grace is shaktipata, the descent of power, graded and not earned (Tantraloka chapter 13 [M for the chapter, U for "nine grades"]). Kshemaraja's commentary on sutra 1 lists five functions under other names (abhasana, rakti, vimarsana, bijavasthapana, vilapana) [U on the mapping to the five acts].
Coomaraswamy's "The Dance of Siva" (1918) shows the five acts in the Nataraja image: the drum for creation, the open hand for preservation, the fire for destruction, the planted foot on the dwarf for concealment, the raised foot for release. He says the dance is cosmic play whose purpose is to release souls from illusion and whose place is the heart [M for the gist]. His source is Tamil Saiva Siddhanta (the Unmai Vilakkam and the Tirumantiram), not Kashmir [M, 70 percent].
Use for the essay: "The tradition counts five things the whole does: it shows, holds, withdraws, hides and gives itself back." Hiding sits in a list with showing, and the Nataraja is a picture many readers know.

5. The three malas: the most exact ancient taxonomy of the veil [M]
Claim: Three malas ("stains") bind the experiencer. Anava mala is the primal limitation, the sense of being small and incomplete, "I am only this". Mayiya mala is the stain of difference: seeing oneself as other than the world and other people (bheda-buddhi). Karma mala is the sense of being a doer who acts for results and carries their residue.
The same family has the five kanchukas (cloaks): kala (limits power to act), vidya (limits knowledge), raga (limits fullness, so desire appears), kala as time (limits eternity), niyati (limits omnipresence into cause and place). Each cloak shrinks one of Shiva's five powers [M, confident].
Who: Abhinavagupta (Tantraloka, Tantrasara), Kshemaraja; summarised by Singh and Wallis. Experiencers are classed by how many malas remain (sakala, pralayakala, vijnanakala); the allocation differs between schools [U].
Difference to flag: in Tamil Saiva Siddhanta (Meykandar, 13th century) anava is a beginningless substance clinging to the soul like husk on rice. In Kashmir it is Shiva's own self-limitation [M].
Use for the essay: a three-layer veil: feeling small, seeing others as other, and believing I author my acts and keep the receipts. Alex's wall, screen-distance and karma map onto these roughly; the mapping is mine. The Tamil Saiva Siddhanta (pati, pashu, pasha: Lord, soul, bond) has the same three bonds and keeps souls plural; Coomaraswamy's Nataraja comes from it.

6. Contraction and expansion: sankoca and vikasa [M for the terms, U for numbering]
Claim: The key technical word of the Pratyabhijna-hrdayam is sankoca, contraction. Gist of sutras 4 to 6: the individual experiencer is consciousness itself in contracted form; when consciousness steps down from its own level and is narrowed by its objects, it is called citta, the mind. The opposite word is vikasa, expansion (a sutra near the end makes the expansion of the central channel the way to the bliss of consciousness [U on the number]). In the Spanda tradition the same rhythm is unmesha (opening, as of eyelids) and nimesha (closing). The first Spanda Karika says in gist that the world's arising and dissolving are the opening and closing of the Lord's eyes.
Who: Kshemaraja; Vasugupta or Kallata; Dyczkowski (1987, 1992); Singh (1980).
Use for the essay: sankoca is the tradition's word for Alex's "braced", vikasa for "released". Sankoca is first a narrowing of the scope of awareness and power, and only secondarily a clench in the body; the bodily reading comes from practice texts and modern translators.

7. Recognition as the method [M; verse numbers U]
Claim: Pratyabhijna means re-cognition. Bondage is non-recognition, liberation is recognising what one already is, and nothing new is acquired. Utpaladeva's opening verse says in gist that he writes to make the recognition of the Lord easy for everyone. The path has four upayas: anupaya (no means), shambhavopaya (will or sheer intent), shaktopaya (discernment), anavopaya (breath, body, form). The Vijnana Bhairava gives 112 techniques, among them resting in the gap between breaths and meeting fear, grief, surprise or a sneeze with open attention; the Spanda Karikas teach finding the ground in a surge of strong emotion. In Abhinavagupta grace (shaktipata) comes first. The last sutra of the Pratyabhijna-hrdayam says in gist that lasting samadhi comes by recognising the identity with consciousness again and again on coming out of samadhi, so practice enters ordinary activity. Liberation while living (jivanmukti) is accepted.
Use for the essay: "recognition" fits clause three better than "return", and a top rung of "no method" echoes dropping effort. Caveat: Sanderson and others stress that recognition is tied to initiation (diksha) and ritual, so a purely psychological reading is a modern repackaging.

8. Lives, karma and ethics in Kashmir [M for rebirth, U for the ethics point]
Claim: Rebirth is assumed. Karma mala is the residue of action, and grace can cut through it unearned. I know of no place where the school derives non-harm from oneness, and its ritual life includes transgressive Kaula rites.
Use for the essay: do not cite Kashmir for the fingers. Cite it for the veil and for recognition.

### B. Systems that keep the world and souls real inside one whole

9. Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita [M; the sankoca point U at 80 percent]
Claim: There is one reality, Brahman as Narayana. Souls (cit) and matter (acit) are real, eternal, and exist as his body (sharira), while he is their self (sharirin). The relation is inseparability (aprthak-siddhi). The world is his body in a gross or subtle state. Souls stay plural and individual even in release. "That thou art" says that the "you" has Brahman as its self; it does not erase the soul.
Who: Ramanuja (c. 1017 to 1137 by tradition [U]), Sri Bhashya on the Brahma Sutras, Gita Bhashya. Proof text: the Antaryamin section of the Brihadaranyaka (3.7): the one who dwells in earth, water and so on, whose body they are. Translation: Thibaut, SBE 48 (1904, public domain). Studies: Carman (1974), Lipner (1986).
Contraction vocabulary: I believe he says the soul's own knowledge is boundless but is contracted (sankoca) by karma in bondage and expands (vikasa) in release [U, about 80 percent; check the Sri Bhashya on release].
Karma: God presides over the fruits (Brahma Sutra 3.2.38, which Shankara also reads this way [M]).
Veil: not maya. He attacks Shankara's avidya with a long list of objections [M]; the soul's knowledge is narrowed and Brahman is untouched. Return: life with God in Vaikuntha, no merging.
Letting go: prapatti, surrender, is central (Gita 18.66, in gist: give up all other supports and take refuge in God alone). The Sri Vaishnava schools split on it: the Vadakalai (Vedanta Desika) say the soul clings, like a baby monkey to its mother; the Tenkalai (Pillai Lokacharya, Manavala Mamuni) say the soul is carried, like a kitten by the scruff [M]. Mumme, The Srivaisnava Theological Dispute (1988).
Use for the essay: the Indian witness for "the many are real, inside one whole". The monkey and the cat is a ready picture for Part three: do I grip or am I carried.
Specialists object: Shankara's school says a real many cannot be one.

10. The bhedabheda family [M for the outline, U for dates]
Claim: A family of Vedanta readings in which Brahman is one and the many are real, so oneness and difference are both true. Bhaskara (c. 8th or 9th century) holds that Brahman really transforms into world and souls, and that souls are Brahman limited by conditions (upadhis) such as body and mind. Liberation is the removal of those limits. Nimbarka (12th or 13th century) teaches dvaitadvaita, a natural difference and non-difference like the sun and its rays, or a snake and its coils. Yadavaprakasha, Ramanuja's early teacher, belongs here.
Use for the essay: Bhaskara's upadhi picture is the closest Indian version of "the whole, with a limit placed on it". Dates unchecked. The Advaita reply is that one thing cannot be both.

11. Chaitanya and acintya-bhedabheda [M; the quotation locator U]
Claim: Chaitanya (1486 to 1534) taught devotion to Krishna. His followers Jiva Goswami (Sat-sandarbhas, 16th century) and Baladeva Vidyabhushana (Govinda Bhashya, 18th century) formulated acintya-bhedabheda: inconceivable oneness and difference. The soul is a minute part of Krishna's power, a "marginal power" (tatastha shakti) between the spiritual and the material that may face either. The term is later than Chaitanya, who left only eight verses (Shikshashtaka).
Veil: forgetting. I recall Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya 20.117, saying in gist that the soul, forgetting Krishna, has been turned away since beginningless time, and so maya gives it the pain of rebirth [M on the gist, U on the locator]. Basis: Vishnu Purana 6.7.61 on the three powers (para, the souls, and ignorance and karma) [U on the verse].
Letting go: Shikshashtaka 3 asks the devotee to be humbler than a blade of grass, more tolerant than a tree, to give honour to others and expect none [M, confident].
Return: the aim is prema, loving service, kept in separation as much as in union; merging (sayujya) is the lesser goal.
Use for the essay: the clearest Indian text where forgetting causes bondage. It fits clauses one and two, and does not fit "the separation ends", which Alex should say.

12. The Yoga Vasistha (and its core, the Moksopaya) [M for the contents, U for locators]
Claim: A long Sanskrit dialogue between the sage Vasistha and the prince Rama, in six books. The one consciousness (cit, Brahman) is all there is. The mind, through its own intention (sankalpa), projects world after world, each containing minds that project further worlds. Ignorance is the mind's holding to its own projection; liberation is dropping it, even while living.
Stories that fit "many lives of one": Queen Lila, who with a goddess's help sees her dead husband living another life in another world; Gadhi, who dips into water and lives a whole life as an outcaste in moments; Bhusunda the crow, who remembers many cycles of creation.
Stages: seven stages of knowledge (desire for the good, inquiry, thinning of the mind, clarity, non-clinging, non-perception of objects, and the "fourth") and seven of ignorance [U on the list; I believe it is in the Utpatti book, around 3.118]. A stage-map in Indian clothes.
Effort: Book 2 argues that self-effort (purusakara) beats fate, since fate is only earlier effort [U on chapters]. A strong match for Alex's agency theme.
Translations: Venkatesananda (1984, 1993); Aiyer, Laghu Yoga Vasistha (1896, public domain).
Scholars: Walter Slaje and colleagues date the original Moksopaya to about the 10th century in Kashmir, the longer Yoga Vasistha being later [M, 70 percent].
Use for the essay: the best Indian story of one consciousness living many lives and worlds, and a source for "effort beats fate" in Part three. Check stories and numbers first.

13. Upanishadic knots and release [M for the gist, U for verse numbers]
Claim: The shared root already speaks of tying and untying. Katha 2.3 and Brihadaranyaka 4.4.7 say in gist that when all desires lodged in the heart are released (pra-muc), the mortal becomes immortal here; Mundaka 2.2.8 says the knot of the heart is cut. Check verse numbers.
Use for the essay: a plain Sanskrit "let go" under every system above, matching his tension-as-knot picture.

### C. The contraction and expansion vocabulary elsewhere

14. Sufi qabd and bast [M; the order of chapters and the Qushayri wording U]
Claim: Qabd (contraction, constriction, grasping) and bast (expansion, spreading) are two states (ahwal) of the heart sent by God. They are also two of the divine names, al-Qabid and al-Basit. The Quran (2:245) says in gist that God contracts and expands [M].
Who: al-Qushayri, Risala (completed 1046), a chapter on qabd and bast; translations by Knysh (2007) and von Schlegell (1990). Hujwiri's Kashf al-Mahjub has a similar section (Nicholson's translation, 1911, public domain).
Gist: they rank above fear and hope, which face the future, while qabd and bast face the present moment. Accept the state and do not fight it, since the traveller did not cause it; in bast keep manners, since expansion tempts a person to overreach [M, 75 percent]. Rumi treats qabz and bast in Masnavi Book 3 [U].
Parallel: Ibn Arabi's "breath of the All-Merciful" is the same rhythm at cosmic scale, an out-breath that makes the world and an in-breath that gathers it [M]. Chittick may treat al-Qabid and al-Basit in The Sufi Path of Knowledge [U].
Use for the essay: "A state comes, you do not cause it, and you do not push it away." A good line for the practical part.

15. Pali sankhitta and vikkhitta [M; the commentary point M at 75 percent]
Claim: In the Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10, and Digha Nikaya 22), the contemplation of mind lists pairs of states to be known as they are: with lust and without, with hatred and without, with delusion and without, contracted (sankhitta) and scattered (vikkhitta), developed and undeveloped, surpassed and unsurpassed, concentrated and unconcentrated, freed and unfreed [M, confident]. Translations: Nanamoli and Bodhi (1995), Analayo (2003).
Catch: the commentary (Buddhaghosa) glosses "contracted" as the mind with sloth and torpor and "scattered" as the mind with restlessness [M, 75 percent]. So Pali "contracted" is a dull, shrunk mind, not Alex's tense one.
Use for the essay: cite it with the sloth caveat, or leave it out.

16. Tzimtzum, katnut and gadlut [M]
Claim: In Lurianic Kabbalah (Isaac Luria, Safed, 16th century; Chaim Vital's Etz Chaim), the Infinite (Ein Sof) contracts (tzimtzum) to leave an empty space for a world. The light then enters, the vessels shatter, and sparks fall. Gathering them is repair (tikkun). Hasidic writers use katnut (smallness, constricted mind) and gadlut (greatness, expanded mind) for the states of the human mind. Chabad reads tzimtzum as apparent only: from God's side nothing is withdrawn (Tanya, Shaar ha-Yichud ve-ha-Emunah).
Scholars: Scholem, Major Trends (1941), and his essay on self-contraction of God; Idel (1988), who argues the early sources may mean concentration and not withdrawal [U]; Elior on Habad (1993). Green's Ehyeh (2003) and Matt's God and the Big Bang (1996) are modern bridges.
Difference: tzimtzum is a cosmic act leaving a void. Sankoca is a limit on awareness within a fullness that is never absent.
Use for the essay: the broken vessels give separation a wound-and-repair shape. Katnut and gadlut are the closest Jewish pair to braced and released.

17. Daoist song [U for the history; M for the Laozi points]
Claim: Chinese song (鬆) means loose, free of knotted tension. In Taiji it is a named discipline (loosen the waist, in Yang Chengfu's ten essentials [M, 85 percent]), and modern qigong has "relaxation and quiet" methods [U]. The classical roots are Daodejing 76 (the living are soft, the dead stiff [M]) and Zhuangzi's fasting of the heart-mind and sitting and forgetting [M]. As a technical term song is mostly later than the classics.
Use for the essay: the nearest Chinese word for "unclench", a bodily art term and not a doctrine of the one.

18. Western cousins [M]
Cusanus (De docta ignorantia, 1440): God as complicatio (enfolding), the universe as explicatio (unfolding). Bohm: enfolded and unfolded order (1980). Reich: expansion and contraction as the body's two basic movements [U on wording]. Aurobindo, The Life Divine (1939 to 1940): involution, in which the Divine hides in matter, and evolution, in which it recovers itself [M]. Use for the essay: mention one, not all.

19. Who used or ignored Kashmir Shaivism among the comparers [M for the outline, U for specifics]
Guenon read Hinduism mainly through Shankara's Advaita and distrusted Tantra [M]. Coomaraswamy used Saiva Siddhanta for the Nataraja. I know of no Kashmir terms in Schuon [U]. Huxley's anthology (1945) predates the English scholarship on Kashmir, and I do not believe it cites Abhinavagupta [U]. Alan Watts's hide-and-seek (The Book, 1966) is lila and concealment in a pop key; I do not believe he uses the Shaiva terms [M for the book, U for the rest]. Wilber's involution and evolution tell a similar story; whether he cites Kashmir Shaivism I cannot confirm [U].
Use for the essay: Alex can say that this school sits outside the perennial anthologies, and say what he is adding.

## 4. How they compare: the method

The Kashmir authors compare in order to rank. Abhinavagupta absorbs other systems as partial views taken from lower levels of the 36 tattvas (the levels from Shiva down to earth): roughly, the materialist speaks from the body level, the Samkhya from the level of purusha, the Vedantin from near maya, the Buddhist of the void from a state of dissolution, and the Trika sees all of them from above [M for the device, U for which view sits where]. Paul Hacker calls this style inclusivism: the other is real, partial and lower. Alex should not copy it; perennial authors sometimes slip into it.

The Pratyabhijna writers also argue from recognition. Everyday recognition ("this is that man I saw yesterday") joins memory and perception in one judgment and so presupposes one continuing knower, which they use against Buddhists who deny such a knower (Utpaladeva's Siddhitrayi [M for the gist]). For Alex's "who carries over?", the school's answer is one knower, carried by memory, which differs from a soul that travels.

The modern comparers work differently. Coomaraswamy sets image against image and text against text (Eckhart, Plato, Upanishad, sutra) to show one metaphysical idea. Watts goes metaphor first, with hide-and-seek as the picture. Wilber builds a stage map and says traditions describe different stretches of one road. The Traditionalists (Guenon, Schuon) rank traditions by metaphysical purity and split an outer layer from an inner one.

## 5. What scholars say

- Alexis Sanderson: Kashmir Shaivism is a ritual, initiatory Tantric religion, and reading it as a free-floating philosophy of oneness loses its mantra, initiation and Kaula transgression. The label is modern [M].
- Raffaele Torella and Isabelle Ratie: the Pratyabhijna is a rigorous philosophy of self-awareness argued against Buddhist epistemology, and not a loose monism [M].
- Paul Hacker: Indian inclusivism absorbs other views and does not treat them as equals [M].
- Lipner and Carman: Ramanuja's body-soul relation is organic and theistic, and not a variant of Advaita [M].
- Slaje and others: the Moksopaya predates the longer Yoga Vasistha [M, 70 percent]. Scholem and Idel disagree on the shape and sources of tzimtzum [M].
- Steven Katz (1978) and Jorge Ferrer (2002) argue that mystical experience is shaped by tradition, against a common core. Every comparative claim in the essay faces that objection [M].
