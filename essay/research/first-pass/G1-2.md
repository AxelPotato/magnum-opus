## 1. The picture in brief

Theosophy's primary texts give Alex the nearest ready-made statement of his axiom, and also some useful differences. The Secret Doctrine opens with three propositions: one boundless, immutable Principle; the periodic appearing and disappearing of universes; and the fundamental identity of all souls with the Universal Over-Soul, together with an obligatory pilgrimage of every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Blavatsky's gloss says the spark wins individuality only by its own effort, with no privileges. That is "one whole, experiencing separation, returning", in her vocabulary.

Her karma is impersonal. It is a law of harmony, and "we reward or punish ourselves". The Glossary says karma neither punishes nor rewards, and the Mahatma Letters say the ultimate is "law", not a God. The same SD passage on karma, as I remember it, says its ways would not be inscrutable if people worked in union rather than strife, which is close to Alex's fingers.

The Key to Theosophy supplies the lives machinery: personality as a mask over a lasting individuality, devachan as a rest between lives, and a reply to "why do we not remember". The Voice of the Silence and Light on the Path supply the ethics of separateness ("Kill out all sense of separateness"; compassion that refuses private liberation).

Four cautions. Isis Unveiled (1877) rejected general reincarnation and the doctrine developed later. Her borrowing from secondary sources was heavy. Her own texts say they contain "blinds". A board of "Lords of Karma" is mostly a later popular layer. Also, in Theosophy the whole cycle repeats, and the return gives a conscious individual, not a dissolved one.

## 2. Findings

F1. The first proposition, the Principle.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine (1888), Vol. I, Proem, "three fundamental propositions", SD I 14 in the 1888 pagination.
Words: remembered wording, unverified: "An Omnipresent, Eternal, Boundless, and Immutable PRINCIPLE on which all speculation is impossible". She adds that it is beyond thought, citing the Mandukya Upanishad's "unthinkable and unspeakable" (gist).
Parallels: Vedanta (Parabrahm), Mandukya Upanishad, Mulaprakriti. She also coins "Be-ness" for what antecedes Being.
Tier: wording M, page M.
Use: the Theosophical form of "one whole". The Principle is not a personal God and not "consciousness" in any ordinary sense.

F2. The second proposition, periodicity.
Who: SD I 16.
Words: paraphrase. The Universe is eternal as a boundless plane and periodically the playground of numberless universes that appear and disappear, like a tidal ebb and flow.
Parallels: Hindu days and nights of Brahma (manvantara and pralaya).
Tier: M, page M/U.
Use: it blocks an easy reading of "the separation ends". Here the ending is a pause, and the cycle recurs.

F3. The third proposition, the "Unknown Root" and the obligatory pilgrimage.
Who: SD I 17.
Words: remembered wording: "The fundamental identity of all Souls with the Universal Over-Soul, the latter being itself an aspect of the Unknown Root; and the obligatory pilgrimage for every Soul, a spark of the former, through the Cycle of Incarnation (or Necessity)". It continues "in accordance with Cyclic and Karmic law".
Parallels: Emerson's Over-Soul; Pythagorean and Egyptian "cycle of necessity"; Hindu karma.
Tier: wording M, page M.
Use: the best single Theosophical match for Alex's three clauses. The Over-Soul is a term from Emerson (1841), and "the Cycle of Necessity" is her Greek and Egyptian echo.

F4. The gloss: no privileges.
Who: SD I 17, the paragraph after the propositions.
Words: remembered wording, shortened: the spark passes through the elemental forms and "acquired individuality, first by natural impulse, and then by self-induced and self-devised efforts (checked by its Karma)". The "pivotal doctrine" admits "no privileges or special gifts in man", except those won by his own Ego "through personal effort and merit".
Parallels: none named in this paragraph. It reads as her Theosophical version of the ladder from mineral to archangel.
Tier: M, page M.
Use: for the point that nobody is excused and nobody is favoured. The individuality is won. This is a difference from a pure "dissolve into the One": the spark becomes a conscious, independent Buddhi and not an absorbed drop.

F5. The comparer's claim, in her own words.
Who: Blavatsky, SD I, Introductory (1888).
Words: gist: the esoteric philosophy reconciles all systems, strips every religion of its outward human garments, and shows the root of each to be identical with that of every other great religion. Wording is remembered; I could not give the page (roman or arabic).
Parallels: all "great religions", with Hindu, Buddhist, Kabbalistic, Egyptian and Gnostic examples throughout.
Tier: gist M, locator U.
Use: a short statement of the method Alex follows. Quote only after checking.

F6. The karma passage, "absolute harmony".
Who: SD, Vol. I, the karma-nemesis section (which Part or Addendum it sits in: U). Page: I remember 643 in the 1888 first edition. I cannot account for 634; it may be a transposition, or another edition. Unresolved.
Words: remembered wording: "The only decree of Karma, an eternal and immutable decree, is absolute Harmony in the world of matter as it is in the world of Spirit." She then says it is "we, who reward or punish ourselves", according to whether we work with nature or break the laws of that harmony.
A related sentence, as I remember it: the ways of Karma would not be inscrutable "were men to work in union and harmony, instead of disunion and strife". Gist only; wording unverified.
Parallels: she mentions Providence, Fatalism and chance as the three ways people misread it. No tradition is named in that sentence.
Tier: wording M, page U (643 versus 634 open).
Use: this is the best Theosophical support for karma as "something I did to another part of myself". It ties karma to union. Check the page and the next two sentences before quoting.

F7. Karma in the Key to Theosophy.
Who: Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy (1889), the section on karma, near the end (Section number U).
Words: remembered wording: "Karma is the unerring law which adjusts effect to cause, on the physical, mental and spiritual planes of being." She calls it the ultimate law and says it is not a being.
The bough: I also remember an image of a bent bough springing back, used to explain restoration of balance, but I am unsure it is in the Key and unsure of the words. Do not use.
Parallels: Hindu and Buddhist karma; "Nemesis" in the Greek sense is said to apply only to bad karma.
Tier: first wording M, locator U; bough U.
Use: karma as an equilibrium law, not a verdict.

F8. Devachan in the Key.
Who: Key to Theosophy, the sections on post-mortem states and on Kama-loka and Devachan (numbers U).
Words: paraphrase. After death the personality passes through Kama-loka, where its shell fades. The Ego enters devachan, a state of subjective bliss in which each person lives out the highest aspirations of the life just ended. It is not a place and not eternal. Its length depends on the life's spiritual yield, and she gives figures of many centuries (I remember "ten to fifteen centuries"; unconfirmed, U). Thirst for life then draws the Ego back.
Parallels: she links devachan to the Tibetan term for the "Land of Bliss" (Sukhavati); the Heaven of other religions is called a rudimentary version.
Tier: gist M, figures U.
Use: Alex's "soul continues between lives" has a detailed Theosophical form, but it is a dream-rest, and séance "spirits" are shells in her system. Keep the claim light.

F9. Personality as mask, individuality as the lasting thing.
Who: Key to Theosophy, the sections on the Ego and on reincarnation (numbers U). Glossary, "Sutratma".
Words: paraphrase. "Personality" is the mask (Latin persona) of one life. The "Individuality" is the higher Ego, the continuing one, which collects the spiritual yield of each life and sheds the rest. In the Glossary the Sutratma is the "thread-soul" on which lives are strung like beads.
Parallels: Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7 (the "thread" that strings worlds and beings together; locator M, wording not checked). Vedanta's Sutratman and Hiranyagarbha.
Tier: gist M, locators U.
Use: matches Alex's "lives and the thread" picture exactly, and gives it a source. Check the Glossary entry.

F10. "Why do we not remember?"
Who: Key to Theosophy, section on reincarnation (number U).
Words: paraphrase of her reply. (1) Memory belongs to the brain, and the brain of each life is new. (2) We do not remember most of this life either: early childhood, or what we did on a given day. (3) Experience persists as faculty and character, and the Ego's memory is intact, only not reachable from the personality.
Parallels: none named; she speaks as a plain-reasoning apologist.
Tier: gist M, locator U.
Use: Alex can use the childhood comparison and credit her. Stevenson-type evidence is a separate matter.

F11. "Unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity".
Who: the brief gives this wording and I could not place it. The idea is in SD I 17 (F4). I believe a similar sentence is in the Key's discussion of why spirit descends into matter, but the phrase may be a paraphrase by a later writer.
Tier: wording U, idea M.
Use: do not quote this phrase until it has been located. Quote F4 instead.

F12. The comparer's genealogy and the three objects.
Who: Key to Theosophy, Section I (Theosophy and the Theosophical Society).
Words: paraphrase. "Theosophia" means divine wisdom. She traces the name to the third-century Alexandrian "Eclectic Theosophical School" of Ammonius Saccas, whose aim was to reconcile all sects and peoples. The Society's objects: universal brotherhood without distinction; the comparative study of religion, philosophy and science; the investigation of unexplained laws of nature and latent powers.
Parallels: Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, Hindu and Buddhist teachings.
Tier: M, locator M for Section I.
Use: says why Theosophy compares at all. Scholars doubt that Ammonius founded any such school (he left no writings); that picture comes from earlier historians of the 1700s.

F13. Isis Unveiled on reincarnation, against Spiritism.
Who: Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (New York, Bouton, 1877), two volumes (Science, Theology). Chajes, Recycled Lives: A History of Reincarnation in Blavatsky's Theosophy (Oxford University Press, 2019).
Words: paraphrase. In 1877 she rejected the French Spiritist doctrine (Kardec) that human spirits regularly return in new bodies, allowing only exceptional cases, and spoke of a "cycle of necessity". Chajes argues the full reincarnation doctrine was built up later, in the years after the move to India in 1879, and reached the form of the Secret Doctrine by the mid-1880s. The decisive years in her account are about 1879 to 1882 (date U). I also remember later Theosophical claims that Isis had been misread and always meant the Eastern doctrine; I am unsure how Chajes weighs that (U).
Parallels: Kardec; Pythagorean metempsychosis; Hindu and Buddhist rebirth, which she brought in after India.
Tier: gist M, dates U, page numbers: locator unknown.
Use: Alex should not say that Blavatsky always taught reincarnation. A true line: she began as a critic of the popular version and then taught her own.

F14. Glossary: Maya.
Who: Blavatsky, Theosophical Glossary (London, 1892, posthumous, compiled from her notes and edited by others; unlike the shorter glossary at the back of the Key, 1889).
Words: remembered wording: "Illusion; the cosmic power which renders phenomenal existence and the perceptions thereof possible." It says that in Hindu philosophy only the changeless is real, and what changes and ends is maya.
Parallels: Hindu philosophy (Vedanta).
Tier: wording M, which glossary U.
Use: this definition is close to Alex's "veil". Specialists on Advaita say "illusion" is a poor translation (see the repo notes: maya and mithya mean "dependent").

F15. Glossary: Karma.
Who: Theosophical Glossary.
Words: remembered wording: "Karma neither punishes nor rewards; it is simply the one Universal Law which guides unerringly, and, so to say, blindly, all other laws". It opens "Physically, action; metaphysically, the Law of Retribution". It also calls it "that law of re-adjustment" that restores disturbed equilibrium.
Parallels: orthodox Buddhism (she ties karma to the chain of causation; her exact link is U and may be loose).
Tier: wording M, locator U.
Use: karma without a judge. It fits Alex's refusal to name an administrator.

F16. Glossary: Devachan, Over-Soul, Manvantara.
Who: Theosophical Glossary, and Emerson.
Words: Devachan: paraphrase, "dwelling of the gods", the blissful state between two incarnations (M). Manvantara: remembered wording: "A period of manifestation, as opposed to Pralaya (dissolution, or rest)". It applies the term to a Day of Brahma and to the reign of one Manu. Figures like 4,320,000,000 years are remembered, U. Over-Soul: I cannot confirm that the Glossary has an entry. The term is Emerson's: "The Over-Soul" (Essays, First Series, 1841). Remembered wording: "Within man is the soul of the whole; the wise silence; the universal beauty, to which every part and particle is equally related; the eternal ONE."
Parallels: Puranas (days of Brahma); Tibetan bde-ba-can; Emerson.
Tier: Devachan M; Manvantara M; Emerson wording M (public domain, easy to check); Glossary entry for Over-Soul U.

F17. Voice of the Silence, structure and provenance.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (London, 1889).
Words: paraphrase. "Chosen fragments from the Book of the Golden Precepts", translated and annotated by her. Three fragments: I "The Voice of the Silence", II "The Two Paths", III "The Seven Portals". She says she learned the precepts by heart.
Parallels: she presents it as Mahayana. Scholars have not identified a Tibetan "Book of the Golden Precepts"; it reads as her own composition on Mahayana themes.
Tier: structure M, provenance claim M.
Use: say "attributed by Blavatsky to a Mahayana text she called the Book of the Golden Precepts, for which no source has been found".

F18. Voice, the key lines.
Who: Voice of the Silence. Fragment numbers are my memory, U; paragraph numbers unknown.
Words: remembered wording: "Mind is the great Slayer of the Real. Let the Disciple slay the Slayer." Fragment I (M/U). Remembered: "Thou canst not travel on the Path before thou hast become that Path itself." I believe Fragment I (U). Gist: when "at one" with the Soul-Thought of Nature (alaya) the disciple is invincible, and "in separation" he becomes the playground of samvriti, origin of delusions. Fragment I (U). Gist, Fragment III last lines: "Compassion speaks and saith: can there be bliss when all that lives must suffer?" (M for the end of Fragment III). A line about merging "the Ocean in the drop, the drop within the Ocean" and not separating one's being from Being; fragment U.
Parallels: Mahayana: alaya (store-consciousness, which Yogacara specialists do not equate with a universal soul; here the comparer equates it), samvriti (conventional truth), the bodhisattva.
Tier: lines M, fragment numbers U.
Use: the compassion line is the cleanest expression of "return includes all".

F19. The Two Paths and the Seven Portals.
Who: Voice, Fragments II and III.
Words: paraphrase. The Two Paths are liberation for oneself (the arhat or pratyeka-buddha) and the path of renunciation (the bodhisattva, who stays to help). The Seven Portals are named: dana (charity), shila (harmony in word and act), kshanti (patience), viraga (indifference), virya (dauntless energy), dhyana (contemplation), prajna (wisdom). I am confident of the list; the order of the first four I trust less (U).
Parallels: Mahayana paramitas. The Mahayana list is six; viraga is her addition.
Tier: M, order U.
Use: shows the comparer adding a term to a traditional list.

F20. Light on the Path.
Who: Mabel Collins, Light on the Path (London, Redway, 1885), Part I rules, Part II.
Words: remembered wording: "Kill out all sense of separateness." I am about 85 percent sure of the words and unsure of the rule number (U). Another line, Part II: "Each man is to himself absolutely the way, the truth, and the life."
Parallels: the line echoes John 14:6 deliberately (it is turned inward).
Tier: wording M/U, number U.

F21. The Mahatma Letters.
Who: A. T. Barker (ed.), The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett (London, Unwin, 1923). Barker numbering. Chin 1993 re-ordered chronologically.
Words: remembered wording: "Parabrahm is not a God, but absolute immutable law." I place it in Letter 10, near the passage "we deny God both as philosophers and as Buddhists". Page I think about 52 in Barker (U). The sentence goes on to say Iswar is the effect of avidya and maya.
Devachan and karma: letters discuss both at length (several, about letters 14 to 16 and 23); I cannot give the numbers (U).

Tier: wording M, letter M/U, page U.

F22. Sinnett and Subba Row.
Who: A. P. Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (London, Trubner, 1883). T. Subba Row, lectures on the Bhagavad Gita at the Adyar convention (late 1885 or 1886, U), published in The Theosophist in 1887.
Words: paraphrase. Sinnett set out the sevenfold human, devachan, rounds and root-races, in about ten chapters (chapter numbers U). Max Muller attacked the title in 1893. The "Esoteric Buddhism" name was later disowned. Subba Row, a Madras Brahmin lawyer, gave an Advaita-trained reading, with Logos and Mulaprakriti, and challenged Sinnett's sevenfold scheme.
Parallels: Advaita, Buddhism.
Tier: M, dates and numbers U.
Use: Subba Row is the Indian-trained Theosophical voice. He is a better witness for the Vedanta comparison than Sinnett.

F23. Judge, Besant, Olcott.
Who: Judge, The Ocean of Theosophy (1893) and Letters That Have Helped Me (1891; enlarged 1905). Besant, Karma (1895), Reincarnation (1892), The Ancient Wisdom (1897). Olcott, the "Fourteen Fundamental Buddhist Beliefs" (1891).
Words: paraphrase only. Judge's Ocean is a primer on the sevenfold human, karma and reincarnation. The Letters are practical spiritual advice. Besant's Karma treats karma as the resultant of forces (the "parallelogram of forces" image, U) and uses Hindu categories of stored, ripening and presently made karma (sanchita, prarabdha, kriyamana; M for the terms, U for her use of them in this book). ## 4. How they compare

Genealogy, not only resemblance. Blavatsky does not say that traditions converge because they describe the same reality. She says they descend from one archaic "Wisdom-Religion", and the Key traces the name Theosophy to Ammonius Saccas (F12). The differences are then explained as later damage: priestly corruption, mistranslation, and deliberate concealment ("blinds", F26).

Term-by-term equations. The Glossary and Voice match a term from one tradition to one from another: devachan with the Land of Bliss, alaya with the universal soul, the Over-Soul with Emerson's usage. Her exposition adds viraga to the paramitas. These are the places where specialists object.

Symbol reading. In Part II of the SD she reads egg, lotus, tree, serpent and triangle symbols across cultures as cipher for one cosmogony, and says symbols have several "keys" (number U).

Living witness. Her Masters stand as the claimed source, so the comparison rests on testimony as well as texts.

Eclectic quoting. Her Preface calls the book a nosegay (F25), and Coleman shows the flowers came from secondary books.

A contrast for Alex. The perennialists he cites argue from convergent inner experience. Blavatsky argues from a single transmission. He leans on the first when he says that traditions "speak of the same things". He should not claim the second.

## 5. What scholars say

Scholars treat Blavatsky as a major figure of modern Western esotericism with mixed credibility (Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996; Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994; Washington, Madame Blavatsky's Baboon, 1993; Lachman, 2012). The Hodgson Report (1885) judged the Mahatma letters fraudulent. Harrison (1986) disputed it. K. Paul Johnson has proposed real historical persons behind the Masters. Chajes (2019) gives the best account of how her reincarnation teaching changed. Coleman (1895) documented her borrowing. Max Muller objected that the Buddha taught nothing esoteric (1893). Her root-race scheme (SD Vol. II) is widely criticised as a racial hierarchy, though she also preached universal brotherhood. Specialists in Buddhism and Advaita reject several of her equations (alaya, maya as illusion, karma and the nidanas). I judge that she is best used as a witness to the idea of unity in nineteenth-century English, and not as an authority on any tradition.
