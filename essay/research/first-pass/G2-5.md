## 1. The picture in brief

Nobody has tested the sentence as written, because no lab manipulation is called "letting go of the tension that holds the sense of separation". What exists is five neighbouring bodies of work.

Meditation trials test a practice that overlaps with letting go. They find small prosocial effects, clearest against doing nothing, weaker against an active control, and, in the one meta-analysis that checked, close to nothing when the teacher was not also an author. Studies of mystical-type experience test the wall thinning for a few hours and find lasting self-reported and observer-reported change, including in prosocial attitudes, in small, selected, hard-to-blind samples. Self-other overlap research shows felt oneness predicts helping, mostly of close others, and the causal reading is disputed. Awe studies show a small-self induction can raise generosity, with uncertain replication, and acute shared stress can also raise giving, which blocks the lazy version "tension always reduces giving". Older helping research (hurry, personal distress, felt security) gives the cleanest support for the mechanism: less distress and more felt safety make people attend to someone else and help.

Verdict: a fair hypothesis with indirect support and two real qualifications. Effects are small and depend on who runs the study and on the control group. And some loosening (less guilt, an independent self-view) lowered reparation and giving in the lab. The teachers' order has no direct research counterpart; the nearest is Eisenberg's sequence from regulation to sympathy to helping. The essay's "it is small" about Cialdini is not something I can confirm: the dispute is about interpretation, not size.

## 2. Findings

### A. Meditation and prosociality

1. Condon, Desbordes, Miller and DeSteno 2013.
Claim: eight weeks of meditation made people likelier to help a stranger in pain. Who: "Meditation increases compassionate responses to suffering", Psychological Science 24(10) [M]. Sample: about 39 people, a course or a wait list; compassion-based and mindfulness-based courses were pooled [M; split U]. Result: in a waiting room, actors ignored a third actor on crutches in pain; about half of the trained gave up the seat, about one in six of the wait list [M gist; U on 50 and 16 percent]. Replication: the same lab's 2015 follow-up (Lim, Condon and DeSteno, PLoS ONE) used a short course against an active control and reported a similar gap [U]. Criticism: tiny sample, one behaviour, wait-list control, so meditation cannot be separated from attention and expectation. A co-author, Willa Miller, is a Buddhist teacher; whether she taught the course I cannot confirm, and it matters for finding 3 [U]. Traditions: Tibetan mind training and Pali loving-kindness as ancestors. Use: "one small controlled study", after checking.

2. Weng et al. 2013; Leiberg, Klimecki and Singer 2011.
Weng, Fox, Shackman and colleagues with Davidson, "Compassion training alters altruism and neural responses to suffering", Psychological Science 24(7) [M]. About 41 adults, two weeks of audio-guided practice, compassion training against reappraisal training [M gist; numbers U]. Measure: the Redistribution Game, where a bystander may spend their own money to even out an unfair split between strangers [M]. The compassion group spent more [M]; the brain findings come from small groups. Effect size not recalled. No independent replication I know of [U]. Criticism: small, and the reappraisal control may itself dampen empathy, inflating the gap [my inference, U]. Leiberg, Klimecki and Singer, "Short-term compassion training increases prosocial behavior in a newly developed prosocial game", PLoS ONE 6(3) [M]: brief compassion training against memory training; the compassion group helped more, partly through positive affect [M gist; N U, a few dozen]. Klimecki and colleagues 2013 (Cerebral Cortex) found compassion training raised positive affect while empathy training raised negative affect [M]. Use: the empathy-versus-compassion split fits the essay. Feeling the other's pain as your own distress is the wall defending itself; warmth without distress is the wall relaxed.

3. Kreplin, Farias and Brazil 2018.
"The limited prosocial effects of meditation: A systematic review and meta-analysis", Scientific Reports 8 [M]. As I recall: about 22 studies of meditation with empathy, compassion, connectedness or aggression as outcomes [M gist; count U]. Effects were small to moderate against passive controls, mostly absent against active controls, and the authors report the effect appeared where the teacher was also an author and not where the teacher was independent [M on direction; U on every figure]. So the answer to your check is yes as I remember it: the effect shrinks to nothing reliable when the teacher is not a co-author. They also flagged weak study quality and possible publication bias [M]. Criticism: the teacher contrast is across studies, so confounded with quality and era; the authors say so [U]. Companion: Luberto and colleagues 2018 (Mindfulness 9), small to moderate gains with low-quality evidence [U]. Use: the most important caution for Alex, who teaches the method. A candid essay says the people who report the effect most are those who teach it, and he is one.

4. Van Dam et al. 2018.
"Mind the Hype: A Critical Evaluation and Prescriptive Agenda for Research on Mindfulness and Meditation", Perspectives on Psychological Science 13(1) [M; pages U]. Argument: "mindfulness" names too many things; trials are small and often lack active controls; harms are under-reported; commerce and media run ahead of data. It does not call meditation useless; it asks for definitions, controls and harm reporting [M]. It does not test prosociality, but names the weaknesses of findings 1 to 3. Companion: Lindahl and colleagues 2017 (PLoS ONE) on difficult meditation experiences [M]. Use: cite once, as the reason the essay says "observation, not a study".

5. Where calming can reduce giving.
Hafenbrack, LaPalme and Solal 2022, "Mindfulness meditation reduces guilt and prosocial reparation", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology [M title; authors U]: brief mindfulness lowered guilt about a remembered wrong and lowered willingness to make amends in several experiments [M gist; numbers U]. Poulin and colleagues 2021, "Minding your own business? Mindfulness decreases prosocial behavior for those with independent self-construals", Psychological Science [M]: brief mindfulness lowered helping among people with independent self-views, not among interdependent ones [M gist]. Opposite case: Berry and colleagues 2018, brief mindfulness raised help for an ostracised stranger through empathic concern [U]. Replication: unknown for all three. Use: the honest qualification for "saying sorry first". Guilt is a motor of apology, and a calmer person who keeps a separate self-view was not made more generous. That second result is close to Alex's own model: the wall, not the calm, does the work.

### B. Mystical-type experience and lasting change

6. Pahnke 1966 and Doblin 1991.
Pahnke and Richards, "Implications of LSD and experimental mysticism", Journal of Religion and Health 5, 1966, from Pahnke's 1963 Harvard thesis [M]. The Good Friday Experiment, Marsh Chapel, Boston, 1962: 20 divinity students, double-blind, psilocybin against nicotinic acid (doses about 30 mg and 200 mg, U) [M gist]. Scoring used nine categories from Stace: unity, transcendence of time and space, positive mood, sacredness, noetic quality, paradoxicality, ineffability, transiency, persisting positive change [M]. The psilocybin group scored higher on most, at the session and at six months [M]. Doblin, "Pahnke's Good Friday Experiment: a long-term follow-up and methodological critique", Journal of Transpersonal Psychology 23(1), 1991 [M]: he traced nearly all participants 25 years on; the psilocybin group still called the session a high point of their spiritual lives [M gist; counts U]. He also criticised the blinding (the niacin flush faded), guides who could tell, and an unreported case of a psilocybin subject who needed an antipsychotic injection [M gist]. Effect size: large on the questionnaire. Traditions: Stace's list drew on Christian, Hindu, Buddhist and Sufi texts; the ninth category is a James-style fruits test. Use: historic anchor, with the Doblin caveat.

7. Griffiths et al. 2006, 2008, 2011.
Griffiths, Richards, McCann and Jesse, "Psilocybin can occasion mystical-type experiences having substantial and sustained personal meaning and spiritual significance", Psychopharmacology 187, 2006 [M]. 36 hallucinogen-naive adults interested in spirituality, double-blind crossover, psilocybin against methylphenidate [M gist; N U]. About 60 percent met a "complete" mystical criterion [U]. At two months about two thirds ranked the session among the five most meaningful of their lives [M gist; U]. Community observers (friends and family) rated positive change in attitudes and behaviour [M]. The 2008 follow-up at 14 months kept most of this (roughly 58 percent top-five meaningful, 64 percent a moderate or greater gain in well-being, U). The 2011 study: 18 adults, four doses, effects rose with dose [M gist]. Replication: the acute mystical effect has been reproduced at other sites (NYU, Imperial, Zurich) [M]. Criticism: self-selected spiritual volunteers, functional unblinding, retrospective self-report, much from one group. Use: the observer ratings are the nearest thing to the communal confirmation of finding 20.

8. Griffiths et al. 2018; MacLean et al. 2011.
"Psilocybin-occasioned mystical-type experience in combination with meditation and other spiritual practices produces enduring positive changes in psychological functioning and in trait measures of prosocial attitudes and behaviors", Journal of Psychopharmacology 32(1) [M; pages U]. About 75 volunteers, randomised to groups differing in dose and in support for meditation and spiritual practice, followed for months [M gist; N U]. Reported: the high-dose, high-support group showed larger and more durable gains, including prosocial attitudes and behaviours on participant and community-observer measures, and the strength of the acute mystical experience predicted gains [M gist; scales U]. This is the closest published test of the essay's sentence, and it says the unity experience matters and so does the practice after it. MacLean, Johnson and Griffiths 2011 found mystical experience led to a lasting rise in the trait Openness, not obviously generosity [M gist]. Criticism: small selected groups; blinding is hard; researcher allegiance to the compounds parallels the teacher problem [M]. Use: the nearest published test, with limits.

9. The Mystical Experience Questionnaire, Hood's scale, Stace.
Barrett, Johnson and Griffiths 2015, "Validation of the revised Mystical Experience Questionnaire in experimental sessions with psilocybin", Journal of Psychopharmacology 29 [M]: MEQ30, four factors (mystical, positive mood, transcendence of time and space, ineffability) [M gist]. Roots: Pahnke's questionnaire, from Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960), with extrovertive and introvertive types [M]. Hood, "The construction and preliminary validation of a measure of reported mystical experience", Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion 14(1), 1975: 32 items, factors introvertive, extrovertive, interpretation [M]; Hood and colleagues 2001 confirmed the structure in the United States and Iran [M gist]. A 1996 Hood item may be the textbook The Psychology of Religion, 2nd edition [U]. Criticism: the instruments embed Stace's common-core thesis, which Katz (1978) and Proudfoot (1985) denied and Forman (1990) defended; items cue the answer. A structure that replicates across cultures shows people answer in parallel, not that they refer to the same thing (G1-1 reached the same point). Use: the MEQ is not independent support for "the traditions say the same".

10. Ego dissolution and self-transcendence.
Nour, Evans, Nutt and Carhart-Harris 2016, "Ego-Dissolution and Psychedelics: Validation of the Ego-Dissolution Inventory", Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 10 [M]: eight items on loss of self-boundary and unity; scores track dose and correlate with MEQ-type scores [M gist]. Letheby and Gerrans 2017, "Self unbound", Neuroscience of Consciousness [M]: ego dissolution as loss of the self-model; it does not show the self is absent or oneness true. Milliere 2017, "Looking for the self", Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 11 [M]: it is several distinct alterations, and the philosophy built on it is over-read. Yaden and colleagues 2017, "The varieties of self-transcendent experience", Review of General Psychology 21(2): transient states of decreased self-salience and increased connectedness [M]. Traditions: Rolland's "oceanic feeling" (1927) and Freud's reply (1930) [M]; advaita; Buddhist no-self. Use: where the essay's computer picture (the wall as a model) meets measurement; it fits either reading, and the essay should say so, as Part two does with Metzinger.

11. Forstmann and Sagioglou 2017.
"Lifetime experience with (classic) psychedelics predicts pro-environmental behavior through an increase in nature relatedness", Journal of Psychopharmacology 31(8) [M]. A large online survey, about 1,500 respondents [U]: lifetime use predicted self-reported pro-environmental behaviour, mediated by nature relatedness, openness controlled [M gist]. Criticism: cross-sectional, self-selected, self-reported; the reverse arrow cannot be excluded. Use: "the border includes the world" is measurable, with a weak design.

### C. Self-other overlap

12. Aron, Aron and Smollan 1992.
"Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale and the structure of interpersonal closeness", JPSP 63(4), 596 to 612 [M]. One item: seven pairs of circles with growing overlap, labelled self and other [M]. Test-retest about .8 over two weeks, correlation with longer closeness scales, and prediction of whether a relationship lasted three months [M gist; figures U]. Davis, Conklin, Smith and Luce 1996 (JPSP) showed perspective taking raised self-other overlap [M gist]. Criticism: self-report about a named person, nearly always a close one. Use: one picture, ten seconds, which suits "an experiment you can run on yourself".

13. The Cialdini, Batson and Maner dispute.
Cialdini, Brown, Lewis, Luce and Neuberg 1997, JPSP 73(3), 481 to 494 (in the essay's reference list; the rest is from memory). As I recall: with hypothetical and real helping situations, once oneness (the IOS plus a "we-ness" item) was in the model, empathic concern lost its separate power to predict helping and oneness kept it; oneness was higher for kin and close others [M gist; study count and N U]. The same issue carried Batson, Sager, Garst, Kang, Rubchinsky and Dawson, "Is empathy-induced helping due to self-other merging?" (495 to 509) [M]: experiments that manipulated empathy found helping rose without mediation by merging, and they argued the oneness measure was confounded with closeness [M gist]. A reply by Neuberg and colleagues followed [M; title U]. Maner, Luce, Neuberg, Cialdini, Brown and Sagarin 2002, "The effects of perspective taking on motivations for helping: still no evidence for altruism", PSPB 28 [M]: with oneness and sadness controlled, empathy did not independently produce helping [M gist]. Batson's Altruism in Humans (2011) keeps empathy-altruism and finds merging unsupported [M]. Status: unresolved, two camps reading the same regressions; a dispute about why empathy leads to helping, which never tested letting go. I know no effect size, so "and it is small" is unchecked [U]. A usable point: on Cialdini's reading the line between selfish and selfless giving blurs, which is the axiom's point; Batson answers that merging would still be egoism [M].

14. Oneness measures and the cost side.
Garfield, Drwecki, Moore, Kortenkamp and Gracz 2014, "The Oneness Beliefs Scale: Connecting spirituality with pro-environmental behavior", JSSR 53(2) [M]: the scale predicted pro-environmental attitudes and behaviour beyond general religiosity [M gist; numbers U]. McFarland, Webb and Brown 2012, "All humanity is my ingroup", JPSP 103(5) [M]: identification with all humanity predicted concern for and giving to distant strangers beyond community and national identification [M gist]. Swann and colleagues 2009 (identity fusion, JPSP) [M]: fusion with a group predicts willingness to sacrifice for it, the cost side, since group-scale oneness can raise hostility to outsiders [M gist]. Replication of each: unknown. Qualifier: evidence for generosity is strong for close and in-group others, thin for strangers, absent for "everything". Use: claim no more than "people who feel more connected show more care, and the direction of cause is open".

### D. The small self and generosity

15. Piff et al. 2015, with Bai 2017 and Yaden 2016.
Piff, Dietze, Feinberg, Stancato and Keltner, "Awe, the small self, and prosocial behavior", JPSP 108(6) [M]. Five studies [U]: dispositional awe predicted generosity in an economic game; a minute looking up at tall eucalyptus trees rather than a building led people to pick up more spilled pens; awe videos raised generosity and ethical choices; a feeling-small measure mediated [M gist]. Samples about a hundred each [U]. Bai and colleagues 2017 found awe shrinks the self and raises collective engagement in the United States and China [M gist]. Yaden and colleagues 2016 analysed astronauts' "overview effect" reports [M]. Replication: no large preregistered replication of the pen study that I know of [U]; brief inductions that change later behaviour replicated poorly in the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 project (about a third of 100 studies, U), which is context, not a verdict on Piff. Qualifier: a transient induction is not habitual tension, though "small self" is the mediator the essay's border needs. Use: pair with the arrow's space picture.

16. von Dawans et al. 2012.
von Dawans, Fischbacher, Kirschbaum, Fehr and Heinrichs, "The social dimension of stress reactivity: acute stress increases prosocial behavior in humans", Psychological Science 23(6) [M; title U]. Men took the group Trier Social Stress Test (speech and arithmetic before a panel) and then played trust, sharing and ultimatum-type games; stressed men were more trusting, trustworthy and sharing toward group members [M gist; N and effect sizes U, tens of participants]. Frame: "tend and befriend" (Taylor and colleagues 2000) [M]. Later work is mixed: some studies find stress raises giving in men, others no change or a fall, with sex, timing and target mattering [U]. Replication of this exact result: unknown. Qualifier: acute shared stress is not the habitual bracing of the essay. It contradicts the lazy sentence "tension always reduces giving". Use: if the essay says tension, say chronic and private.

17. Older helping work: hurry, distress, felt security, mood.
Darley and Batson 1973, "From Jerusalem to Jericho", JPSP 27(1) [M]: about 40 seminary students sent across campus to give a talk; those told they were late helped a slumped man far less than those with time (about 10 percent against about 63, U), and the talk's topic, the Good Samaritan or not, made no reliable difference [M gist]. Fits: mild pressure removes helping, and being told what to value does not restore it ("without anyone telling them to be"). Criticism: small, one setting, situationist over-reading [U]. Batson, O'Quin, Fultz, Vanderplas and Isen 1983 (JPSP 45): people high in personal distress helped less when escape was easy; empathic people helped either way [M gist]. Eisenberg and Fabes 1990 (Motivation and Emotion 14): better emotion regulation goes with sympathy rather than personal distress, and with helping [M gist]. Mikulincer, Shaver, Gillath and Nitzberg 2005, "Attachment, caregiving, and altruism: boosting attachment security increases compassion and helping", JPSP 89(5) [M]: priming felt security raised compassion and willingness to help, partly by lowering personal distress [M gist; N U]. Priming methods have a weak record after 2012, so a lead only [U]. Counterweight: negative-state relief (Cialdini, Darby and Vincent 1973) says sad people help to repair their own mood, so repair it another way and they help less [M]. Use: felt security is the best analogue of loosened tension and the cleanest support for the mechanism.

### E. Order of change, markers, comparers

18. The order: listening, giving, clearer perception.
I know no study that tracked this order. Nearest pieces. (a) Eisenberg's sequence, regulation then sympathy then helping [M; a concept, not a longitudinal meditator study]; it matches "attention leaves the self and lands on someone else". (b) The ReSource Project (Singer and colleagues, Leipzig), nine months in modules ordered presence, affect, perspective [M gist]; the order is designed, not observed. (c) Vago and Silbersweig 2012, the S-ART framework (self-awareness, self-regulation, self-transcendence) [M]; a theory. (d) The Theravada ten fetters: the stream-enterer drops self-view, doubt and ritual clinging; ill-will weakens at the second stage and goes at the third [M]. That is a tradition's order, self-view first, ill-will later, quotable as the tradition's claim and not as evidence. (e) Ego-development stage research (Loevinger, Cook-Greuter), with reports from a Transcendental Meditation group, so an allegiance caution [U]. For "clearer perception": Mascaro and colleagues 2013 (SCAN), compassion training raised empathic accuracy, small N [M gist]; Kang, Gray and Dovidio 2014, six weeks of loving-kindness lowered implicit bias against outgroups versus a discussion control [M gist]; Desbordes and colleagues 2012, training changed amygdala response to emotional pictures [M gist]. None is an order. Use: call the order the teachers' observation, and name Eisenberg's as the nearest research sequence.

19. The essay's markers and their research cousins.
Options in a hard moment: Fredrickson and Branigan 2005 (Cognition and Emotion 19), positive film clips widened the list of actions people wanted to take (separate from the retracted "positivity ratio" paper) [M]; Easterbrook 1959, arousal narrows cue use [M]; Greenberg, Reiner and Meiran 2012 (PLoS ONE), meditators less rigid on a water-jar task [M gist; design U]. Length of a reaction: Davidson's "affective chronometry" names rise time, peak and recovery [M]; Jain and colleagues 2007, mindfulness reduced rumination against relaxation [M gist]. Saying sorry first: Breines and Chen 2012 (PSPB 38), a self-compassion induction raised motivation to make amends [M gist]; Leary and colleagues 2007, self-compassion lowered defensiveness [M]; Crocker, Niiya and Mischkowski 2008, affirming values reduced defensiveness through other-directed feeling [M gist]. Hafenbrack (finding 5) is the counter. Giving unasked: Crocker and Canevello 2008 (JPSP 95), in a semester-long roommate study, compassionate goals predicted more support given and received, self-image goals the reverse [M gist; N U]; Bockler, Tusche and Singer 2016 show altruistic, norm-driven and strategic prosociality are separable [M]. Use: each marker has a researched cousin; "saying sorry first" is where the evidence is mixed.

20. The comparers who tie measures to the traditions.
James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902): the test by fruits. He refuses to judge religious states by origin and uses immediate luminousness, philosophical reasonableness and moral helpfulness (Lecture I) [M, paraphrase]. His saintliness lectures (about XI to XV) list marks including a feeling of a wider life than selfish little interests, elation as the outlines of confining selfhood melt, and a shift to loving affections, and warn of excess [M gist; quote only after checking]. Pahnke's ninth category and Griffiths' observer ratings are modern versions. Wilber's three strands, injunction, apprehension and communal confirmation, in Eye to Eye (1983) and later [M; locator unknown]: do the practice, look at what appears, check with others who did the same. That maps onto line 157's self-experiment. Critics say the confirmation comes from the already trained, so is circular (Ferrer 2002) [U]. Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957), ranked experiences by moral and theistic quality [M gist]. Huston Smith, Cleansing the Doors of Perception (2000), paraphrased: drugs can occasion religious experience, which is not yet a religious life [M; wording U]. Tradition-internal fruit tests: Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65) [M]; Matthew 7:16 [M]; Bhagavad Gita 12.13, the devotee hates no being and is friendly and compassionate [M gist; verse U]. Theosophy: the first object of the Society, a nucleus of universal brotherhood [M]. Use: James is the cleanest bridge, since the essay already trusts fruits.

21. Verdict on the sentence, study by study.
Supports, indirectly: Condon, Weng, Leiberg; Griffiths 2006, 2008, 2018; Aron and Cialdini; Piff; Darley and Batson, Batson 1983, Eisenberg, Mikulincer (the best mechanism evidence); Forstmann, Garfield, McFarland (correlational).
Qualifies: Kreplin, Van Dam, the Batson and Maner replies, von Dawans, Swann, the MEQ critics.
Contradicts the plain version: Hafenbrack (less guilt, less reparation), Poulin (calm without interdependence lowered helping), negative-state relief.
Overall: letting go should make a person more available to others, and the lab record shows it mainly where letting go means felt safety, compassion training or belonging, and not where it means plain calm.

## 4. How they compare

By instrument. Stace listed traits he found in mystics of several traditions. Pahnke made the list a questionnaire, Hood a scale, Griffiths' group the MEQ. A score measures how near a report is to Stace's list. Cross-cultural checks show the scale works across languages, not that the experiences are one.

By fruits. James judged states by what they did to a life; Pahnke's last category and Griffiths' friend-and-family ratings put numbers on it; Smith and Zaehner ask whether a drug experience is a lived mysticism.

By method. Wilber's three strands make the claim checkable by anyone who does the practice; what is compared is what the practice shows, not what the texts say.

By borrowed practice. Loving-kindness from metta, compassion courses from Tibetan mind training, MBSR from Zen and vipassana, stripped of doctrine and tested by function, which sidesteps whether the doctrines match.

Differences are explained four ways. Stace: same core, different interpretation. Zaehner: different experiences, different moral and theistic quality. Katz: concepts shape experience from the start, so no pure core. Scientists: dose, setting, expectation. Alex's "different words, same things" sits with Stace against Katz, and the essay can say so.

## 5. What scholars say

Katz (1978), Proudfoot (1985), Sharf (1995) and Taves (2009) hold that concept shapes experience and the unity core is partly a modern Western construction; Forman (1990) and Hick reply [M]. Psychology of religion treats the MEQ and Hood scales as reliable but Western-built [M]. Meditation research has its own correctives: Van Dam, Kreplin, and Sharf's "Is mindfulness Buddhist?" (2015) [M]. Psychedelic trials are criticised for functional unblinding, expectancy and researcher allegiance [M gist]. On helping, the field mostly accepts that empathy leads to helping and disputes why [M].

## 6. Leads for Alex's essay

1. Lines 153 to 157 need one sentence that tells the truth about the lab record. Candidate, in his voice, to be checked: "The lab record is thinner than the claim. In controlled trials meditation raises helping a little compared with doing nothing, less compared with another training, and the one meta-analysis that looked found most of the effect in studies where the teacher was also an author. I teach this, so weigh me accordingly." Needs: Kreplin 2018 wording and figures.
2. Line 157, "and it is small": replace with "and is disputed", or check Cialdini's effects. As written, the size claim is unsupported.
3. Name the nearest tests of the mechanism: hurry cut helping among seminary students whatever they had just been told (Darley and Batson 1973), and priming felt security raised compassion and help (Mikulincer and colleagues 2005). They test pressure and safety, the essay's tension, better than meditation trials do.
4. Use Griffiths 2018 as the closest published test of felt unity leading to lasting prosocial change, with its limits (small, selected, self and friends as raters). It also says practice after the experience matters, which sets up Part three.
5. Specify "saying sorry first". Brief mindfulness lowered guilt and reparation (Hafenbrack 2022), and calm without interdependence lowered helping (Poulin 2021). Alex means not defending the self, not feeling less guilt; the essay should say which.
6. Build the self-experiment on Wilber's three strands: practise, note what you notice, ask someone who knows you whether it shows. The last step is Griffiths' observer rating in miniature.
7. Bridge to the arrow: the astronaut "overview effect" (Yaden 2016) is a documented report of the border thinning from height.
8. Say plainly that lab work thins the border by drug, awe or training and does not test tension itself. The closest tension measures are personal distress, felt security and defensiveness.
9. Check first, in order: Kreplin 2018; Hafenbrack 2022 and Poulin 2021; Griffiths 2018; Cialdini 1997, Batson 1997, Maner 2002; Darley and Batson 1973; Mikulincer 2005; von Dawans 2012; Piff 2015; Condon 2013.
