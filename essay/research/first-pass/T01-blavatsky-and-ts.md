## 1. The picture in brief

The TS was founded in New York on 17 November 1875 by Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge and a few others, out of the Spiritualist scene. Its first stated aim was research into the hidden laws of nature. Over about twenty years the order of the aims flipped. Brotherhood came first, the study of religions, philosophies and sciences second, the investigation of unexplained laws and latent powers third. The move to India in 1879 gave the comparing an Eastern direction.

Blavatsky's two big books are comparative encyclopedias. Isis Unveiled (1877) is mostly Western (Hermetic, Kabbalistic, Neoplatonic). The Secret Doctrine (1888) is mostly Indian and Buddhist in vocabulary. Her explanation for why religions agree is historical: one archaic Wisdom-Religion, kept by initiates, from which the outer religions descend and have decayed. Her explanation for difference is corruption, literal reading and symbol. Her authority rested on hidden Masters, and that is the weak point. The Hodgson report (1885) called her phenomena fraud, Vernon Harrison's 1986 paper attacked the report, and historians now mostly set the question aside and study what the movement did.

Two things matter for Alex. First, the Secret Doctrine opens with three propositions (one principle; the universe appearing and vanishing in cycles; the identity of all souls with the universal soul and their pilgrimage under karma). That has the shape of his axiom and its consequences, but it is given as received teaching, not as logic. Second, he can say plainly how he differs: he explains agreement by logic, not by a lost original or hidden teachers.

## 2. Findings

1. Her life before 1873

Claim: Born Helena von Hahn on 12 August 1831 (new style) at Ekaterinoslav, Russian Empire (now Dnipro). Father an army officer of German descent, mother the novelist Helena Fadeyeva, who died young. In 1848 (some accounts 1849) she married the much older Nikifor Blavatsky and left within months. She later claimed about a decade of travel from the early 1850s, including Tibet and study under Masters. Almost none of it is documented.
Who: Meade (1980), Washington (1993), Lachman (2012), Cranston (1993).
Words: paraphrase. Parallels cited: none.
Tier: [M] for dates and family; [M] that the travels are undocumented; the first meeting with Master Morya in London in 1851 is her own account [M].
Use: One sentence at most. "A Russian-born writer and medium who said she was taught by hidden teachers" is accurate and does not endorse the teachers.

2. America and Spiritualism, 1873 to 1875

Claim: She reached New York in July 1873. In 1874, at the Eddy farmhouse in Chittenden, Vermont, she met Olcott (1832 to 1907), lawyer, Civil War investigator and journalist, who was writing up the Eddy seances (People from the Other World, 1875). She presented herself as a medium but said the visible spirits were not the dead. She later told correspondents that the Masters had sent her to prove real phenomena and correct Spiritualist doctrine.
Who: Olcott, Old Diary Leaves vol. 1 (1895); Lavoie (2012); Meade.
Tier: [M]. The 1875 Corson letter as the source of the "sent to America" claim is [U].
Use: Lavoie's point is worth one clause: the TS began inside Spiritualism and defined itself against it.

3. The founding, September to November 1875

Claim: On 7 September 1875 George H. Felt lectured on the lost canon of proportion of the Egyptians in Blavatsky's rooms. Olcott proposed a society for such study. On 8 September the name Theosophical Society was chosen (credited to Charles Sotheran). Officers were elected on 30 October. The inaugural meeting and Olcott's presidential address on 17 November 1875 is the conventional founding date. Olcott was President, Blavatsky Corresponding Secretary, Judge counsel.
Who: Olcott, Old Diary Leaves vol. 1; Campbell (1980).
Tier: [M] for dates and Olcott as President; [U] for other officers and the name credit.
Use: "Founded in New York in 1875" and "17 November" are safe. The draft's line that Judge was among the founders is right.

4. The first aim, 1875

Claim: The 1875 Preamble had one aim: to collect and diffuse knowledge of the laws that govern the universe. It explained the name as seeking knowledge of the supreme power and higher spirits, and hoped that by going deeper into ancient esoteric philosophies than modern science had gone, members could find proof of an "unseen universe" (the title of an 1875 science book by Balfour Stewart and P. G. Tait). I recall no brotherhood and no comparing of religions in it.
Who: the 1875 Preamble and By-laws, reprinted in Old Diary Leaves vol. 1; Campbell.
Words: recalled near-verbatim, not for quotation.
Tier: [M] for the single aim; [M, about 70 percent] for the explanation of the name.
Use: Alex's note that comparative study came into the wording later is right. The TS did not start as a comparative society.

5. Isis Unveiled (1877), the first comparing

Claim: Her first book, two volumes (Science, then Theology), attacked materialist science and the Church and argued that old Hermetic, Kabbalistic and Neoplatonic traditions held one suppressed knowledge. In the Preface she calls the work, as I recall near-verbatim, a plea for recognising the Hermetic philosophy, the anciently universal Wisdom-Religion, as the only possible key to the Absolute in science and theology. Examples are mostly Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, Gnostic and Christian, with Brahmanism and Buddhism as older versions of Christian doctrines.
Who: Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (J. W. Bouton, New York, 1877), Preface.
Parallels cited: Hermes and Egypt, Pythagoras, Plato and the Neoplatonists, Kabbalah, Gnostics, Brahmanism, Buddhism.
Tier: [M] that the Preface says this; [M] on the wording.
Use: Her aim in her own words. Check the printed Preface before quoting (public domain). She names it Wisdom-Religion; the Traditionalists later say primordial tradition.

6. India and the Eastern turn, 1878 to 1882

Claim: Blavatsky and Olcott left New York in December 1878 and reached Bombay in February 1879, after an alliance with Dayananda Saraswati's Arya Samaj (broken in 1882). In October 1879 she began the journal The Theosophist. On 25 May 1880 in Galle, Ceylon, she and Olcott took the Buddhist five precepts in public. Olcott wrote A Buddhist Catechism (1881) and worked for Sinhalese Buddhist schools. Headquarters moved to Adyar, Madras, in December 1882. Blavatsky became an American citizen in 1878.
Who: Olcott, Old Diary Leaves vols. 2 and 3; Prothero (1996).
Tier: [M] for the years; [U] for exact days other than 25 May 1880.
Use: Explains why Theosophy's vocabulary (karma, mahatma, devachan) is Sanskrit and why Isis (1877) is more Hermetic than later Theosophy.

7. The Objects: how the wording changed

Claim, as I recall the stages:
(a) 1875: one aim, knowledge of the laws governing the universe.
(b) About 1878 to 1882, in Bombay and Madras, a list of three: form the nucleus of a universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, colour or creed; a study aim naming Eastern literatures, religions and sciences (the form "Aryan and other Eastern" was in use in the 1880s); investigate unexplained laws of nature and the psychical powers latent in man. Brotherhood moves to first place, which fits the Mahatma Letters (finding 9).
(c) 1896: the second Object is reworded to the comparative form: encourage the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science. The first by then names race, creed, sex, caste or colour.
(d) Minor later edits (I recall 1949 and 1975) leave the present form: nucleus of the universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour; encourage the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science; investigate unexplained laws of nature and the powers latent in man.
Who: TS Adyar, the Society's Rules and its page on the Objects; Campbell; Santucci.
Words: recalled, not for quotation.
Tier: [M] for stages (a), (b) and the 1896 reform; [U] for exact years in (b) and (d) and for when "sex" and "caste" were added.
Use: Do not quote "comparative study of religions, philosophies and sciences" as the Society's text. The present wording is "comparative religion, philosophy and science". Also true: membership requires agreement only with the first Object, and a freedom of thought statement (1920s, I think [U]) says no teacher or writer, Blavatsky included, can impose views on members. That fits "examples, not a proof".

8. The motto

Claim: "There is no religion higher than truth" translates the Sanskrit satyat nasti paro dharmah, the family motto of the Maharajas of Benares, adopted by the TS around 1880 and set on its seal. I believe it also stands on the title page of The Secret Doctrine with that attribution.
Who: TS Adyar; the Secret Doctrine title page.
Parallels cited: dharma is wider than "religion" (law, duty, right order), so a specialist could say the English narrows it.
Tier: [M] for origin and approximate date (matches the repo notes); [U] for the title page of the first edition.
Use: It puts truth above any one tradition, which suits Alex's opening ("not religious"). Say "around 1880", not a closer date.

9. The Masters and the Mahatma Letters

Claim: From 1880 to 1884 letters reached A. P. Sinnett, editor of The Pioneer in Allahabad, and A. O. Hume (later a founder of the Indian National Congress), said to come from two Masters, Morya and Koot Hoomi, by non-ordinary delivery at Adyar and Simla. They were published in 1923 (A. T. Barker). Sinnett's The Occult World (1881) and Esoteric Buddhism (1883) spread the teaching: seven principles of man, planetary chains, kamaloka, devachan, reincarnation as the rule. In 1875 and 1876 the Masters were Egyptian (the Brotherhood of Luxor, names such as Tuitit Bey), later replaced by Tibetan and Indian ones. Theosophists cite a long K.H. letter to Hume (1880, numbered 10 in Barker's edition) for the claim that the "Chiefs" wanted a real universal brotherhood started.
Who: Barker (1923); Sinnett; Godwin (1994); Campbell; Johnson (1994).
Tier: [M] for people and books and for the two sets of Masters; [U] for the letter number and wording.
Use: Do not cite the Letters as evidence. They are Theosophy's own account of why brotherhood is Object one. Two numbering systems exist (Barker 1923; Hao Chin's chronological edition, 1993), so any locator must name the edition.

10. The Coulomb affair, Hodgson (1885), Harrison (1986)

Claim: In 1884, while Blavatsky and Olcott were in Europe, Emma and Alexis Coulomb, her housekeeper and carpenter at Adyar, accused her of faking the Masters' letters through a hidden panel behind the shrine; the Madras Christian College Magazine printed it. The Society for Psychical Research (founded 1882) sent Richard Hodgson to India (late 1884 to early 1885). His report, SPR Proceedings vol. 3 (December 1885), said the letters were hers and the phenomena fraud. The committee's summary called her, as I recall, one of the most accomplished, ingenious and interesting impostors in history. She left India in March 1885 and did not sue. In 1986 Vernon Harrison, a document examiner (and, I think, a former president of the Royal Photographic Society [U]), published "J'Accuse" in the SPR's own Journal (vol. 53), arguing that Hodgson's handwriting evidence and method were flawed. A fuller book followed in 1997. The SPR did not retract the report.
Who: Hodgson (1885); Harrison (1986, 1997); Lachman (2012) for a balanced reading.
Words: paraphrase; the "impostors" sentence is [M] and not for quotation.
Tier: [M]. The SPR's own 1986 statement is unverified and known to me mainly through Theosophical sources: do not use it.
Use: One clause: the SPR judged her phenomena fraudulent in 1885, and a later paper in its own journal challenged that. That is the line the repo notes already settled on.

11. London, 1887 to 1891, and the three late books

Claim: She left India ill in 1885, lived in Wurzburg and Ostend, and settled in London in 1887. She founded the magazine Lucifer and the Blavatsky Lodge, and published The Secret Doctrine (1888, two volumes, subtitled The Synthesis of Science, Religion and Philosophy), The Key to Theosophy (1889) and The Voice of the Silence (1889). Annie Besant reviewed the Secret Doctrine, met her in 1889 and joined. Blavatsky died of influenza in London on 8 May 1891; Theosophists keep 8 May as White Lotus Day.
Who: Cranston; Lachman; Meade.
Tier: [M].
Use: The Secret Doctrine's subtitle is her own name for her method: synthesis.

12. The Esoteric Section

Claim: In October 1888 she opened an inner circle, first the Esoteric School of Theosophy and by about 1890 the Esoteric Section, bound by a pledge and under her as Head, to give students teaching beyond the open Society. After 1891 Besant led it, a main cause of later splits.
Who: Campbell; Cranston.
Tier: [M] for 1888 and the Head role; [U] for exact names and the date of the change.
Use: Only if Alex says there was an inner school. It qualifies "creedless": the open Society had no creed, the inner section had a pledge and a teacher.

13. The Secret Doctrine's three propositions: an axiom with consequences

Claim: The Proem to the Secret Doctrine states three fundamental propositions, as I recall: (1) an omnipresent, eternal, boundless, immutable Principle beyond thought; (2) the universe appearing and vanishing in cycles, like tides or day and night; (3) the fundamental identity of all souls with the Universal Over-Soul, and for each soul an obligatory pilgrimage through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Over-Soul is Emerson's word. The structure of the whole book follows: the One, manifestation, the pilgrimage of the many, return.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, Proem.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels cited: Hindu (Brahman, days and nights of Brahma), Kabbalah (the Boundless), Buddhist cycles, Emerson's Over-Soul, the Stanzas of Dzyan (a text she said she translated; no one has found it).
Tier: [M] for structure and paraphrase.
Use: The Theosophical version of Alex's axiom. He can say Blavatsky stated a three-part principle of nearly the same shape (one whole, its cycles of appearing, the soul's pilgrimage and return), and that she gave it as received teaching while he derives consequences and says they fit.

14. Karma, rebirth and the self

Claim: Karma, for her, is a law of harmony, not of punishment. In the Secret Doctrine (vol. 1, near the end, perhaps p. 643 of the 1888 edition [U]) she says, as I recall near-verbatim, that the one decree of Karma is absolute harmony in matter and in spirit, so it is not Karma that rewards or punishes but we who reward or punish ourselves, by working with or against Nature's laws. On rebirth, Isis Unveiled (1877) rejected Kardec's Spiritist rebirth and allowed a return to earth only in exceptional cases. By the Secret Doctrine and the Key (1889) the Ego (individuality) reincarnates over many lives and the personality (the persona, the mask) does not, with kamaloka and devachan between lives. Julie Chajes (2019) argues she held two doctrines in succession.
Who: Blavatsky (1877, 1888, 1889); Chajes, Recycled Lives (Oxford University Press, 2019; title approximate).
Parallels cited: Hindu karma and samsara, Buddhist kamma, Plato's Phaedo and Republic, Pythagoras.
Tier: [M]; the page locator is [U].
Use: Her statement that we reward or punish ourselves sits beside "something I did to another part of myself" (the fingers). Two cautions: her Ego versus personality is not Alex's ego, and she changed her view on rebirth between 1877 and 1888, so "Blavatsky taught reincarnation" needs a date.

15. The Key to Theosophy: the plain statement of aim

Claim: The Key (1889) is a question-and-answer book. It explains the word as Greek theosophia, divine wisdom, used (she says) by the third-century Alexandrian school of Ammonius Saccas and the Philalethians. She describes their aim, and the modern TS's, as reconciling all religions, sects and nations under a common system of ethics based on eternal verities. She also says Theosophy is not a religion but divine knowledge, and the Society is not a sect.
Who: Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy (1889), the opening chapter on the meaning of the name.
Words: the core phrase is recalled near-verbatim [M]; do not quote until checked.
Parallels cited: Ammonius Saccas (teacher of Plotinus), the Neoplatonists, Buddhism, Hinduism, Kabbalah.
Tier: [M].
Use: Her clearest plain statement of the comparing aim, and the goal is ethics, not only doctrine. Scholars note that almost nothing certain is known of Ammonius's teaching and that the picture of him reconciling all faiths is later. Treat the lineage as her story.

16. The Voice of the Silence (1889): the veil and the way

Claim: A short devotional work in three Fragments, presented as translated from a Tibetan Book of the Golden Precepts. It contrasts the path of private liberation with the path of renunciation for the world's sake (the Bodhisattva ideal). Lines she is known for, recalled near-verbatim and unverified: the mind is the great slayer of the real and the disciple must slay the slayer; do not separate your being from Being but merge the ocean in the drop and the drop in the ocean; compassion is the law of laws.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (1889), Fragments I to III.
Parallels cited: Mahayana (Bodhisattva, Arhat, Pratyekabuddha), Hindu (Atman and Brahman).
Tier: [M] for the themes; [U] for the exact wording and which Fragment holds each line.
Use: A source for the veil and for the image of ocean and drop, close to Alex's whole and its parts. The phrase "heresy of separateness" is common in Theosophical writing and often credited to her; I cannot place it [U].

17. After 1891: schisms and spread

Claim: Judge led the American section out in 1895 after charges against him (forging Master messages, said his accusers). Besant and C. W. Leadbeater built a large clairvoyant literature (later called neo-Theosophy); Leadbeater found the boy Krishnamurti in 1909, who dissolved the Order of the Star in 1929. Rudolf Steiner and the German section split off in 1912 and 1913 (Anthroposophy). Besant, Judge and Dharmapala were at the Chicago Parliament of Religions in 1893. Guenon's Le Theosophisme (1921) called it a pseudo-religion. Max Muller (1893) said there is no esoteric Buddhism.
Who: Campbell; Godwin; Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004).
Tier: [M]; Judge's exact charge [U].
Use: Keep Theosophy (her teaching) apart from the TS and from Leadbeater's later claims.

18. Reach and credit

Claim: Historians credit the TS with being among the first Western organisations to treat Asian religions as sources of truth, not as errors. In Ceylon Olcott became a Buddhist reformer; in India Theosophists helped Indian elites regain pride in their own texts. Gandhi's Autobiography (Part I, around chapter 20 [U]) says two Theosophist brothers in London gave him Edwin Arnold's Gita and urged him to read Blavatsky's Key, which made him want to read Hindu books and cleared the missionary notion that Hinduism is superstition (paraphrase). Coomaraswamy joined the TS in 1907 and later broke with it (matches file P03). Yeats joined in 1887 and left the Esoteric Section in 1890.
Who: Gandhi, Autobiography; Prothero (1996); Campbell.
Tier: [M]; chapter number [U].
Use: A bridge to the talk's Gandhi. The comparing had effects.

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

Concordance by term. She matches words across languages: Parabrahman with the Boundless and the Absolute, Maya with illusion, Atman with the Over-Soul. Antoine Faivre (Access to Western Esotericism, 1994) names this practice concordance and lists it as a secondary mark of Western esotericism [M]. The Secret Doctrine sets long passages side by side from the Puranas, the Kabbalah (the Zohar and the Chaldean Book of Numbers), Hesiod, Genesis, Egyptian and Chinese sources.

Concordance by symbol. Egg, serpent, lotus, tree and cross are read as one shared vocabulary with several levels of meaning (she speaks of keys, seven by my memory [U]). A myth that looks different in each culture is read through the key to show the same content.

Genealogy. She puts one hidden source behind everything: the archaic Wisdom-Religion of Egypt and the East, kept by initiates, taught in the Mysteries, handed through Ammonius, the Gnostics and the Kabbalists, and re-presented through the Masters. Similarity becomes evidence of descent.

Esoteric and exoteric. Every religion has an outer form for the many and an inner teaching for the few. Where religions seem to disagree, the outer forms disagree and the inner meaning agrees.

Explaining difference. Difference comes from priests' corruption and literal reading of allegory, adaptation to each people and age, a new messenger in each age (Buddha, Jesus, Krishna read as teachers of one wisdom), and lost keys.

Manner. She likens her book, following Montaigne, to a nosegay of culled flowers with only the string her own [M, near-verbatim; locator unknown]. The string, though, is the Masters' system. She does not compare to test whether the traditions agree. She compares to show that they do, and she knows the answer in advance.

Critics say the method cannot fail (disagreement is called corruption), it reads Indian and Buddhist terms through a Western occult frame (Buddhist anatta against seven principles), and it selects outer forms to suit. Defenders say it was a bold act of respect for Asian traditions in an age of missionary scorn.

For Alex the contrast helps. He explains agreement by one axiom and difference by vocabulary and by layers (public teaching, inner teaching). That avoids her weakest parts, the lost original and the corruption story, and keeps her strength: saying where terms may match.

## 5. What scholars say

Meade (Madame Blavatsky: The Woman Behind the Myth, 1980) is the sceptical biography: a gifted fraud, with a private life Theosophists contest. Washington (Madame Blavatsky's Baboon, 1993) is witty and unconvinced and treats the Society as a root of the Western guru culture. Cranston (HPB, 1993) is the sympathetic insider's life and defends the Masters. Lachman (Madame Blavatsky: The Mother of Modern Spirituality, 2012) is the balanced middle: doubtful of the phenomena, generous about her influence. K. Paul Johnson (The Masters Revealed, 1994) argues the Masters were modelled on real Indian figures; most academics do not accept his identifications. Godwin (The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994) places the TS inside older esoteric societies of the Enlightenment era. Campbell (Ancient Wisdom Revived, 1980) is the standard academic history, in phases and schisms. Hanegraaff (New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996; Esotericism and the Academy, 2012) treats Theosophy as a source of modern occultism and the New Age, an attempt to reconcile science and religion in a disenchanted age. Santucci edited Theosophical History from 1985 and is the careful archival voice; he also wrote on race in Theosophy. Lavoie (The Theosophical Society: The History of a Spiritualist Movement, 2012) argues it was more Spiritualist than it admitted. Also: Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, 2001) on how Theosophy claims authority through tradition, science and experience; Chajes (2019) on rebirth; Prothero (The White Buddhist, 1996) on Olcott. William Emmette Coleman (1895) traced much of Isis and the Secret Doctrine to nineteenth-century books; later scholars accept heavy borrowing and argue about what it proves.

A caution on race: the Secret Doctrine's root races (Lemurian, Atlantean, Aryan) carry racial hierarchies that are unacceptable now, and later occultists borrowed them (Goodrick-Clarke, The Occult Roots of Nazism, 1985 [M]).
