## 1. The picture in brief

Outside the West, "the traditions say the same thing" is older and more practical than the Renaissance and Theosophical story. It appears as a hymn line (Rigveda), a parable (Udana), scripture (Gita, Quran), state policy (Ashoka, Akbar), ritual (Gandhi's prayers, Japan's shared shrines), a doctrine of manifestations (honji suijaku, Baha'i) and a personal experiment (Ramakrishna, Dara).

Six explanations of why the forms differ keep recurring: different names for one thing; partial views of one whole; different paths to one summit; one source appearing in many forms; different levels of readiness; and going under the forms to the heart. Each is a claim by the comparer, not by the traditions compared.

Two points matter for Alex. First, almost every reconciler has a top. Dara reads Vedanta in Sufi terms. Vivekananda puts Advaita highest. Kukai puts Shingon highest. Zongmi puts the Buddhist One Mind teaching highest. The Baha'i scheme puts the latest revelation last and best. Alex's top is his axiom, and the essay can say so.

Second, the thinkers closest to his three clauses (one ground, a veil or forgetting, a return) are the metaphysicians: Ibn Arabi, the Awakening of Faith with Zongmi, Aurobindo, Wang Yangming. The tolerance voices (Akbar, Kabir, Nanak, Gandhi, Ashoka) mostly reconcile to stop violence and give an ethic, which suits his karma and fingers argument. Some keep rebirth (Sikhs, Aurobindo) and one rejects it (Baha'i), so the lifetimes step is not unanimous even among reconcilers.

## 2. Findings

1. Rigveda 1.164.46
Claim: a riddle hymn says the inspired poets speak of what is one in many ways, and lists Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, Yama and Matarishvan as names. The phrase, as remembered, is "ekam sat viprah bahudha vadanti". Sat means what is, the real.
Who: Rigveda, book 1, hymn 164, verse 46. Griffith (1896, public domain); Jamison and Brereton (2014).
Parallels: later Hindus, and Coomaraswamy (P03 reports this), cite it as a plea for tolerance.
Tier: M for the Sanskrit and locator; U for any English wording.
Use: the oldest "many names, one" line. Say it is about gods, probably fire or the sun, and that later readers made it a slogan. Max Mueller's word for the Vedic pattern was henotheism.

2. Udana 6.4, the blind men and the elephant
Claim: the Buddha tells it about quarrelling sectarians who cling to views on whether the world is eternal, whether the soul is the body, and so on. A king has blind men touch one part each; each names the elephant from that part (a pot, a winnowing basket, a plough, a granary, a pillar, a broom); they come to blows. The verse after it says some recluses and brahmins cling to their views and see only one side.
Who: Udana (Khuddaka Nikaya), chapter 6, sutta 4. Thanissaro and Ireland translate it.
Parallels: later a Jain, Hindu, Sufi and Western pluralist picture. I cannot name a Jain text that tells it [U].
Tier: M.
Use: the original point is against clinging to views on unanswerable questions, with the Buddha as the one who sees. It is not an original claim that all religions are parts of one truth. Write "the story is old and has been used in many ways".

3. Rumi: the elephant in the dark house, the grapes, Moses and the shepherd
Claim: three Masnavi stories. (a) People feel an elephant in a dark room and call it a fan, pipe, pillar or throne; a candle in each hand would end the disagreement. The sense eye is like a palm; look at the sea, not the foam. (b) Four men share one coin, a Persian, an Arab, a Turk and a Greek; they quarrel because each names a grape in his own language (angur, inab, uzum, istafil); someone who knew all four languages would reconcile them. (c) God tells Moses to leave a shepherd's naive prayer alone: each community has its own idiom, God looks at the heart, and God is not made pure by praise.
Who: Rumi, Masnavi (Nicholson's numbering). Elephant in Book III near line 1259; grapes in Book II near 3681; Moses and the shepherd in Book II near 1720 to 1820. Locators [U].
Parallels: Persians, Arabs, Turks, Greeks, Hindus (in the elephant story the exhibitors are Indians).
Tier: M for the stories; U for numbers. Nicholson's translation is old enough to be public domain, so quote it once checked.
Use: the grapes story is the cleanest "different words, one thing" parable I know. The candle and the sea and foam serve the veil. The elephant's context in Rumi is the limit of sense perception, not plurality of religions.

4. Rumi and Ibn Arabi, the religion of love
Claim: Rumi says the religion of lovers is apart from all religions and their creed is God. A famous Divan poem says he is neither Christian, Jew, Magian nor Muslim, has seen the two worlds as one, and seeks, knows, sees and calls one. Ibn Arabi says his heart has become capable of every form (pasture for gazelles, monastery for monks, temple for idols, the Kaaba, the Torah tablets, the Quran) and that he follows the religion of Love wherever its camels turn.
Who: Rumi, Masnavi II near line 1770 [U]; Divan-i Shams, in Nicholson's Selected Poems from the Divani Shamsi Tabriz (1898). Ibn Arabi, Tarjuman al-Ashwaq (c. 1214), poem 11, Nicholson 1911.
Tier: M for content; U for numbers, and U for whether the Divan poem is Rumi's own (Barks's versions are loose).
Use: both Nicholson texts are public domain and could be quoted once checked. Ibn Arabi later wrote a commentary saying the poems are mystical, which the essay should not hide.

5. Ibn Arabi: the God of beliefs, the Adam chapter, He and not He
Claim: (a) do not tie yourself to one creed and reject the rest; God is too wide for one creed, and each community worships the God of its belief; the proof text is Quran 2:115, "wherever you turn is the face of God". (b) The Fusus al-Hikam opens with the Real wishing to see the essences of the Names in a comprehensive being, the human: the One needs a mirror to know itself. (c) The Noah chapter says that describing God only as beyond the world, or only within it, limits Him; the knower holds both.
Who: Fusus al-Hikam (1229); Futuhat al-Makkiyya. Translations: Austin (1980), Chittick (1989). Locators unknown.
Tier: M for ideas; U for locators. He denied tanasukh (return into this world) as most Muslims did, and taught continual transformation after death [M, details U].
Use: (b) is the nearest classical statement of "a whole that wants to know itself cannot do it from nowhere". The rebirth point is a limit to state. The descent and ascent arcs (nuzul, su'ud) of his school fit the start and return clauses [M].

6. Quran 2:285, 5:48, 2:115
Claim: 2:285 says the believers make no distinction between any of God's messengers. 5:48 says that to each community God gave a law and a way; had He willed, He would have made one community; so compete in good works; to God is your return, and He will tell you what you differed about. 2:62 and 5:69 say that those who believe in God and the last day and do good have their reward.
Who: the Quran. Translations vary.
Tier: M.
Use: scripture-level support for "many ways, one return"; the clause "to God is your return" echoes the third axiom clause. Limits: classical commentators read these inside the Abrahamic frame, and some held 3:85 narrowed 2:62. Pluralist readers include Ibn Arabi, Rumi, Shah-Kazemi and Ayoub [M].

7. Bhagavad Gita 4.11, 7.21, 9.23
Claim: 4.11, as remembered: however people approach me, I meet them so; people follow my path in all ways. 7.21: whatever form a devotee wants to worship with faith, I make that faith steady. 9.23: devotees of other gods worship me too, but by the wrong rule (avidhipurvakam). 7.23: their fruit is limited.
Who: Bhagavad Gita (Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva).
Parallels: other gods; Vivekananda and Gandhi extend it to other religions.
Tier: M.
Use: quote 4.11 and 7.21 only if you say what the text is. It is inclusivist: other worship reaches Krishna, imperfectly. Do not write "the Gita says all religions are equal".

8. Dara Shikoh, Majma-ul-Bahrain (c. 1655) and Sirr-i Akbar (1657)
Claim: a Mughal prince (1615 to 1659), a Qadiri Sufi, held that the Sufis and the Hindu monotheists differ only in words. The title echoes Quran 18:60, where Moses reaches the meeting of the two seas and finds the one who has knowledge from God (Khidr). He lines up terms: elements, attributes, three Hindu gods against three angels, and the Upanishads as the "hidden book" of Quran 56:78 (P01 reports a verified source for the last two).
Who: Majma-ul-Bahrain; Sirr-i Akbar (about fifty Upanishads into Persian); Mukalama-i Baba Lal wa Dara Shikoh, his dialogues with a Hindu ascetic (c. 1653 [M]). English: Mahfuz-ul-Haq (1929). Study: Supriya Gandhi, The Emperor Who Never Was (2020). Executed 1659.
Parallels: Sufism and Upanishadic Vedanta.
Tier: M; P01 reports [V] for the term pairings.
Use: the first systematic term-by-term comparer, and a method model. Be fair: he rendered Brahman in Sufi words, and historians warn against making him a modern secular hero.

9. Akbar, the Ibadat Khana, sulh-i kull
Claim: the emperor (r. 1556 to 1605) built a House of Worship at Fatehpur Sikri (1575), first for Muslim scholars, from about 1578 for Hindus, Jains, Zoroastrians and Jesuits too, to debate before him. Abu'l-Fazl articulated "peace with all" (sulh-i kull). Sanskrit works went into Persian (the Mahabharata as Razmnama). An inscription on the Buland Darwaza (1601) gives a saying of Jesus that the world is a bridge to cross and not to build on [M].
Who: Abu'l-Fazl, Akbarnama and Ain-i Akbari; Badauni (hostile); Jesuit accounts. Modern: Truschke, Culture of Encounters (2016); Moin, The Millennial Sovereign (2012).
Parallels: Islam (Sunni and Shia), Hinduism, Jainism, Zoroastrianism, Christianity.
Tier: M.
Use: comparison by debate did not yield sameness but yielded a policy of peace. Historians doubt that "Din-i Ilahi" was a religion, so do not call it one.

10. Kabir
Claim: a weaver of Varanasi (fifteenth century, dates uncertain) who mocks Hindu and Muslim orthodoxy and points to a God beyond names, within. Widely attributed lines: the Hindu says Ram, the Turk says Rahim, they fight and die without knowing the secret; God is not in temple, mosque, Kaaba or Kailash but near you, as oil is in sesame and fire in flint; when the pot breaks the water in it merges with the water around it; the drop merges into the ocean, which all know, but few know the ocean merging into the drop.
Who: the Bijak, the Kabir Granthavali, the Guru Granth Sahib. Vaudeville, A Weaver Named Kabir (1993); Hess and Singh, The Bijak of Kabir (1983); Tagore and Underhill, Songs of Kabir (1915).
Parallels: Hindu, Sufi, Nath yogis, Vaishnava bhakti.
Tier: M for the lines as widely quoted; U for any verse number.
Use: the pot and the water, and the ocean in the drop, are the best folk images for "the separation ends" and "the whole lives in each part". Write "attributed to Kabir", since the tradition is oral and loose.

11. Guru Nanak and the Guru Granth Sahib
Claim: Guru Arjan compiled the scripture in 1604 (final form 1678, enthroned as Guru 1708). It includes Gurus' hymns and those of about fifteen other saints, among them Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev and the Sufi Baba Farid. It teaches one formless God (Ik Onkar), karma and rebirth, and release through the Name and living by God's order (hukam). A Guru Arjan hymn (Ramkali, about page 885 [U]) lists Ram and Khuda, pilgrimage and Hajj, Veda and Quran, and says the one who knows the Order knows the secret. "Na koi Hindu na Musalman" is a janamsakhi saying, not scripture [M].
Who: Guru Granth Sahib; Nanak (1469 to 1539). McLeod, Guru Nanak and the Sikh Religion (1968).
Tier: M; U for pages.
Use: a scripture that is an anthology by design, and that keeps karma, rebirth and return (light into light). Sikh scholars reject "Hindu-Muslim synthesis" as the origin: Sikhs hold the revelation came to Nanak.

12. Ramakrishna (1836 to 1886)
Claim: said to have practised Tantra, Vaishnava devotion, Advaita under Totapuri, Islam (a few days, c. 1866) and Christianity (c. 1874), and to have reached the same realization by each. Images: the roof reached by stone stairs, wooden stairs, bamboo or rope; one lake with many landing steps where Hindus, Muslims and Christians draw the same water and call it jal, pani and water. The slogan "yato mat tato path" is as remembered; part-2-notes could not confirm it.
Who: The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna (Mahendranath Gupta; English by Nikhilananda, 1942); Saradananda, Lila Prasanga.
Tier: M. The practice claims and dates come from disciples (U).
Use: the lake and the roof are the best "same thing, different words" images, from someone who tried the paths. Critics: the practices were short and inside a Hindu frame.

13. Vivekananda and the Parliament of 1893
Claim: put Ramakrishna's idea before the West. In Chicago he quoted a hymn about streams mingling in one sea and Gita 4.11, and said each faith must take in the spirit of the others and grow by its own law.
Who: 11 and 27 September 1893; later Raja Yoga and Karma Yoga (1896) on each soul being potentially divine (locator unknown).
Tier: M; P01 reports [V] for the wording, so I do not repeat it.
Use: the best-attested stream and sea image. He ranks Advaita highest, so he is an inclusivist (section 5).

14. Rammohan Roy (1772 to 1833)
Claim: belief in one God is the natural faith of all, and rituals, idols and miracles are accretions; strip each religion to one God plus ethics.
Who: Tuhfat al-Muwahhidin (Persian, c. 1804); Upanishad translations (1816 to 1819); The Precepts of Jesus (1820); the Brahmo Sabha (1828) and 1830 trust deed opening its house to all creeds.
Parallels: Vedanta, Islamic rationalism, Unitarian Christianity.
Tier: M.
Use: the Enlightenment method, the opposite of Alex's, which keeps the strange parts (karma, lifetimes).

15. Rabindranath Tagore (1861 to 1941)
Claim: the infinite expresses itself in the finite and the finite returns by love; he sets the Upanishads, Kabir, the Bauls (Bengal's mendicant singers) and Buddhist compassion in one stream.
Who: Sadhana (1913); The Religion of Man (Hibbert Lectures 1930, book 1931); Songs of Kabir (1915).
Tier: M; U for chapters.
Use: the poetic voice; optional.

16. Sri Aurobindo (1872 to 1950)
Claim: the One involved itself into matter and forgot; it evolves back through life, mind and supermind; the world is a real manifestation, not an illusion; the soul (psychic being) continues through lives and grows; all life is yoga.
Who: The Life Divine (serial 1914 to 1919, book 1939 to 1940); The Synthesis of Yoga; Essays on the Gita; The Secret of the Veda. Chapter locators unknown.
Parallels: Veda, Gita, Vedanta (against Shankara's illusion reading), Tantra, modern evolution.
Tier: M.
Use: the nearest structural match to the three clauses in this slice: involution, ignorance, evolutionary return, rebirth. It also supports a staged scale like the arrow.

17. Gandhi's prayers and sayings on religion
Claim: the ashram's daily prayer used Hindu hymns, Gita verses, Quran recitation, a Buddhist chant, a Parsi prayer and Christian hymns ("Lead, Kindly Light" [M]). Religions are different roads to one point; all are true and imperfect; equal respect ("sarva dharma sambhava"). He changed "God is Truth" to "Truth is God" (1931).
Who: Hind Swaraj (1909), chapter on Hindus and Muslims [U]; Young India and Harijan; Ashram Bhajanavali; the Dandi March bhajan with "Ishwar Allah tero naam" [M, U on who added the line].
Tier: M.
Use: comparison as daily practice. His Gita reading as anasakti (non-attachment in action) bridges to Part one's letting go, and the bhajan "Vaishnava jana" (one who feels another's pain as his own) bridges to the fingers [M].

18. Baha'i progressive revelation
Claim: the Founders are Manifestations of one God, like successive risings of one sun; their spiritual teaching is one and their social laws change with the age; humanity is like a child being educated. The soul begins at conception and goes on after death without returning to this world.
Who: Baha'u'llah, Kitab-i-Iqan (1861); Abdu'l-Baha, Some Answered Questions; Shoghi Effendi, Gleanings (1935).
Parallels: Abraham, Moses, Krishna, Buddha, Zoroaster, Jesus, Muhammad, the Bab. The Iqan leans on Quran 2:285.
Tier: M; U for Gleanings numbers.
Use: the clearest "one source, many forms" with a time axis. It conflicts with Alex: it rejects reincarnation and ends with its own founder on top. Many Muslims reject the scheme, so an included tradition can refuse it.

19. Jain anekantavada
Claim: reality has many sides; each viewpoint (naya) is partial and true from where it stands; statements are qualified (syat, "from a standpoint"). A verse attributed to Haribhadra says he has no bias for Mahavira nor hatred for Kapila, and one should accept whoever speaks reasonably [U on locator].
Who: Siddhasena Divakara, Sanmati-tarka (c. fifth century); Haribhadra (eighth), Saddarshanasamuccaya, an even-handed survey of six systems; Mallisena, Syadvadamanjari (1292); Umasvati, Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (souls help one another).
Tier: M for doctrine; U for the verse.
Use: gives the elephant an Indian philosophical frame. Limits: Shankara's Brahmasutra commentary (about 2.2.33 to 36) attacks it, and Jains hold that only their path liberates and that souls are many.

20. Chinese "three teachings are one" and the Confucian one body
Claim: Confucianism, Daoism and Buddhism are said to share a source or to do different jobs. The Xici commentary of the Yijing says that under heaven the end is one and the thoughts many, the return one by different roads. A Song emperor, Xiaozong, is reported to have said: Buddhism for the mind, Daoism for the body, Confucianism for the world [M, U on source]. Lin Zhao'en (1517 to 1598) founded a Three-in-One teaching in Fujian. Neo-Confucians borrowed from Buddhism: principle is one, manifestations many (Cheng Yi, Zhu Xi), and one moon in all waters (from Yongjia Xuanjue). Zhang Zai's Western Inscription says Heaven is my father, Earth my mother, all people my siblings. Wang Yangming (Inquiry on the Great Learning, 1527) says the great person forms one body with Heaven, Earth and all things, selfish desire makes the mind small, and the pang at a child about to fall into a well shows it (from Mencius 2A6).
Who: Yijing Xici; Zhang Zai (1020 to 1077); Wang Yangming (1472 to 1529). Berling, The Syncretic Religion of Lin Chao-en (1980); Brook, "Rethinking Syncretism" (Journal of Chinese Religions, 1993).
Tier: M; U for the Xiaozong source.
Use: Wang Yangming is the best non-Western parallel to two fingers of one hand and to the small and big square, and he names the cause of separation. Caution: Brook says the formula was often rhetoric or joint worship, not a claim of identical doctrine.

21. The East Asian Buddhist One Mind: Awakening of Faith, Zongmi, Wonhyo
Claim: the Awakening of Faith in the Mahayana says One Mind has two gates, true thusness and arising and ceasing; unawareness stirs a thought, a world of separate things appears, and "initial awakening" returns to "original awakening". Zongmi (780 to 841) ranked Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist teachings in his Inquiry into the Origin of Humanity (c. 820s [U]) and argued that Chan and the doctrinal schools agree. Wonhyo (617 to 686) wrote on harmonizing doctrinal disputes from One Mind.
Who: Awakening of Faith (attributed to Asvaghosa; probably Chinese, sixth century); Gregory, Tsung-mi and the Sinification of Buddhism (1991).
Tier: M.
Use: the East Asian text closest to the three clauses, and an answer to "what about Buddhism". Limits: Critical Buddhism (Hakamaya and Matsumoto; Hubbard and Swanson, Pruning the Bodhi Tree, 1997) argues that "one ground" thinking contradicts dependent origination. Say "some Buddhist schools say it like this, and others dispute it".

22. Japan: honji suijaku and Kukai's ten stages
Claim: from the eighth century shrines and temples merged. Honji suijaku says Buddhas and bodhisattvas are the original substance and the kami their local traces (Amaterasu with Dainichi; Hachiman as a bodhisattva). Ryobu Shinto and Sanno Ichijitsu Shinto built it out. In 1868 the state ordered the two separated (shinbutsu bunri), and attacks on Buddhism followed. Kukai (774 to 835) set out ten stages of mind (Jujushinron, 830), from animal appetite through Confucian and Daoist stages and the Buddhist vehicles in order to the Shingon stage. A Laozi phrase, "soften your light, join the dust" (wako doujin), was used to explain how Buddhas appear as kami [M].
Who: Teeuwen and Rambelli, Buddhas and Kami in Japan (2003); Grapard, History of Religions (1984); Kuroda on the kenmitsu system; Hakeda, Kukai: Major Works (1972).
Tier: M; U for the mountain-and-moon proverb (many paths up, one moon at the summit, author unknown to me) and for the tree saying credited to Yoshida Kanetomo (Shinto root, Confucianism branches, Buddhism fruit).
Use: Kukai's ladder is a staged scale of mind that includes other traditions as earlier rungs, which is the design of the arrow. The kami and the Buddhas show "one source, many manifestations" as shared worship for a thousand years, with a hierarchy inside.

23. Short extras
Ashoka's Rock Edict 12 (c. 256 BCE): do not honour only your own sect and condemn others; concord is good, so that all may listen to one another's teaching [M]. Thapar; Dhammika.
Sri Yukteswar's The Holy Science (1894) was written to show the harmony of Christian and Hindu scriptures and sets out seven realms (lokas). His pupil Yogananda read Gospels and Gita as one teaching (The Second Coming of Christ, 2004) [M; U on details].

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

Name-matching. Put a term from A beside a term from B and call them equal. Dara pairs elements, attributes, three Hindu gods with three angels, and the Upanishads with a hidden book of the Quran. Rumi's grapes is the same idea as a joke. Risk: the comparer picks the match, and Dara's Vedanta sounds Sufi.

Dialogue and the debate hall. Dara sits with Baba Lal; Akbar's Ibadat Khana seats scholars of many creeds in one room. The yield is education and a policy, rarely a finding of sameness.

Translation as comparison. Dara's Persian Upanishads and Akbar's Persian Mahabharata make texts readable to the other side, and the choice of terms carries the thesis. 

Anthology by design. The Guru Granth Sahib includes Kabir, Ravidas and Farid on purpose, each named.

Ranking by depth. Zongmi, Kukai, Vivekananda and the Baha'i scheme order the traditions by how far each sees. The ranker lands on top.

Practice and ritual. Ramakrishna lived several religions, Gandhi's prayers recited them, Japanese shrine-temples worshipped both. Sameness is lived before it is argued.

Parable and image. The elephant, the lake with many steps, the roof, one moon in many waters, the grapes, the pot in the water. The images do the work of a proof and stay vague on purpose.

How differences are explained: the form is shaped by the listener (Gita, Rumi's Moses and shepherd); partial standpoint (Jain, Udana); local custom around one core (Ramakrishna, Gandhi); one source at different times (Baha'i, Zongmi); later corruption (Rammohan Roy); different jobs (the Chinese formula).

## 5. What scholars say

Inclusivism. Paul Hacker (died 1979; essays collected 1983, in German) argued that Hindu tolerance is mostly inclusivist: it absorbs the other and ranks it lower. Halbfass (India and Europe, 1988) and Richard King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) say the Ramakrishna and Vivekananda universalism is a modern Neo-Vedanta shaped in dialogue with the West. The Gita verses fit Hacker's pattern.

Dara and Akbar. Recent historians (Gandhi 2020, Truschke 2016, Moin 2012) place both inside Persianate ideas of sovereignty and mysticism, not as modern secular syncretists, and doubt that Din-i Ilahi was a religion.

Kabir and Nanak. Vaudeville, Lorenzen and McLeod question "synthesis of Hinduism and Islam". Kabir attacks both, and the Sant tradition has its own roots.

The elephant. Lesslie Newbigin (1989) noted that the pluralist reading needs a teller who sees the whole elephant. Stephen Prothero (God Is Not One, 2010) argues the religions ask different questions. John Hick used the elephant for his pluralist hypothesis.

Three teachings. Timothy Brook (1993) says the formula was often rhetoric or temple-sharing. 

One Mind. Critical Buddhism rejects it as non-Buddhist; others defend it as Mahayana's own growth.

Jain anekanta. Matilal (1981) and Long (2009) call it perspectivism with a truth to find.

Honji suijaku. Teeuwen and Rambelli show it was a hierarchy, Buddhas first. Kuroda argued that "Shinto" as a separate religion is largely a modern construction.
