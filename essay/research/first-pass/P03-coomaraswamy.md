## 1. The picture in brief

Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947) is the most learned of the three men usually named as founders of the Traditionalist or perennialist school, with Guenon and Schuon. He came to the subject from the other end: geologist, then historian of Indian and Sinhalese art, then from the 1930s a writer on metaphysics who read Sanskrit, Pali, Greek and Latin sources side by side. His claim is that the doctrine of the Veda, the Upanishads, the Buddha's discourses, Plato, Plotinus, Eckhart and the Sufis is one doctrine in different languages, and that the agreement comes from a common source in a metaphysical truth that does not change, and not from borrowing.

His central pairing is the Self and the self. The Self (Atman, Spirit, Plato's nous, Eckhart's spark) is the one real knower, the same in all beings. The self (the mortal, empirical "I") is what the traditions tell you to deny, forget or sacrifice. From that follow his readings of transmigration (the Lord is the only transmigrant), of Buddhism (anatta denies the self, not the Self), and of the way (self-naughting, recollection, the Sun door).

For Alex the value is high and so is the risk. High, because Coomaraswamy states the logic of his axiom better than almost anyone, with checkable texts. Risk, because he was not a collector of witnesses. He held that a tradition is understood from inside and he disliked syncretism. He read Buddhism through Vedanta, a reading Theravada scholars reject. His school treated Theosophy as a counterfeit and, as far as I know, rejected individual reincarnation. Alex's two comparing movements cannot be quoted as if they agree.

7. The one and only transmigrant

Claim: He opens the 1944 paper with a dictum of Shankara (Brahma Sutra Bhashya 1.1.5): there is no transmigrant but the Lord. What goes through all lives is the one Spirit in all beings, and no separate individual soul makes a journey of its own. In the same paper he draws on Plato and Plotinus, noting that in the spiritual Self of all beings all selves and their doings are one simple act of being. Who: Coomaraswamy, "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (JAOS supplement, 1944; Selected Papers vol 2). The words [V]: "Verily, there is no other transmigrant but the Lord" (advaita-vision.org result, citing the paper's opening and BrSBh 1.1.5). Whether the paper uses the thread-spirit (sutratman) image, tied to Bhagavad Gita 7.7 (all this strung on the Lord like gems on a thread), I recall from his other papers and cannot confirm: [M]/[U]. Traditions: Advaita Vedanta, Plato, Plotinus. Tier: [V] dictum, title, date, Plato and Plotinus link; [S] gist. Use: this answers the question Alex asks himself ("if there is only one mind, who is it that carries over?"). Coomaraswamy's answer: only the one does. Caution: it fits the axiom better than it fits a soul that continues and grows, because the individual is the thing that does not carry over. See finding 17.

8. What transmigrates in Buddhism: the 1916 view

Claim: In 1916 he wrote that Buddhism nowhere teaches the transmigration of souls, only of "character", and that no thing passes from one life to another. Who: Buddha and the Gospel of Buddhism (1916), section "Samsara and Kamma" (Heidelberg digital library, coomaraswamy1916, pages 0158 to 0163). The words [V, as printed in the summary]: "the transmigration of character, of personality without a person", and that samsara "is not for Gautama the wandering of any thing". A summary also printed, with the book location not certain (it may be the 1943 book): "empirically speaking nothing, nothing that we can call anything, transmigrates" (dashes in the printed text replaced by commas). Same passage [V, summary]: popular thought, Brahmanical or Buddhist, conceives the doctrine "animistically", as an individual soul passing on. By 1943 he reads Buddhism as consistent with Vedanta (finding 10), so his own view moved. Tier [V] for the printed wording, [U] for how he later described the change. Use: a real expert changing his mind, which suits Alex's tone, and the cleanest statement of the Buddhist version Alex gives ("no fixed soul, yet something continues"). I recall he also discusses the Milindapanha lamp [M].

9. Recollection, Indian and Platonic

Claim: In the first 1944 paper he sets Plato's doctrine that learning is recollection (anamnesis) beside the Indian use of "remembering" (smrti; Pali sati) for knowing what one is. Forgetting is the condition (avidya), recollection is the return. Who: Coomaraswamy (title and date [V], zenon.dainst.org). Plato: Meno, Phaedo, Phaedrus; the river of forgetfulness in the Myth of Er, Republic 10 [M]. The Pali sati, usually "mindfulness", means plainly "memory" [M]. The paper's content beyond the title is [M]. Use: it ties together Alex's veil paragraph and his sentence about the river of forgetfulness. Check how he handles the Meno link.

10. Buddhism consistent with Vedanta: anatta and Atman

Claim: He reads the Buddha's "not self" as aimed at the empirical self, the five aggregates, and not at the Self. The Buddha works by elimination, as the Upanishads do with "not this, not this". He cites the ideal of a self "become Brahma" and holds that the Arhat is, in the end, the Spirit within all beings. Who: Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), via the Advaita-l archive and other results. The words [V, as printed in a mailing list quoting him, source work not confirmed]: "it is only by a process of elimination that the thought is reached that the Subject is No-thing". The same quote says the Upanishads do not begin by denying a knowing, lasting subject as the Buddha does. Another result prints the goal of the Arhat as "brahma-bhutena-atmana", "with the self that is Brahma-become", and points to the first line of the Kena Upanishad [V, summary]. Traditions: Upanishads, Pali Canon. Tier [S] gist, [V] printed phrases. Use: the best-known Traditionalist bridge between the two, so "Buddhism says it in other words" gets a named source. He must say it is contested (finding 19). The Pali brahma-bhuta is read by Theravada commentary as "become holy", not "become Brahma" [M].

11. The two selves: the core pairing

Claim: A scripture that says "self" speaks in one of two senses: the Self (Atman, Spirit, the only knowing subject, one in all) or the self (the contingent, affected "I"). Much of his reading consists in showing that scriptures of different traditions use this pair and are misread when it is lost. The figures he lines up, as I know them: the two birds on one tree, one eating and one watching (Rigveda 1.164.20, Mundaka 3.1.1, Shvetashvatara 4.6); the chariot of Katha 1.3.3 to 9 and Plato's chariot in the Phaedrus (246 to 254); the Gospel sayings that he who hates his soul (psyche) in this world keeps it unto life eternal (John 12:25), that one must deny oneself (Mark 8:34), and "of mine own self I can do nothing" (John 5:30); and Delphi's "Know thyself" read as "know thy Self". [V] for the Mundaka birds (esamskriti result); [M] for the other loci. Who: "The Vedanta and Western Tradition" and "On the Indian and Traditional Psychology, or rather Pneumatology" (titles [V]); Roger Lipsey, "The Two Selves: Coomaraswamy as Man and Metaphysician", Studies in Comparative Religion 6(4), Autumn 1972 [V, Matheson Trust and World Wisdom results]. Two phrases as printed in summaries [V]: Atman is used reflexively for "oneself" in any sense or for the spiritual Self, the only knowing subject, to be told from "the affected and contingent I"; and a Brihadaranyaka line he cites, "How couldst thou know the knower of knowing?" Traditions: Vedic, Upanishadic, Platonic, Christian. Use: it names what Alex's machine picture is after. The small self is the program inside its walls, the Self is the machine.

13. Maya, lila and the "why" of the whole

Claim, as I know his argument [M]: maya is not simply illusion. It is the divine power of measuring out the one into forms, from the root ma, to measure or build (Latin materia and mater are relatives). The made world is not unreal, and the mistake is to take the made thing as self-existent. Lila answers Alex's question why a complete God would create: God works "without a why", like a child at play. He sets Brahma Sutra 2.1.32 and 2.1.33 (lilakaivalyam) beside Eckhart's "without why" (sunder warumbe), Heraclitus fragment 52 (Aion a child at play) and Plato's Laws 803 (man a plaything of God). I am confident of his paper "Lila" in the Journal of the American Oriental Society (1941) [M]. "Play and Seriousness" and "Measures of Fire" (titles [V]) belong with it. A secondary article (MDPI result) credits him with comparing Eckhart's work without a why to the Upanishadic teaching of action with no ulterior aim [V, summary; I do not trust the locator printed there]. Tier [M] for the equations, [V] for the titles and secondary credit. Use: a checkable addition to the "why would the whole do this?" paragraph, which now rests on Brahma Sutra 2.1.33, the Kabbalists, Plotinus and the hadith. Eckhart, Heraclitus and Plato can be quoted fuller in old translations.

19. How the Traditionalists and the scholars rate him

Traditionalist side [V]: Wikipedia and Matheson Trust results give him with Guenon and Schuon as one of three founders. The World Wisdom result calls Roger Lipsey a leading authority on him. Mircea Eliade wrote "Some Notes on Theosophia Perennis: Ananda K. ## 4. How he compares: the method, concretely

1. The unit of comparison is a sentence or a symbol, not a religion. He picks one formula (the two birds, the door of the sun, "no transmigrant but the Lord") and follows it through texts in the original languages. Alex already works this way, one consequence at a time.

2. He sets the texts in parallel and lets them speak: an Indian line, a Greek line, a Latin or German line, then a short comment. His claim is that any reader can compare the words and see the sameness. Titles like Figures of Speech or Figures of Thought (1946) [V] state the principle: a symbol is a precise language, not decoration, so a sun door in a temple, a Gospel saying and an Upanishadic verse can be one idea in three media [M].

3. He explains the sameness by a common source, not by borrowing or common psychology. The agreement is of doctrine, which is a metaphysical truth that does not change. Alex differs: he proposes a common logic (the axiom). Coomaraswamy would call the logic the doctrine itself, which is close, but he would deny that a doctrine can be judged by someone standing outside every tradition [M].

4. He explains differences by audience and level. A teaching for householders differs from one for monks. The Buddha's "no self" addressed people who took the empirical self for the Self. His "Paths" essay is the short version: paths differ at the foot and meet at the top, and you cannot see that from the foot [V gist].

5. He distrusts "evolutionism", "psychologism" and "historicism", which flatten old doctrine into early groping [M]. Alex's arrow looks like progress, so a Traditionalist reader may bristle, though they accept ascent on an individual path.

6. He refuses syncretism: blending traditions loses the form that carries the truth, and a person should walk one orthodox path [M]. A reader from his school will find Alex's witness approach thin, and Alex can say so himself.

## 5. What scholars say, short

Respected for the range and precision of his reading, his pioneering work on Indian art, and for bringing Indian metaphysics to Western humanists [M]. Standing criticisms: different periods and schools flattened into one voice; a strong Advaita tilt, most visible on anatta [V, Rahula]; little interest in historical development [M]; the anti-modern politics of his school [M]. The Traditionalists regard him as foundational and the model of rigour [V]. He changed his own mind at least once (findings 8 and 10).
