## 1. The picture in brief

The draft says the traditions "never met" (line 51) and that a coherent system could be what "independent traditions reached separately" (line 23). Both are half true.

For the Axial period (about 800 to 200 BCE) the best case for separate emergence is China against everyone else. India, Iran, Israel and Greece were joined by the Persian empire from about 520 BCE, so contact was possible, though influence on the essay's own ideas (one whole, veil, rebirth, karma, return) is demonstrated in few places. Rebirth may have arisen more than once.

After that the witnesses are linked. Plotinus feeds Christian, Islamic and Jewish mystics. Buddhism feeds Daoism and Neo-Confucianism. Sufis and Vedantins have talked since the eleventh century. The Buddha became a Catholic saint, Josaphat. Nearly everything an English reader knows of the Eastern witnesses came through nineteenth century translators and comparers (Anquetil-Duperron, Schopenhauer, Müller, the Theosophists, Evans-Wentz), whose equations are in the text.

The movement's authors mostly do not claim independence. Guénon, Schuon, Smith and Blavatsky explain agreement by common origin. Independence is the claim of Jaspers, Jung, Obeyesekere and the psychologists. The convergence tests (Hood's scale, the Mystical Experience Questionnaire) use a list from one Western philosopher and mostly Western samples. Cognitive science predicts shared ideas from shared minds, which cuts both ways.

Recommendation: stop saying "never met", say some met constantly and some did not, count hubs and not names, and flag which is which. Section 6 has the table and the rewrite.

## 2. Findings

1. Jaspers and the independence premise. Claim: the great spiritual and philosophical breakthroughs came in China, India, Iran, Israel and Greece between about 800 and 200 BCE, in separate places with no demonstrable link. Who: Karl Jaspers, Vom Ursprung und Ziel der Geschichte (1949); English The Origin and Goal of History (1953), the chapter on the Axial Period; locator unknown. He presents the features as arising independently and almost together, calls the thesis empirical and open to challenge, and leaves early contact open [M gist, U on the last point]. Cites: Confucius, Laozi, Upanishads, Buddha, Zarathustra, the prophets, Homer, the philosophers. Tier [M]. Use: the one respectable academic source behind "never met". It covers 800 to 200 BCE only, not Eckhart, Rumi or Kabbalah.

2. Later versions keep the premise and change the explanation. Claim: Armstrong, The Great Transformation (2006), reads four traditions as ethical responses to violence and urbanisation [M, U on framing]. Schwartz ("The Age of Transcendence", Daedalus, 1975) and Eisenstadt (The Origins and Diversity of Axial Age Civilizations, 1986) define the step as a break between a transcendent and a mundane order, which made new elites of sages and clerics [M]. Bellah, Religion in Human Evolution (2011), treats Israel, Greece, China and India, defines the step as "theoretic culture" (thinking about thinking, after Merlin Donald), and says it grows from older layers [M]. Tier [M]. Use: three reasons why similar ideas appeared apart (ethics under violence, a structural break, a cognitive step). None says "they saw the same truth".

3. Critics, and where the dating is contested. Claim: six hundred years is wide enough to catch most first-millennium thinkers; the choice of regions is retrospective; the key dates are unsettled. Who: Assmann, Achsenzeit (2018); Arnason, Eisenstadt and Wittrock (eds.), Axial Civilizations and World History (2005), which treats it as a frame and not one event [M]; Mullins and colleagues, American Sociological Review (2018), a Seshat test I recall as a weak fit for a sharp break [U]. Dating: the Buddha's death about 480 or about 400 BCE (the Göttingen symposium ed. Bechert, 1991 to 1997, leaned to about 400); Zoroaster from the sixth century BCE to the second millennium on linguistic grounds; early Upanishads about the eighth to sixth century (Olivelle); the Daodejing in its present form fourth to third century, with Guodian slips about 300; the Rigveda about 1500 to 1000, so "one reality, many names" (1.164.46) is pre-axial [M]. Tier [M]. Use: the Axial thesis is a rough frame. The essay's sources span three thousand years, so independence has to be argued pair by pair.

4. Persia as the contact zone. Claim: from about 520 BCE the Achaemenid empire touched both the Aegean and the Indus. The Ionian cities (Thales, Xenophanes, Heraclitus) were Persian subjects. India paid the largest tribute in Herodotus (3.94). Skylax sailed down the Indus for Darius (4.44). Ctesias wrote on India from the Persian court about 400. Greek knowledge of India predates Alexander [M]. Who: Herodotus; M. L. West, Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient (1971), arguing Near Eastern and Iranian input into early Greek thought [M]; Burkert, The Orientalizing Revolution (1992) [M]. Tier [M]. Use: "never met" is false for Greece and India. "Met and borrowed the key ideas" is unproven.

5. Pythagoras, Plato and Indian rebirth. Claim: the first Greek evidence for rebirth is the story of Pythagoras and a beaten dog (Xenophanes, Diels-Kranz 21 B7) [M]. Burkert, Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism (1972), treats Pythagoras as a shaman-like teacher and the mathematical school as later; his view on India I do not recall [U]. Bremmer, The Rise and Fall of the Afterlife (2002), I remember as a Greek-development reader [U]. McEvilley, The Shape of Ancient Thought (2002), argues repeated India and Greece contact from the Bronze Age, including on Pythagoras via Persia [M]. Kingsley, Ancient Philosophy, Mystery, and Magic (1995), ties Empedocles and the Pythagorean tradition to Iranian and Central Asian shamanism, not directly to India [M]. McEvilley was an art critic, and specialists mostly call the Indian-origin case possible and unproven [M]. Tier [M]. Use: the safe sentence is "at about the same time, with no demonstrated link".

6. Rebirth as a repeated invention. Claim: Obeyesekere, Imagining Karma (2002), compares rebirth beliefs among Amerindian and other non-literate peoples with Greek and Indian cases, treats "rebirth eschatologies" as arising repeatedly and the ethicized form (karma) as a later step [M gist, U on his verdict for Greece]. Bronkhorst, Greater Magadha (2007), argues karma and rebirth belong to the eastern Gangetic world of Jains, Ajivikas and Buddhists, outside Vedic ritual, and entered Brahmanical texts such as the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad by borrowing [M]. Tier [M]. Use: the best support for "lives" as independent convergence, and from an anthropologist. It also warns that Upanishads and Buddhism are not independent of each other.

7. Greece and India after Alexander. Claim: from about 330 BCE contact is documented. Pyrrho went to India with Alexander (Diogenes Laertius 9.61) [M]. Beckwith, Greek Buddha (2015), argues Pyrrhonism adapted early Buddhist teaching; most classicists find it possible and unproven [M]. Ashoka's edicts (about 260 BCE) name five Hellenistic kings and include Greek texts at Kandahar [M]. The Milindapañha records a dialogue of the monk Nagasena with the Indo-Greek king Menander (reigned about 165 to 130 BCE), including the chariot argument for no-self; a real meeting behind it is doubtful [M]. Gandharan Buddhist art shows Greek style, and a Greek envoy, Heliodorus, raised a pillar to Vasudeva at Besnagar about 110 BCE [M]. Tier [M]. Use: for these pairs "met" is a fact. The open point is which way the doctrines moved.

8. Plotinus and the Gordian expedition. Claim: Plotinus (about 204 to 270) joined Gordian III's Persian campaign in 242 to 243 to learn Persian and Indian philosophy and fled after Gordian's death (Porphyry, Life of Plotinus 3) [M]. His writings show no use of Indian texts; scholars explain him from Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, Pythagoreans, Numenius and his teacher Ammonius Saccas (A. H. Armstrong's Loeb introductions) [M]. Indian knowledge was around him: Clement of Alexandria names Brahmans, Sarmanai and Boutta (Stromata 1.15) [M]; Bardaisan of Edessa described Indian sages in a report Porphyry preserves (De abstinentia 4.17 to 18) [M]. Harris (ed.), Neoplatonism and Indian Thought (1982), compares them [U]. The Theosophical story that Ammonius founded an "Eclectic Theosophical School" is Theosophy's own and has no ancient support [M]. Tier [M]. Use: the famous bridge is real as intent and unproven as influence. Write "he went looking, and what he wrote is Greek".

9. Neoplatonism into the Abrahamic mystical layers. Claim: three documented channels. Christian: Plotinus in Marius Victorinus's Latin shaped Augustine (Confessions 7); Pseudo-Dionysius (Syria, about 500) applied Proclus to Christian terms; Eriugena translated him in the 860s; Eckhart (about 1260 to 1328) cites Dionysius, Augustine, the Liber de causis and Maimonides [M]. Islamic: the Arabic Plotinus (the Theology of Aristotle, ninth century, al-Kindi's circle) and the Liber de causis (from Proclus) fed al-Farabi, Avicenna, Ismaili thinkers and Suhrawardi; Ibn Arabi (1165 to 1240) uses Neoplatonic and Hermetic material beside the Qur'an [M]. Jewish: Isaac Israeli and Ibn Gabirol came before the Bahir (12th century) and Zohar (about 1280s); Scholem stressed Gnostic roots and possible Cathar contact for gilgul, Idel stresses older Jewish traditions [M, U on Cathars]; Sufi influence on Bahya's Duties of the Heart and on Abraham Maimonides's circle is documented (Fenton) [M]. Who: McGinn (1991, 2001); Adamson, The Arabic Plotinus (2002); Chittick; Scholem (1941); Idel (1988); locators unknown. The phrase "unity of being" (wahdat al-wujud) is, I recall, the label of his followers and critics and not his own [M, U on first use]. Tier [M]. Use: Eckhart, Ibn Arabi and the Kabbalists share one Greek tributary and also have separate scriptural roots. Do not count them as three independent witnesses to Upanishadic ideas.

10. Gnostic, Manichaean and Cathar routes. Claim: Mani (216 to 274) grew up in a Jewish-Christian baptist community (Cologne Mani Codex), travelled to India about 240 to 241 by later sources, and built a church that named Buddha, Zoroaster and Jesus as earlier apostles [M]. In Central Asia and China it took Buddhist and Daoist vocabulary [M].  A Manichaean and Arabic route carried the Buddha's life west: Bodhisattva became Budhasaf, Yudasaf, Georgian Iodasaph, Greek Ioasaph, Latin Josaphat, a saint in the Roman calendar (Barlaam and Josaphat) [M]. Origen's pre-existence of souls was condemned in the sixth century [M].  Who: Lieu, Manichaeism in the Later Roman Empire and Medieval China (1985) [M]. Tier [M]. Use: Mani did on purpose what Alex does, so he is a comparer. "Never met" fails again.

11. Vedanta and Sufism. Claim: contact is documented, with a timeline. Al-Biruni (about 1030) translated Patanjali and set Indian and Sufi sayings side by side [M]. Ernst: the Amritakunda, an Indian yoga text, was rendered into Arabic and Persian early in the Sultanate, and yogic breath practices entered Chishti and Shattari Sufism[M gist, U on dates]. Akbar's translation bureau produced the Razmnama (Mahabharata) in the 1580s [M]. Dara Shikoh wrote Majma al-Bahrayn (about 1655), arguing the two vocabularies differ in words, and the Sirr-i Akbar (1657), about fifty Upanishads in Persian, which Anquetil-Duperron put into Latin [M]. Zaehner, Hindu and Muslim Mysticism (1960), argued Bistami's ecstatic sayings show Vedantic influence through Abu Ali al-Sindi; I recall it being widely doubted [M claim, U reception]. The elephant and the blind men is Indian (Udana, Jain) first, then Sana'i (Hadiqat al-haqiqa, early 12th century), then Rumi (Masnavi III) [M], along the Indo-Persian frontier [U]. Tier [M] unless marked. Use: the elephant example is a likely borrowing. Use it as transmission and not as independent agreement.

12. Buddhism into Daoism and Neo-Confucianism. Claim: Buddhism reached China in the first century CE and was first expressed with Daoist terms (geyi, "matching concepts") (Zürcher, The Buddhist Conquest of China, 1959) [M]. A Daoist counterclaim, the huahu theory (Laozi went west and became the Buddha), circulated by about 300 [M]. The Lingbao scriptures (fifth century) took karma, rebirth, hells and the bodhisattva ideal from Buddhism (Zürcher 1980; Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures, 1997) [M]. Song Neo-Confucians borrowed Buddhist and Daoist practice (quiet sitting) while criticising Buddhism [M]. Tier [M]. Use: split early Daoism (Daodejing, Zhuangzi, before any Buddhism) from later Daoism, Chan and Neo-Confucianism. Only the first group is independent.

13. The nineteenth century channel, part one: Anquetil to Schrödinger. Claim: Anquetil-Duperron's Latin Oupnek'hat (1801 to 1802, from Dara's Persian) was Schopenhauer's door to the Upanishads; he read it about 1814 and later praised it as the consolation of his life [M]. Schopenhauer put "the veil of Maya" and "tat tvam asi" into Western philosophy and read Kant's appearance and thing-in-itself into Hindu and Buddhist sources [M]. Thomas Taylor's translations of Plotinus and Proclus fed Emerson and Blavatsky [M]. Emerson (The Over-Soul, 1841; Brahma, 1857) read Wilkins's Gita and Rammohan Roy's Upanishads (Christy, The Orient in American Transcendentalism, 1932) [M]. Schrödinger came to Vedanta through Schopenhauer (Moore, Schrödinger: Life and Thought, 1989) [M]. Tier [M]. Use: the draft cites Schrödinger on the Upanishads as a modern echo. It is a documented channel, so write "he read the Upanishads and found his view there".

14. The nineteenth century channel, part two: translators and revivals. Claim: Müller's Sacred Books of the East (1879 to 1910) framed texts with his own equations, and Deussen (1899) read Brahman as the Absolute in Kantian and Schopenhauerian terms [M]. Olcott and Blavatsky took the Five Precepts at Galle in 1880, and Olcott's Buddhist Catechism (1881) shaped the Sinhalese reform that Gombrich and Obeyesekere call "Protestant Buddhism" [M]. Vivekananda and Dharmapala spoke in Chicago in 1893 [M]. Hacker (1983) called Hindu tolerance "inclusivism", placing others beneath Advaita; Halbfass (India and Europe, 1988) and King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) show neo-Vedanta forming in dialogue with the West; McMahan (2008) and Sharf (Numen, 1995) show modern Buddhism foregrounding "experience" under Protestant, Romantic and scientific pressure [M]. D. T. Suzuki compared Zen and Eckhart (1957) after working in the Open Court and Theosophical circles [M]. Müller's Ramakrishna: His Life and Sayings (1898) is a likely route for Ramakrishna's lake parable into English [U]. Tier [M] unless marked. Use: when a modern Hindu or Buddhist says the religions agree, flag that this voice grew in conversation with the West.

15. Evans-Wentz and the bardo. Claim: The Tibetan Book of the Dead (1927) was compiled by W. Y. Evans-Wentz, a Theosophist since about 1901, from Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's translation of the Bardo Thodol; the English title imitates the Egyptian Book of the Dead, and the notes carry Theosophical glosses (Lopez, The Tibetan Book of the Dead: A Biography, 2011) [M]. Jung's 1935 commentary read it as a map of mind [M]. Tier [M]. Use: bardo is a good witness. Cite a modern translation (Coleman and Jinpa, 2006) and say which.

16. Guénon and Schuon on independence. Claim: Guénon (Introduction générale à l'étude des doctrines hindoues, 1921; Le Roi du Monde, 1927) holds that authentic traditions descend from one primordial tradition, rejects syncretism and outside comparison as profane, and requires entry through one tradition's initiatic chain; he attacked Theosophy (Le Théosophisme, 1921) [M]. Schuon (De l'unité transcendante des religions, 1948) holds each religion to be a separate divine form of the one formless Absolute, agreeing at the esoteric centre and differing at the exoteric surface [M]. Coomaraswamy, Smith and Nasr follow it [M]. Tier [M]. Use: for them "independent" means independent historically and one in source metaphysically, so contact is beside the point. They would object to Alex's method, since he stands in no tradition and compares from outside.

17. Smith, Hick and the honest minimal claim. Claim: Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976; later retitled The Common Vision of the World's Religions), reads the traditions as maps of one hierarchy of reality [M, U on title order]; his 1987 article "Is There a Perennial Philosophy?" is more modest [M]. Hick, An Interpretation of Religion (1989), calls the "post-axial" faiths different human responses to the Real in itself, judged by one function (turning from self-centredness to Reality-centredness) and not by shared doctrine [M]. Critics (P. J. Griffiths, 2001) say a Real that cannot be described empties the claim [M]. Tier [M]. Use: Hick gives the safest wording: a shared function and pattern, with doctrines that differ.

18. The Theosophical descent claim. Claim: Blavatsky (Isis Unveiled, 1877; The Secret Doctrine, 1888; The Key to Theosophy, 1889) holds that the religions are branches of one archaic Wisdom-Religion kept by adepts, and that its teachings were checked by generations of seers [M gist, U wording]. The Stanzas of Dzyan have no known source outside her writings, and historians (Washington 1993, Johnson 1994, Hanegraaff 1996) find the descent claim unfalsifiable [M].  Tier [M]. Use: Theosophy's explanation is the opposite of independence. The essay already says it borrows the comparison and not the Masters; add that it borrows the comparison and not the explanation.

19. Katz and independence within a tradition. Claim: Katz (ed., Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis, 1978) argues that a mystic's tradition shapes the experience, so reports from one lineage are not independent samples [M]. Forman (1990) answers with the contentless "pure consciousness event"; Zaehner (1957) separates three kinds of mystical experience [M]. Tier [M]. 20. Hood's Mysticism Scale outside the West. Claim: Ralph Hood built a 32-item scale in 1975 (Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion 14) on Stace's introvertive and extrovertive criteria [M]. Hood and colleagues (JSSR 40, 2001) report a three-factor structure (introvertive, extrovertive, interpretation) confirmed in the United States and Iran [M paper, U title]. Chen, Hood, Watson and others studied Tibetan Buddhists and Chinese Buddhist monks and nuns (JSSR, about 2011); the structure partly replicates, with differences in the interpretation factor and in items using God language [U]. People in several cultures answer in a similar pattern, which supports a shared structure of reports; but the scale encodes Stace's list, and samples are literate and religious [M]. Tier [M] unless marked. Use: after checking, "in questionnaire studies in America, Iran and two Buddhist communities, the reports line up" is a safe line.

21. Pahnke, Griffiths and the MEQ. Claim: Pahnke's Good Friday Experiment (Marsh Chapel, Boston, 1962; thesis 1963; Pahnke and Richards 1966) gave ten divinity students psilocybin and ten nicotinic acid, double-blind, and scored nine categories drawn from Stace; the psilocybin group scored higher on most [M]. Doblin (1991) followed them up after 25 years and criticised the blinding and the guides [M]. Griffiths and colleagues (Psychopharmacology, 2006) ran 36 volunteers double-blind against an active placebo; about two thirds rated the session among the five most meaningful of their lives [M]. The MEQ30 (Barrett, Johnson and Griffiths, 2015) has four factors: mystical, positive mood, transcendence of time and space, ineffability [M]. Tier [M]. Use: citeable for "the experience can be elicited and measured". The samples were Christian students and spiritually active Westerners scored on a Western list, so it does not test whether independent traditions share it.

22. Cognitive science of religion. Claim: Boyer (Religion Explained, 2001), Atran (In Gods We Trust, 2002), Barrett (Why Would Anyone Believe in God?, 2004), Bloom (Descartes' Baby, 2004) and Bering (Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2006, on souls) argue that intuitions about agents, minds that outlast bodies and moral accounting come from ordinary cognition, so similar ideas recur across cultures [M]. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010) show most samples are "WEIRD" [M]. Tier [M]. Use: this predicts a continuing soul and karma-like justice anywhere, true or not. Convergence therefore fits either view and cannot alone support the axiom.

23. Jung, psychic unity and the logic of independent witnesses. Claim: Jung (the collective unconscious, 1936) proposed inherited forms of imagination yielding similar symbols without transmission and offered the "Solar Phallus Man" case; Shamdasani (Cult Fictions, 1998) and others point to cryptomnesia and Jung's access to the source, and Jung later said archetypes are forms, not inherited images [M]. Behind him stand Bastian's Elementargedanken and Tylor's psychic unity, against the diffusionists; Boas (1896) argued similarity alone cannot tell invention from borrowing; Galton (1889) noted that cultures cannot be counted as independent samples when they share descent or contact [M]. Condorcet (1785) and Hume (Enquiry, section 10, 1748, on rival miracles cancelling) both need independence for agreement to count [M].  Tier [M]. Use: the formal reason to group witnesses into hubs. ## 4. How they compare: the method

Jaspers and Bellah compare by date and structure and treat parallel timing as the finding. Müller compared words and myths to show descent. Guénon and Schuon compare symbols and doctrines and explain agreement by one source, so contact is irrelevant to them. Blavatsky adds a historical story (a brotherhood, a lost parent doctrine) and treats agreement as inherited. Hick compares functions. James, Stace, Pahnke, Hood and Griffiths compare reports against a fixed list that comes from Stace. Jung compares images and posits a shared psyche. Cognitive scientists compare the mental tools that produce beliefs. Anthropologists since Galton ask whether the units compared are independent at all.

Example. A soul that continues can be explained by revelation, contact, shared ancestry (Indo-European or Near Eastern), shared experience, shared cognition, shared problems (death, justice), shared translators, or shared logic, which is Alex's own. Each sets a different bar for "independent".

## 5. What scholars say

Historians of religion mostly accept that traditions changed one another and that the "perennial" frame grew in the West in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. King, Halbfass and Hacker treat the Hindu universalist voice as partly a response to Orientalism; Sharf and McMahan treat modern Buddhist emphasis on experience as an import. The Axial Age is used as a heuristic and doubted as a period. Specialists treat India and Greece contact as certain after Alexander and unproven before. Katz's contextualism dominated the 1980s, Forman answered it, and no consensus exists. Psychologists find the Hood and MEQ structures replicable but Western-based. Academic opinion of Theosophy's descent claim is poor, though its influence on the comparative frame is acknowledged.
