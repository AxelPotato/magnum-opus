## 1. The picture in brief

The trouble that line 17 names, "if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing", has a name in analytic philosophy of mind: the decombination problem. It mirrors the combination problem, which James posed in 1890 against the idea that many small feelings add up to a big one. Cosmopsychism says the cosmos as a whole is the one basic subject. Its frame is Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism (2010): the whole is basic, the parts real but derived. Shani, Nagasawa and Wager, and Goff defend versions. Kastrup's analytic idealism adds a mechanism by analogy, dissociation. Chalmers (2019) and Miller (2017) press the objection that a subject containing subjects is no easier than subjects adding up. Miri Albahari (2019) is the one analytic author who builds on the mystics on purpose.

The problem is older than all of them. Advaita (Gaudapada, Shankara) says the question belongs to the level of appearance and that ignorance has no beginning. Ramanuja, about nine hundred years ago, gave the sharpest form of the objection: where does the ignorance sit? Western idealists asked it as the problem of finite centres, and James made it Lectures IV and V of A Pluralistic Universe.

Clause three of the axiom (the separation ends) has almost no analytic literature. It is the religious clause. Most of these authors do not claim that religions agree; they sort theories by logical form. The comparing is done by the perennialists, Schrödinger, Albahari and Kastrup.

Audit of the opening: broadly sound. Four items need work: the Schrödinger "religion, not science" locator, the "most neuroscientists" sentence (no survey), the "God or Nature" locator (Ethics IV), and "free will is a story the brain tells".

## 2. Findings

### Part A. The literature

1. Priority monism.
Claim: existence monism says there is one concrete thing. Priority monism says there are many but the cosmos is the one basic thing and the parts depend on it.
Who: Jonathan Schaffer, "Monism: The Priority of the Whole", Philosophical Review 119(1), 2010 (pages [U]); "Is There a Fundamental Level?", Noûs, 2003; the Stanford Encyclopedia entry "Monism" [M].
The words (paraphrase): three arguments. Gunk: if matter has no smallest parts, there is no bottom-up base, so only the top can be basic. Entanglement: an entangled system has a definite state as a whole while its parts have none of their own, so dependence runs from whole to part. And the Aristotelian slogan that the whole is prior to the part.
Traditions: he lists the monist pedigree as Parmenides, Plotinus, Spinoza, Hegel, Bradley, Royce [M]; he does not compare religions.
Tier: [M].
Use: gives clause one an academic name without any claim about mind. "Parts real but derived" fits Alex's wall between programs, real inside the system.

Nagasawa and Wager join Schaffer's frame to panpsychism under the label "priority cosmopsychism" [U on the coinage].
Traditions: monist religious thought as precedent, with Neoplatonic, Spinozist and Vedantic names [U].

5. Kastrup and the dissociation analogy.
Claim: reality is one universal mind. The inanimate world is the outside appearance of its activity. A living organism is the outside appearance of a dissociated part of it, as an "alter" in dissociative identity disorder looks like a person from outside and a separate stream of experience from inside.

The words (paraphrase): dissociation is a natural process that at the cosmic scale needs no trauma. DID is the one known case in which a single mind presents as several centres divided by memory barriers.
Objections in the literature: DID is a contested diagnosis, and where it occurs the alters share one brain and one body, so the analogy borrows the unity it is meant to explain; it names a process but not the cosmic cause or purpose; critics treat it as a pointer to an answer and not an answer [named critics U].
Traditions: Schopenhauer and Jung (his Decoding books, 2020 and 2021); More Than Allegory (2016) reads religious myths as symbolic statements of metaphysical truth [M].
Tier: [M] works, [U] criticisms.
Use: line 21 says critics ask why and how one mind would split. Add the clause that matters: the analogy is with dissociative identity disorder, which is itself disputed and happens inside one brain.

9. Advaita and Schopenhauer: the problem at the level of appearance.
Claim: from the highest standpoint nothing has been divided, so the problem does not arise; at the working level ignorance is beginningless and neither real nor unreal.
Who: Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 2.32 (repeated at 3.48): no dissolution, no origination, no one bound, no seeker, no one liberated; this is the highest truth [M, paraphrase]. Later schools dispute where ignorance sits: the Bhamati school (Vacaspati Mishra) puts it in the individual self with Brahman as object, the Vivarana school (Prakasatman) in Brahman as seat and object [M for the split, U for details].

10. Ramanuja's objection.
Claim: Advaita's avidya cannot be placed. In the individual soul, it is circular, since the soul is its product. In Brahman, pure self-luminous knowledge, it contradicts scripture.

Tier: [M] that the objection exists and concerns the locus, [U] the numbered list.
Use: Ramanuja is a premodern priority monist with no illusion: the many are real and dependent. That is where the schools split, and it bears on line 45, where Alex defines illusion as a wrong reading of something real. "Where does the ignorance sit" is the question a fair reader will ask, and it is nine hundred years old.

## 4. How they compare

Analytic philosophers sort; perennialists match.

Schaffer, Nagasawa and Wager, Shani and Chalmers classify. They lay out the logical possibilities (existence or priority monism; micro or cosmic; Berkeleyan or Kantian) and slot historical figures in: Spinoza as a monist, Fechner as a cosmopsychist, Bradley as an absolute idealist. The comparison is by logical form and never claims two religions say the same thing. A Vedantin and a Neoplatonist can sit in one box and mean different things.

Goff compares explanatory hypotheses: in Why? theism, axiarchism and cosmic purpose compete. Religions appear as rivals.

Albahari takes mystical reports as data: she groups reports from contemplative traditions around witness-consciousness and asks which philosophy of mind they favour. Differences between traditions are mostly doctrinal overlay. This is the nearest to the perennial method.

Kastrup reads religions as allegory: myths are symbolic reports of one metaphysical reality, with Schopenhauer and Jung as translators. Schrödinger starts from a puzzle (one world, many minds) and cites the Upanishads and the mystics as precedent, not proof. Ramanuja and the Advaitins compare from inside, as rivals reading the same scripture; Ramanuja's question separates the schools more than any survey unites them.

How differences are explained: Albahari and Kastrup say surface differences are clothing; Goff and Chalmers treat them as real rivals; Ramanuja says the question is whether the many are an error or a real dependence, and Advaita declines it. A fair essay can say all three are live.

## 5. What scholars say

Priority monism is a respected minority position in metaphysics. Critics doubt the gunk argument and say entanglement does not force holism. Cosmopsychism is a minority within panpsychism, itself a minority. The standing objection is decombination (Miller 2017); another is that a cosmic mind either knows everything we know or explains nothing about why the world has the shape physics finds.

Kastrup is widely read and seldom taken up in academic journals; the dissociation analogy is the weak point. Hoffman's mild thesis is accepted, his theorem has been challenged by simulation work (Berke and others 2022), and the step from a non-veridical interface to consciousness-first is one even sympathisers do not treat as following.

The standard challenge to common-core arguments such as Albahari's is constructivism: Steven Katz (1978) argues that mystical experience is shaped by tradition and shows no common core; Robert Forman's pure consciousness event (1990) argues the opposite [M].
