FACT CHECK, PART TWO: religions and comparative religion. No files were written or changed. Search tools only. Where a result came from a Theosophical or devotional site, I say so.

Legend: VERIFIED = a search result naming a source supports it. PLAUSIBLE = standard knowledge, not confirmed here. WRONG = correction given.

=== A. THEOSOPHY ===

A1. Founded in New York on 17 November 1875 by Blavatsky, Olcott, William Quan Judge and others. VERIFIED (Wikipedia "Theosophical Society", theosophical.org, Britannica).
A2. Isis Unveiled 1877; The Secret Doctrine 1888 (subtitle "The Synthesis of Science, Religion, and Philosophy"). VERIFIED (Wikipedia, Blavatsky writings pages). Scholars note Isis Unveiled is vague about reincarnation; The Secret Doctrine is where it is worked out.
A3. Motto "There is no religion higher than truth" translates the Sanskrit "satyan nasti paro dharmah". It was adopted around 1880 after Olcott and Blavatsky visited Benares, from the family motto of the Maharaja of Benares. VERIFIED (theosophy.wiki, Theosophical sources). It was not part of the 1875 founding.
A4. Objects. VERIFIED (theosophy.wiki "Objects", ts-adyar.org/objects, Wikipedia):
- 1875 preamble: one aim only, "to collect and diffuse a knowledge of the laws which govern the universe."
- 1886 form: (1) nucleus of a Universal Brotherhood without distinction of race, creed or colour; (2) promote study of Aryan and other Eastern literatures, religions and sciences; (3) investigate unexplained laws of nature and the psychical powers of man.
- Current Adyar wording, adopted in 1896: (1) "To form a nucleus of the Universal Brotherhood of Humanity, without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour"; (2) "To encourage the study of Comparative Religion, Philosophy and Science"; (3) "To investigate unexplained laws of Nature and the powers latent in man."
- So the comparative study of religion in its current form is a later wording. One search result listed only two objects for 1888; treat that as unreliable.
A5. Karma and reincarnation in the West. PLAUSIBLE, with caveats. Theosophy was a main popularizer in English. But Allan Kardec's Spiritism (Le Livre des Esprits, 1857) taught reincarnation earlier (VERIFIED, Psi Encyclopedia). Olcott also helped revive Buddhism in Sri Lanka and wrote a Buddhist Catechism (VERIFIED, Wikipedia).
A6. Hodgson Report. VERIFIED (SPR Psi Encyclopedia, Wikipedia).
- Richard Hodgson went to Adyar for the SPR committee after the Coulomb allegations. The report was published in December 1885 in the SPR Proceedings.
- It concluded that the Mahatma letters and phenomena were fraudulent and that Blavatsky was the author. It closes with the verdict that she was "one of the most accomplished, ingenious, and interesting impostors in history."
- It was assumed to be the SPR's opinion, but it never had the status of an official SPR document.
- The Coulomb letters have not survived.
A7. Harrison. VERIFIED.
- "J'Accuse: An Examination of the Hodgson Report of 1885", Journal of the SPR vol 53, April 1986, pp. 286-310. A book, "H. P. Blavatsky and the SPR", followed in 1997 (Theosophical University Press).
- Harrison was a long-time SPR member and a forgery and handwriting expert.
- He called the report "riddled with slanted statements, conjectures advanced as fact or probable fact, uncorroborated testimony of unnamed witnesses, selection of evidence and downright falsity." He also called it "a highly partisan document forfeiting all claim to scientific impartiality." In 1997 he wrote that it was "even worse than I had thought."
- From the Mahatma letters in the British Library he found three distinct hands: Blavatsky and the two main Mahatma writers.
- Harrison argued the case against her was not proved. He did not prove that the Mahatmas existed.
- What the SPR said: the journal published him. Theosophical sites and Wikipedia say the editor wrote that it did so "in the interests of truth and fair play, and to make amends for whatever offence we may have given," and that the SPR issued a press release on 8 May 1986. That detail comes from partisan-leaning sources, so PLAUSIBLE. Safest print wording: "the SPR's own journal published Harrison's critique in 1986."
A8. Other criticism. VERIFIED. William Emmette Coleman (1895) listed uncredited sources behind Blavatsky's writings, with disputed claims about how much was copied. Also expect criticism of the "root races" doctrine (PLAUSIBLE; not searched in detail).
A9. Scholarly sources. VERIFIED unless noted: Joscelyn Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment (SUNY, 1994); Bruce F. Campbell, Ancient Wisdom Revived: A History of the Theosophical Movement (University of California Press, 1980; Campbell asks Theosophists for a more balanced picture of Blavatsky); Olav Hammer and Mikael Rothstein (eds), Handbook of the Theosophical Current (Brill, 2013). PLAUSIBLE: Hammer, Claiming Knowledge (2001); Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture (1996).
A10. Mainstream view. PLAUSIBLE synthesis. Scholars of Western esotericism treat Theosophy as an important modern esoteric movement and a channel for Asian ideas into the West. They do not accept its picture of one ancient Wisdom Religion behind all faiths, or the Masters, as history. The nineteenth century comparative work is filtered through occult sources.

=== B. THE PERENNIAL PHILOSOPHY ===

B1. Steuco, De perenni philosophia libri X, 1540. VERIFIED (Wikipedia, Lexicon Philosophicum). Steuco was a Vatican librarian.
B2. Leibniz. VERIFIED. Letter to Nicolas Remond, 26 August 1714, with the phrase "perennis quaedam philosophia"; it also praises the "Orientals." Leibniz made it progressive, not a fixed ancient revelation. Huxley says Leibniz coined the phrase. WRONG, Steuco used it first.
B3. Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945), opening of the Foreword. Multiple secondary quotations agree on this wording. VERIFIED in secondary quotation only, so check against the printed book before quoting: "Philosophia perennis, the phrase was coined by Leibniz; but the thing, the metaphysic that recognizes a divine Reality substantial to the world of things and lives and minds; the psychology that finds in the soul something similar to, or even identical with, divine Reality; the ethic that places man's final end in the knowledge of the immanent and transcendent Ground of all being, the thing is immemorial and universal." The "Highest Common Factor ... in its chemically pure state" line is also quoted, but I did not confirm where it sits.
B4. Huxley's anthology. VERIFIED. Short passages arranged by theme, with commentary. Sources include Eckhart, John of the Cross, William Law, Rumi and Sufis, Lao Tzu, the Upanishads, the Gita, the Tibetan Book of the Dead, the Diamond Sutra and Zen.
B5. Schuon. VERIFIED. De l'unite transcendante des religions, 1948; English (trans. Peter Townsend, Faber) 1953. T. S. Eliot praised it. Idea: religions differ at the exoteric level of form and law and meet at the esoteric level.
B6. Traditionalists.
- Guenon: founder of the Traditionalist school, believed in a primordial tradition and attacked modernity. PLAUSIBLE.
- Coomaraswamy: art historian and a principal Traditionalist. VERIFIED (named as a principal thinker).
- Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth: The Common Vision of the World's Religions, 1976. VERIFIED.
B7. William James, Varieties of Religious Experience (1902). VERIFIED fragment: "In Hinduism, in Neoplatonism, in Sufism, in Christian mysticism ... we find the same recurring note, so that there is about mystical utterances an eternal unanimity which ought to make a critic stop and think." James also held that mystical states have authority for those who have them but do not bind others. PLAUSIBLE.
B8. Critiques.
- Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", in Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (OUP, 1978): "There are NO pure (i.e. unmediated) experiences." VERIFIED.
- Wayne Proudfoot, Religious Experience (University of California Press, 1985): experience presupposes beliefs and concepts. VERIFIED.
- Robert Sharf, "Experience", in Critical Terms for Religious Studies (ed. Mark C. Taylor, University of Chicago Press). VERIFIED: meaning comes from public discourse, and "experience" was made into an unassailable category.
- Jonathan Z. Smith: religion is a category scholars imagine, and "the map is not the territory". VERIFIED loosely.
- R. C. Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957): monistic and theistic mysticism differ. VERIFIED.
- Stephen Prothero, God Is Not One (2010): the religions differ. VERIFIED. A reviewer notes he ignores the earlier scholarly critique of perennialism.
B9. Replies. VERIFIED. Robert Forman's "pure consciousness event" answers Katz. John Hick's pluralism says the traditions respond to one ultimate "Real" that cannot be described. W. T. Stace distinguished introvertive from extrovertive mysticism.
B10. Fair summary. PLAUSIBLE, backed by a Cambridge-linked review noting a "sharp backlash" in religious studies. Many scholars now reject strong perennialism (all mystical experience is the same) and are wary of comparison itself. A weaker, family-resemblance view survives, as in Hick, Forman and Huston Smith.

=== C. "SAME THING, DIFFERENT WORDS" PASSAGES ===

C1. Rigveda 1.164.46, "ekam sad vipra bahudha vadanti." VERIFIED verse number. Griffith: "To what is One, sages give many a title: they call it Agni, Yama, Matarisvan." Usual rendering: "Truth is one, sages call it by various names." The full verse also names Indra, Mitra, Varuna. It sits in the Asya Vamasya riddle hymn. Do not overreach: scholars read it as emerging monism, as a single referent behind the gods, or as just the fire. I could not confirm Jamison and Brereton's wording.
C2. Blind men and elephant.
- VERIFIED: Udana 6.4 in the Pali canon (Titthiya or Tittha Sutta). It is used of quarrelling sectarians who each see one side.
- Dating: Wikipedia says about 500 BCE for the Buddhist version and "before 5th century BCE" for Jain agamas. I would not print those dates. Safer is "an early Pali text".
- VERIFIED: the Jain use for anekantavada in Vidyananda's Tattvarthaslokavartika (9th century) and Mallisena's Syadvadamanjari (13th century).
- VERIFIED: Rumi, Masnavi Book III, about verses 1259-1268, elephant in a dark house, with the earlier Persian version in Sana'i (d. 1131). Rumi died in 1273.
- Do not overreach: in the Udana the Buddha is criticising dogmatic sects, not saying each holds a piece of one truth.
C3. Ramakrishna. The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna is Mahendranath Gupta's Bengali Kathamrita (5 vols, 1902-1932), translated by Nikhilananda in 1942. VERIFIED. VERIFIED: the lake with many ghats, where Hindus say "jal", Muslims "pani" and Christians "water", one substance under different names. The slogan "yato mat tato path" is widely attributed, but I could not confirm it verbatim in the Kathamrita. Safest: cite the ghats parable. He is said to have practised Islam from late 1866 and Christianity from late 1874 (Ramakrishna Mission accounts, which are devotional).
C4. Quran 5:48. VERIFIED (Yusuf Ali, via search): "To each among you have we prescribed a law and an open way. If Allah had so willed, He would have made you a single people, but (His plan is) to test you in what He hath given you: so strive as in a race in all virtues. The goal of you all is to Allah." Modern pluralist readings (Nurcholish Madjid, Asghar Ali Engineer, Abdulaziz Sachedina) call it a charter for diversity. PLAUSIBLE: older commentaries read it, in a context about judging by revelation and the Quran confirming earlier scripture, as separate laws for separate communities. Do not claim it says all religions are the same.

=== D. ILLUSION AND THE ONE ===

D1. Advaita. All VERIFIED.
- Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7: "That which is the finest essence, this whole world has that as its self. That is the real. That is the self. That you are, Svetaketu." The refrain recurs through chapter 6.
- Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10: "In the beginning this world was only brahman, and it knew only itself, thinking: 'I am brahman.' As a result, it became the Whole."
- Isha Upanishad 6 and 7: whoever sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings "shrinks not" from anything; for one who sees oneness, "what delusion, what sorrow?" The Sanskrit verb is vijugupsate: recoil, revulsion. "No hatred" is a looser but common rendering.
- Maya and mithya: scholars say mithya means dependent, neither absolutely real nor absolutely unreal, and that "illusion" misleads. Shankara separates waking from dream (Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.2.29). "Brahma satyam jagan mithya" is a traditional summary line, not a quote from the commentaries. PLAUSIBLE.
D2. Buddhism.
- VERIFIED: avidya is the first of the twelve links of dependent origination. The Heart Sutra Sanskrit is "rupam sunyata sunyataiva rupam."
- OVERREACH FLAG: Buddhist emptiness means lack of inherent nature. It is not a One or a Whole. All Buddhist schools reject the Atman, so Advaita and Buddhism disagree about what is left when ignorance ends (VERIFIED, Bodhi, access-to-insight essays).
D3. Plato's cave, Republic Book VII, 514a to 517a. VERIFIED. Prisoners take shadows for reality, one is freed, climbs toward the sun (the Good), and returns. It concerns education and the Forms. It is not an account of a One experiencing separation.
D4. Zhuangzi chapter 2 (Qiwulun, "Equalizing Things", Watson). VERIFIED. The text adds "there must be some distinction", which it calls "the transformation of things" (wuhua). Commentators dispute the reading. Do not call it simply illusion.
D5. Daodejing 16. VERIFIED. Lau: "The myriad creatures all rise together and I watch their return. The teeming creatures all return to their separate roots. Returning to one's roots is known as stillness." Each thing returns to its own root, which is not necessarily one Whole. The text dates to roughly the 4th to 3rd century BCE (VERIFIED).
D6. Acts 17:28. VERIFIED. "In him we live and move and have our being." Paul quotes Greek poets. The first clause is linked to Epimenides, with no surviving text except a Syriac commentary. The second ("we are his offspring") is from Aratus or Cleanthes. Christian readings keep the Creator and creation distinct. Panentheistic readings exist.
D7. Kabbalah. VERIFIED. Isaac Luria (1534-1572) taught in Safed from about 1570 and wrote almost nothing; Hayyim Vital recorded his teaching. Ein Sof contracts (tzimtzum); vessels meant to hold divine light break (shevirat ha-kelim) and sparks scatter into husks; human action repairs the world (tikkun). Fair gloss: this is the closest Jewish structure to "fragmentation and repair", but it is a theology of a Creator and a commanded practice, not a claim that separation is an illusion.
D8. Neoplatonism.
- VERIFIED: Plotinus, from the One to Intellect to Soul, with the soul's return, "the flight of the alone to the Alone" (Enneads VI.9.11, via secondary source).
- VERIFIED: Proclus, Elements of Theology prop. 35: everything that proceeds from a cause "both remains in its cause and proceeds from it and reverts upon it" (mone, proodos, epistrophe). One scholar notes that for Proclus it is ontological, not eschatological.
D9. Lila. VERIFIED. Brahma Sutra 2.1.33, "lokavat tu lila kaivalyam". It answers the objection that creation needs a motive. Shankara compares Brahman to a king at play, a conventional explanation. The lila reading ("God plays at separation") belongs more to later devotional Vedanta.
D10. Hidden treasure. VERIFIED. "Kuntu kanzan makhfiyyan fa-ahbabtu an u'rafa..." Ibn Arabi cited it and said it was sound by unveiling (kashf) but not established by transmission. Ibn Taymiyya said it has no chain, neither sound nor weak. Zarkashi, Sakhawi and Ibn Hajar agree it has no basis (islamqa/hadithanswers). So hadith scholars do not accept it. It is widely quoted in Sufi literature.
D11. Wahdat al-wujud. VERIFIED. Specialists agree Ibn Arabi did not use the phrase. It came into use among his later school (Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi is credited), and Ibn Taymiyya used it as a charge. Who coined it is partly disputed, and so is how far it fits Ibn Arabi. Chittick stresses that he held transcendence and immanence together.

=== E. SOUL, REBIRTH, THE SPACE BETWEEN LIVES ===

E1. Gita 2.13 and 2.22. VERIFIED. 2.13: as the embodied one passes through childhood, youth and old age, so it passes to another body, and the wise are not deluded. 2.22: as a person puts on new garments, giving up old ones, so the embodied one takes new bodies.
E2. Plato. VERIFIED. The Phaedo gives several arguments (cyclical, recollection, affinity, and a final one from Forms). Republic X (Myth of Er, 614b-621d): souls draw lots and choose next lives, then drink from the river of forgetfulness. "The responsibility is with the chooser."
E3. Bardo Thodol. VERIFIED. It belongs to a terma cycle revealed by Karma Lingpa (1326-1386), with the tradition crediting Padmasambhava. It guides the consciousness through the bardo between death and rebirth. The English title and framing come from Evans-Wentz's 1927 Oxford edition (translated by Kazi Dawa Samdup). Evans-Wentz was a Theosophist (joined Point Loma in 1902), and scholars say his preconceptions shaped the presentation. This is a good link between Theosophy and Western reception.
E4. Buddhist rebirth without a self. VERIFIED. In the Milindapanha, Nagasena uses the lamp lit from another lamp: the new consciousness is "neither the same nor another." The text is later than the main Pali Nikayas, perhaps the 1st century BCE or CE (PLAUSIBLE).
OVERREACH FLAG: Buddhist schools split on a state between lives (antarabhava). Sarvastivada, Sammitiya and others accepted it. Theravada, Mahasanghika and Vibhajyavada rejected it (VERIFIED, Wikipedia "Bardo"). So "the soul rests between lives" is Tibetan and Sarvastivada, not Buddhism in general.
E5. Jainism: each soul (jiva) is bound by karma, understood as subtle matter, and transmigrates until it is freed; there is no creator. VERIFIED. Sikhism: rebirth until union with Waheguru, with liberation by grace. VERIFIED, including the Japji line "by karma the robe, by grace the door of liberation". I am fairly sure it is in pauri 4. PLAUSIBLE on the pauri number. Skip the "84 lakh" figure; sources conflict.
E6. Gilgul. VERIFIED. It appears in the Bahir (12th to 13th century) and the Zohar, and is developed in Lurianic Kabbalah (Hayyim Vital, Sha'ar ha-Gilgulim). Saadia Gaon (10th century, Emunot ve-De'ot, treatise 6) rejected it. So Judaism has no single position.
E7. Druze: taqammus; the soul passes at death directly into a new body ("shirt", qamis). VERIFIED.
E8. Origen. VERIFIED.
- He held that souls pre-exist their bodies. He rejected metensomatosis (Contra Celsum).
- A local synod at Constantinople in 543, with Justinian's edict, condemned Origenism.
- The Second Council of Constantinople (553) named Origen among heretics in one of its general anathemas.
- The famous fifteen anathemas against Origenists are, per Richard Price and Tanner, not attributable to the council itself.
- What was condemned was pre-existence and universal restoration (apokatastasis). Reincarnation was not the target.
- WRONG if the essay says "the 553 council banned reincarnation". That claim is popular in Theosophical and New Age writing and is not supported by the scholarship.
E9. Mainstream positions. One life, then resurrection and judgment: Hebrews 9:27, "appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment". Islam: resurrection (ba'th). VERIFIED that tanasukh is generally denied in Sunni and Twelver Shia thought, e.g. al-Mufid, and accepted by Ghulat, Nusayri and Druze groups. Judaism is split (E6). Valentinus and Basilides are said to have held reincarnation. PLAUSIBLE.
E10. Stevenson and Tucker. VERIFIED.
- Stevenson (University of Virginia): Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966); Cases of the Reincarnation Type (4 vols, 1975-83); Reincarnation and Biology (1997, on birthmarks).
- UVA's Division of Perceptual Studies reports over 2,500 cases; one source says 65 detailed reports published.
- Tucker: Life Before Life (2005). Return to Life (2013) is PLAUSIBLE.
- Criticism: Paul Edwards, Reincarnation: A Critical Examination (1996). Others cite cultural expectation (Keith Augustine), parental suggestion, cryptomnesia, selective reporting, and the fact that most cases come from cultures that already believe in rebirth.
- Mainstream: most scientists ignore or dismiss the work. Carl Sagan, in The Demon-Haunted World, listed children reporting past lives as one of three ESP claims that "deserve serious study", and said he did not think it likely to be valid (VERIFIED).

=== F. KARMA, GOLDEN RULE, "DONE TO MYSELF" ===

F1. Galatians 6:7. VERIFIED. "Do not be deceived: God is not mocked, for whatever one sows, that will he also reap." It is moral accountability under God, not rebirth.
F2. Matthew 7:12. VERIFIED. Do to others whatever you would have them do to you; "this is the Law and the Prophets". The negative form is already in Tobit 4:15 (VERIFIED).
Matthew 25:40. PLAUSIBLE, not confirmed here. "As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." It is the Last Judgment parable. It is Christ's identification with the needy, not a claim that all beings are one.
F3. Leviticus 19:18, "love your neighbour as yourself". VERIFIED. Hillel, Babylonian Talmud Shabbat 31a: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour; that is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary; go and learn." VERIFIED.
F4. Quran 17:7. VERIFIED. "If you do good, you do good to yourselves; and if you do evil, it is to them." It speaks to the Children of Israel. Quran 99:7-8: whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it. VERIFIED.
F5. Hadith. VERIFIED. "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." It is Bukhari 13 and Muslim 45, and no. 13 in Nawawi's Forty. Agreed upon (muttafaq alayh). Nawawi reads "brother" broadly.
F6. Analects 15.24 (15.23 in Legge). VERIFIED. Zigong asks for one word to guide life. The Master says "shu" (reciprocity): do not impose on others what you do not desire for yourself. Analects 5.12 is a related passage.
F7. Mahabharata. NOT CONFIRMED. I could not settle the numbering.
- Anushasana Parva: one source gives the Sanskrit "na tat parasya samdadyat pratikulam yad atmanah" at 13.114.8, while Ganguli's translation labels the section CXIII. So 13.113.8 and 13.114.8 are edition differences.
- "5.1517" is how 19th century compilations (Monier-Williams and Muller era) cite the Udyoga Parva (Vidura) passage in the old Calcutta edition. PLAUSIBLE, not confirmed.
- I could not confirm 13.5571.
- Safe print: "Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, about chapter 113 or 114 (edition numbering varies), verse 8."
F8. Udanavarga 5.18. VERIFIED as cited: "Treat not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful." It is the northern Buddhist counterpart of the Dhammapada. The Pali parallel at Dhammapada 129-130 is PLAUSIBLE, not confirmed here.
F9. Anguttara Nikaya 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta). VERIFIED. "Cetanaham, bhikkhave, kammam vadami": it is intention, monks, that I call kamma; having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind.
F10. Karma, what it is not. PLAUSIBLE as the standard account. See Obeyesekere, Imagining Karma, and O'Flaherty (ed.), Karma and Rebirth in Classical Indian Traditions. Buddhist karma is intentional action and its results through cause and effect, with no judge. The Buddha also rejected the view that everything is due to past karma (SN 36.21; PLAUSIBLE). Jainism treats karma as subtle matter. Theistic Hindu schools give God a role in administering results, so "no divine involvement" is wrong for them. Advaita places karma within the realm of apparent individuals, to be transcended by knowledge.

=== G. BIRTH ===

G1. John 3:3, 3:7. VERIFIED. Greek anothen means both "again" and "from above". The Gospel plays on both: Nicodemus hears "a second time into the womb" (3:4), and Jesus answers "of water and Spirit" (3:5). Translators who pick one lose the other.
G2. Dvija. VERIFIED. Manusmriti 2.169: the first birth is from the mother, the second at the initiation (tying of the girdle), the third at consecration for Vedic sacrifice. The upanayana was for the three upper varnas. Caution: the term is tied to caste and ritual in the Dharmashastra. I did not verify the later verse that names the teacher as father.
G3. Eckhart. VERIFIED.
- Central theme of his German sermons: the Word or Son is born in the ground (the "spark") of the soul.
- Cologne inquiry from 1326; he died in Avignon about 1328 during the proceedings.
- Pope John XXII's bull In agro dominico (27 March 1329) condemned 28 propositions (17 heretical, 11 suspect). The Vatican said in 1992 that he himself was not condemned.
- I could not confirm the sermon number. Walshe numbering "Sermon 101" (Dum medium silentium, a Christmas sermon on the Word) is PLAUSIBLE; confirm before citing. Safer: "his German Christmas sermons".
G4. Parallels.
- Corpus Hermeticum XIII is titled "On being born again". VERIFIED.
- Gospel of Philip links rebirth to baptism and the bridal chamber. VERIFIED.
- Gospel of Thomas saying 22: "when you make the two one ... you will enter the kingdom." VERIFIED.
- Zen "great death": Blue Cliff Record case 41 (Zhaozhou) is PLAUSIBLE; Kapleau's The Wheel of Death is VERIFIED as a Zen source.
- Sufi fana and baqa, "die before you die": not verified. The hadith attribution of that saying is doubtful (PLAUSIBLE); do not rely on it.
G5. Most defensible cross tradition statement: many traditions use the image of a second birth, new birth or death and rising for a decisive change. They disagree on what is born, who brings it about, and whether it comes first or last. In John 3 and in dvija usage it is an entry or beginning. In Eckhart it is a lasting state. "Birth as the stage after the top of a school's scale" is the author's own extension of a shared image, not a doctrine any tradition states that way.

=== OVERREACH FLAGS (thesis) ===

1. "They all say the same thing" is too strong. What is defensible: several strands in many traditions treat separateness as not final. They disagree about what the One is and whether it is a someone.
2. The axiom ("one whole, experiencing separation, return") fits best with Advaita Vedanta, Neoplatonism, Ibn Arabi and Sufi unity of being, Lurianic Kabbalah in theistic form, and Theosophy itself. It does not fit Theravada (no self, no One, no in between state), Jainism (many eternal souls, no creator), or mainstream Judaism, Christianity and Islam (Creator and creature, one life, resurrection).
3. Golden rule texts mostly rest on reciprocity or empathy (Hillel, Analects, Mahabharata, Udanavarga). Only some rest on "the other is yourself": Isha Upanishad 6 and 7, Quran 17:7 partly, Matthew 25 loosely.
4. Karma and rebirth are not a "logical consequence" in the traditions' own history. They preceded any such derivation, and three major religions mostly deny rebirth.
5. Theosophy as comparer: lean on later scholars. Blavatsky's one ancient Wisdom Religion is a claim, not a finding. Its sources have been challenged (Coleman 1895), the Hodgson verdict is contested but not overturned in the way partisans suggest, and Evans-Wentz shows its filter on Tibetan texts.
6. Perennialism's strongest claims concern experience, and Katz, Proudfoot and Sharf attack exactly those. A narrower claim about concepts and structure is easier to defend, but then differences in doctrine count against "same".

=== SAFE SENTENCES FOR PRINT (no dashes) ===

The Theosophical Society was founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge and a small group of others.
Blavatsky published Isis Unveiled in 1877 and The Secret Doctrine in 1888, and Theosophy was one of the main routes by which English readers met karma and reincarnation in the late nineteenth century, alongside the earlier Spiritism of Allan Kardec.
The Society's motto, There is no religion higher than truth, renders a Sanskrit line it took up around 1880 from the family motto of the Maharaja of Benares.
Its first statement in 1875 spoke only of gathering knowledge of the laws that govern the universe; the three Objects quoted today, universal brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour, the study of comparative religion, philosophy and science, and the investigation of unexplained laws of nature and the powers latent in man, were worded and reworded over the years and took their present form in 1896.
In 1885 the Society for Psychical Research published a report by Richard Hodgson that called Blavatsky an impostor and said she had written the Mahatma letters herself.
In 1986 the handwriting expert Vernon Harrison, a long standing member of that same society, argued in its journal that Hodgson's method was biased and his evidence selective, and he found the letters were written in different hands; this does not show that the Mahatmas existed, only that the case against her was weaker than long assumed.
Scholars of religion treat Theosophy as an influential modern esoteric movement that helped make Asian religions a popular interest in the West, and most doubt its picture of one ancient wisdom behind all faiths and its claim of hidden Masters.
The phrase perennial philosophy goes back to the Italian scholar Agostino Steuco, whose book De perenni philosophia appeared in 1540; Leibniz used it in a letter of 1714, and Aldous Huxley made it famous in 1945.
Huxley described it as a metaphysic that sees a divine Reality behind the world, a psychology that finds something like or identical with that Reality in the soul, and an ethic that places the final aim of life in knowing it.
Huxley built his anthology from short passages by Christian mystics such as Eckhart and John of the Cross, from the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, and from Taoist, Buddhist and Sufi writers.
Frithjof Schuon argued in The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953) that religions differ in their outer forms and agree at their inner core.
William James wrote in 1902 that mystical reports from Hindu, Neoplatonic, Sufi and Christian sources keep returning to the same notes, a unanimity that a critic should stop and think about.
In 1978 Steven Katz argued that there is no pure unmediated mystical experience, that a mystic's tradition shapes what is experienced, and that the claim of a single common core is therefore unproven; scholars of religion today are mostly wary of strong perennialism, although defenders such as Robert Forman and John Hick offer more modest versions.
Rigveda 1.164.46 says that what exists is one and that the poets speak of it in many ways, calling it Indra, Mitra, Varuna or Agni.
In the Buddhist Udana (6.4) the Buddha tells of blind men who feel an elephant and quarrel, as an image of sectarians who each hold one side; Jain thinkers later used it for many sidedness, and Rumi retells it in the Masnavi with an elephant in a dark room.
Ramakrishna taught that different faiths are different paths to the same God, and he pictured them as the different landing steps of one lake where Hindus, Muslims and Christians draw the same water and call it by different names.
The Quran says that to each community God has given a law and a way, that God could have made one community, and that believers should compete in good deeds (5:48); some modern Muslim writers read this as support for religious diversity, while many traditional commentators read it within the view that the Quran confirms and completes earlier revelation.
In the Chandogya Upanishad (6.8.7) the father tells Svetaketu that the finest essence of which the whole world is made is the self, and that he is that; the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (1.4.10) says that in the beginning this world was only Brahman, which knew itself and thought, I am Brahman.
Advaita Vedanta calls the world mithya, meaning dependent and not ultimately real, and scholars warn that translating this as illusion misleads.
The Isha Upanishad (verses 6 and 7) says that one who sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings recoils from nothing, and asks what delusion or sorrow can remain for one who sees oneness.
Buddhism names ignorance, avidya, as the first link in the chain of dependent origination, and the Heart Sutra says that form is emptiness and emptiness is form; but Buddhists mean that things have no fixed independent nature, not that all is one whole, and Buddhist schools reject the Atman of the Upanishads.
In the cave of Plato's Republic (Book 7) prisoners take shadows for reality until one is freed and climbs toward the sun.
In the second chapter of the Zhuangzi, Zhuang Zhou wakes unsure whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man, and the text adds that there must be some distinction, which it calls the transformation of things.
Daodejing chapter 16 says that the myriad things flourish and each returns to its root, and that returning to the root is called stillness.
In Acts 17:28 Paul tells the Athenians that in God we live and move and have our being, in words drawn from Greek poetry.
In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria, taught in Safed in the sixteenth century, the infinite God withdraws to make room for a world, vessels meant to hold divine light break and scatter sparks, and human action helps repair the world.
Plotinus described all things as flowing from the One through Intellect and Soul, with the soul's task to turn back, and Proclus summed up the pattern as remaining, procession and return.
Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 answers the question why a complete God would create by calling creation play, lila, as in ordinary life.
The saying I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known is quoted by Sufis, notably Ibn Arabi, but hadith scholars do not accept it as a sound saying of the Prophet, and Ibn Arabi himself said it was authentic by mystical unveiling and not by a chain of transmission.
Ibn Arabi did not use the phrase unity of being, wahdat al-wujud, which was attached to his school by later followers and critics, and scholars still dispute how far it fits him.
The Bhagavad Gita (2.13 and 2.22) says that the embodied soul passes through childhood, youth and old age and then into another body, as a person puts on new clothes and leaves the worn ones.
In Plato's Phaedo Socrates gives several arguments that the soul survives death, and the Myth of Er at the end of the Republic tells of souls choosing their next lives before drinking from the river of forgetfulness.
The Tibetan Book of the Dead, revealed by Karma Lingpa in the fourteenth century, guides the dying through the bardo, the state between death and rebirth; the English title and framing come from W. Y. Evans-Wentz's 1927 edition, which was shaped by his Theosophical background.
Buddhist teaching holds rebirth without a permanent self, and in the Milindapanha the monk Nagasena compares it to a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same nor a different flame; Buddhist schools disagreed on whether there is a state between lives, with some accepting it and the Theravada rejecting it.
Jainism teaches that each soul is bound by karma and reborn until freed, and Sikh scripture also teaches rebirth, with liberation through divine grace.
Jewish mystics taught gilgul, the turning of souls, and Isaac Luria's circle developed it, while rationalists such as Saadia Gaon rejected it, so Judaism has no single position.
The Druze believe that at death a soul passes directly into a new human body.
Origen taught that souls exist before birth but rejected the transmigration of souls, and the sixth century condemnations of Origenism targeted pre-existence and the final restoration of all, not reincarnation as such.
Mainstream Christianity, Islam and most Judaism teach one earthly life followed by resurrection and judgment, and Muslim theologians who discussed the transmigration of souls, tanasukh, mostly rejected it, though some minority groups accepted it.
The psychiatrist Ian Stevenson of the University of Virginia published Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation in 1966 and went on to collect more than two thousand cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives, and his colleague Jim Tucker continued with Life Before Life in 2005; critics point to cultural expectation, parental influence, selective reporting and the lack of independent replication, and mainstream science does not accept the cases as evidence of rebirth.
Galatians 6:7 says that whatever a person sows, that will he also reap.
Matthew 7:12 gives the positive golden rule, do to others what you would have them do to you.
In the Talmud (Shabbat 31a) Hillel is reported to have said, What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour; that is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary; and Leviticus 19:18 says love your neighbour as yourself.
The Quran says that if you do good you do good to yourselves and if you do evil it is to yourselves (17:7), and that whoever does an atom's weight of good or evil will see it (99:7 and 99:8).
A saying reported in Bukhari and Muslim has the Prophet say that none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.
In Analects 15.24, numbered 15.23 in older translations, Confucius names reciprocity as the one word for conduct: do not impose on others what you do not want for yourself.
The Udanavarga (5.18) says to treat not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful, and the Mahabharata, in the Anushasana Parva, says that one should not do to others what is harmful to oneself, which it calls dharma in brief.
In the Anguttara Nikaya (6.63) the Buddha says that intention is what he calls kamma.
In classical Buddhist and Hindu teaching, karma means that intentional action has results through cause and effect, without a judge in the Buddhist account, so it is not simple fate, since present choices count, and not simple divine punishment.
Most golden rule texts rest on reciprocity or empathy, and only a few, such as the Isha Upanishad, rest on the idea that the other is oneself.
In John 3:3 and 3:7 the Greek word anothen can mean again or from above, and the Gospel plays on both meanings.
In Hindu tradition a boy of the upper three classes was called dvija, twice born, after the initiation that began his Vedic study, and Manusmriti 2.169 counts a first birth from the mother and a second at that initiation.
Meister Eckhart taught in his German sermons that God is born in the ground of the soul; after an inquiry that began in Cologne in 1326, Pope John XXII condemned twenty eight propositions from his work in 1329, after his death.
Other traditions use similar language, such as Corpus Hermeticum XIII, titled On Being Born Again, the Gospel of Philip's rebirth through baptism and the bridal chamber, and the Zen talk of a great death followed by new life.
Many traditions speak of a new or second birth for a decisive change in a person, though what is born, how, and at what stage varies, so using birth for a step beyond the top of a school's scale extends a widely shared image in the author's own way.
