======== TERMS ========

SAMADHI
(a) Deep, steady absorption in which the mind's usual chatter stops and the sense of a separate watcher loosens.
(b) Yoga Sutras (Patanjali; YS 1.2 defines yoga as stilling the fluctuations of the mind; samadhi is the last of the eight limbs). Also Buddhism, where it is the concentration part of the path ("right concentration").
(c) Errors to avoid: calling it enlightenment itself. In Buddhism it is a training that can be reached without liberating insight. Translating it as "bliss" or "trance". Note that in modern Hindu usage "samadhi" also means a saint's death or tomb shrine.
Status: VERIFIED for YS 1.2 and the Buddhist usage as concentration, one of the three trainings (ghoshyoga.org, Tricycle, Britannica "samma samadhi"). The "last limb" and "tomb" points are PLAUSIBLE.

NIRVANA
(a) "Blowing out" or "cooling": the going out of the fires that keep suffering going (greed, hatred, delusion). Buddhist traditions call it the end of suffering.
(b) Buddhism (Pali nibbana).
(c) Errors to avoid: "heaven". "Annihilation or nothingness". "A blissful mental state" (Theravada treats it as unconditioned, not a mood). "Reachable only at death" (the tradition separates nirvana with remainder, in life, from final nirvana at death, which is PLAUSIBLE).
Status: VERIFIED for the etymology, three fires and unconditioned nature (Tricycle "Nirvana" entries, Wikipedia summary).

MOKSHA
(a) Liberation: release from the cycle of rebirth and from the ignorance that binds a person to it.
(b) Hindu traditions; it is the fourth of the classical four aims of life (dharma, artha, kama, moksha), which is PLAUSIBLE.
(c) Errors to avoid: "the Hindu word for heaven". Treating "Hinduism" as having one doctrine of it. Advaita holds that the self was never really separate from Brahman, so moksha removes ignorance rather than gaining something, and that it can happen while alive (jivanmukti). Dvaita holds release comes only at death.
Status: VERIFIED for the Advaita versus Dvaita split on liberation in life (advaita-vedanta.org archive, Univ. of Calgary jivanmukti item).

SATORI
(a) Japanese Zen word for sudden insight, a moment of waking up to how things are.
(b) Zen, especially Rinzai. Often paired with kensho ("seeing one's nature"). Usage varies by school, and some teachers use kensho for the first glimpse and satori for deeper realization.
(c) Errors to avoid: treating satori as a permanent finished state. Treating it as a drug-like peak. Saying all Zen aims at it (Soto and Dogen put practice and realization together and downplay chasing the experience).
Status: VERIFIED for the kensho/satori usage and the Soto emphasis (Tricycle, Treeleaf summaries). The "Rinzai treats it as the goal, Hakuin" point is VERIFIED only through a summary.

FANA and BAQA
(a) Fana is the passing away of the ego's sense of being the centre. Baqa is what remains or lasts after that, a return to ordinary life with the recognition intact.
(b) Sufism. Quranic root is Q 55:26-27: "everyone upon [the earth] will perish (fan) and there remains (yabqa) the face of your Lord". VERIFIED by quran.com text.
(c) Errors to avoid: fana as literal merging with God or becoming God. The mainstream (Junayd, Ghazali) rejects incarnation (hulul) and union (ittihad). Bayazid Bastami and Hallaj ("ana al-Haqq", executed 922) are the controversial ecstatic voices. Also avoid treating baqa as a footnote: for the "sober" school it is the point.
Status: VERIFIED for definitions and the sober versus ecstatic split (Wikipedia "Fana (Sufism)", Junayd of Baghdad summaries, al-Hujwiri's definition). I would not quote the line "baqa is the return to sobriety after intoxication" as Junayd's own words; the source for it (satyori.com) is weak.

THEOSIS
(a) In Eastern Orthodox Christianity, the lifelong process of coming to share in the life of God by grace, without ceasing to be a creature.
(b) Eastern Orthodoxy (Athanasius, Maximus, Gregory Palamas).
(c) 2 Peter 1:4 is real and is the standard proof text: "partakers of the divine nature" (KJV; Greek theias koinonoi physeos). VERIFIED (OrthodoxWiki, ETS Journal article, Catechism of the Catholic Church 460). Athanasius, On the Incarnation 54: "the Son of God became man that we might become God", VERIFIED as quoted in CCC 460. The theme is therefore not only Eastern: CCC 460 cites 2 Peter 1:4 and the same Athanasius line.
Errors to avoid: "becoming God in essence" or pantheism. Orthodox teaching says humans share God's energies and grace, never the divine essence. Also the line "becoming by grace what God is by nature" is often credited to Athanasius; it is closer to a later summary (Maximus tradition). Treat that attribution as PLAUSIBLY wrong and do not cite it.

"THE KINGDOM OF GOD IS WITHIN YOU" (Luke 17:21)
Greek: ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν.
- KJV: "within you". VERIFIED (KJV text, BibleHub). The KJV also has a margin "among you" (PLAUSIBLE).
- NRSV: "among you" (footnote "or within you", PLAUSIBLE). ESV: "in the midst of you". NASB: "in your midst". NIV 2011: "in your midst" with footnote "Or is within you". VERIFIED (BibleHub, biblestudytools, videobible).
- WRONG in one search source: it said NIV still says "within". That was the 1984 NIV. NIV 2011 changed to "in your midst". NKJV still reads "within you" (PLAUSIBLE).
- The Greek: entos is an adverb or preposition meaning "inside, within". Hymon is genitive plural "of you", so the phrase literally reads "inside of you (plural)". It appears only twice in the New Testament, here and Matthew 23:26 ("the inside of the cup"). VERIFIED (Blue Letter Bible, Bill Mounce summaries).
- The UBS translators' handbook lists three renderings: within you (in your heart), among you or in your midst (now or in the future), and within your reach. VERIFIED (tips.translation.bible).
- The main argument for "among": Jesus is answering Pharisees (v. 20), so "within you" addresses opponents, and the kingdom is present in his ministry. Anchor Bible Dictionary and RSV/ESV/NASB/NIV take "in the midst of you". VERIFIED.
- The main argument for "within": a serious minority position. Ilaria Ramelli, "Luke 17:21: 'The Kingdom of God is Inside You': The Ancient Syriac Versions in Support of the Correct Translation," Hugoye: Journal of Syriac Studies 12/2 (2009): 259-286, argues that "among" would normally use a different Greek idiom and cites Greek and Syriac patristic exegesis. VERIFIED (Hugoye abstract). One search-summary claim that "entos with a plural noun must mean among" is overstated. The grammar allows both.
- Gospel of Thomas, saying 3 (Lambdin translation, Coptic): "the kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you. When you come to know yourselves, then you will become known..." VERIFIED (earlychristianwritings.com, gospel-thomas.net listing). Context: 114 sayings, Coptic manuscript found at Nag Hammadi in 1945; Greek fragments (P.Oxy. 1, 654, 655) from about 200 to 250 CE. Most scholars date the composition to the second century, with some proposals earlier or later. VERIFIED (Wikipedia and other summaries). I could not confirm whether the Greek fragment P.Oxy. 654 reads "entos hymon" in this saying (it is damaged). Safest in print: cite only the Coptic saying and describe the book as "a second century collection of sayings found in Egypt in 1945, not part of the Bible."
- Safest: "within you in the old King James, among you or in your midst in most newer Bibles, because the Greek allows both."

THE TAO (DAO)
(a) "The Way": the unnameable source and pattern of how things go, which the sage learns to move with.
(b) Daoism, from the Daodejing (Laozi).
(c) Errors to avoid: "Tao means God". "One man named Lao Tzu wrote it in one sitting": the Guodian bamboo slips (c. 300 BCE) show earlier partial versions, scholarship doubts a single author, and Sima Qian's account is shaky. VERIFIED (Guodian summaries, SEP archive on Laozi). "Daoism is a philosophy, not a religion" is an over-neat split.
Opening line: "The way that can be spoken of is not the constant way" (many translations differ). VERIFIED (Ziporyn, Lynn).
WU WEI in one line: "effortless action": acting in tune with how things are, without forcing or strain. It does not mean doing nothing. VERIFIED (Slingerland, Effortless Action: Wu-wei as Conceptual Metaphor and Spiritual Ideal in Early China, Oxford University Press, 2003).

JACOB'S LADDER
Genesis 28:12 is the ladder verse. KJV: "And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it." VERIFIED (bible.com, biblia.com). The whole vision runs 28:10-17. Verse 17 is the "gate of heaven" line: "How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven." VERIFIED (biblestudytools KJV). That fits Alex's "door" image directly. Hebrew sullam occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible, so translations vary (ladder, stairway, ramp), which is a point to avoid asserting too firmly. VERIFIED (neverthirsty, ancientfaith blog summaries). John 1:51 reuses the image and John Climacus's "Ladder of Divine Ascent" (c. 7th century) turned it into stages of spiritual progress (PLAUSIBLE).

"STAIRWAY TO HEAVEN"
Led Zeppelin, released 8 November 1971 on their untitled fourth album (usually called Led Zeppelin IV), Atlantic, recorded between December 1970 and February 1971, mostly at Headley Grange. VERIFIED (Wikipedia song and album pages via search). Errors to avoid: claiming it was a hit single (PLAUSIBLE that it was never a standard commercial single at the time) and the backmasking myth.

"OCEAN OF CONSCIOUSNESS" / "SEA OF CONSCIOUSNESS"
Honest answer: as an exact phrase it is modern English. I found no canonical traditional source for it. The imagery is old, though.
- Upanishads: rivers entering the sea and losing name and form, Mundaka 3.2.8 and Chandogya 6.10. VERIFIED.
- Advaita: Shankara uses the ocean with waves, foam and bubbles in his Brahma Sutra commentary (2.1.13). VERIFIED through an Advaita-l summary; the section number deserves a check.
- Buddhism: Lankavatara Sutra has alaya-ocean and vijnana-waves verses (Suzuki translation, section IX per the Tsadra listing). VERIFIED as a heading. Note that "ocean of samsara" is a different common phrase and refers to suffering, not consciousness.
- Yoga Vasistha has cidarnava ("ocean of consciousness") language, VERIFIED only through a search summary. It is a late, medieval-era text.
- Modern: William James, "stream of consciousness" (Principles of Psychology, 1890), VERIFIED. Romain Rolland's "oceanic feeling" in a 1927 letter to Freud, VERIFIED.
Safe usage: say it is a modern phrase for an old image, and do not cite it to a tradition by name.

======== THE SIX STATEMENTS ========

1. BLAKE, HUXLEY, THE DOORS
- Blake wrote, in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, object (plate) 14: "If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, infinite. For man has closed himself up, till he sees all things thro' narrow chinks of his cavern." VERIFIED for the wording of the first sentence (Wikisource transcription, Blake Quarterly). "thro'" is Blake's spelling, and some quote sites print a colon instead of the comma after "is". Plate 14 is VERIFIED (Blake Archive numbering: Bentley, Erdman and Keynes all 14; Fitzwilliam copy H). Date: undated; usually given as c. 1790 to 1793, with recent arguments for 1790. VERIFIED (Viscomi, Blake Quarterly). Do not print "1793" as the date.
- Huxley, The Doors of Perception (1954), takes its title from that Blake line. VERIFIED (several sources). It describes his May 1953 mescaline experience. VERIFIED.
- The Doors took their name from Huxley's book; Jim Morrison proposed it. VERIFIED (Wikipedia, Britannica snippet, American Songwriter). The famous "there are things known and things unknown, and in between are the doors" is not from Blake or Huxley. Quote Investigator traces it to Ray Manzarek talking to Newsweek in 1967, and it is often wrongly credited to Morrison. VERIFIED.

2. "BEFORE ENLIGHTENMENT, CHOP WOOD, CARRY WATER"
- No traditional source found for the two line "before/after" form. Sites attribute it to Zen proverb or to the Buddha; those claims are unsupported.
- The real root is a verse by the lay Chan student Pang Yun (Layman Pang, c. 740 to 808 or 811): "My supernatural power and marvelous activity: drawing water and carrying firewood." The verb is often translated as "chopping," but the Chinese is closer to "carrying firewood." VERIFIED (Lapham's Quarterly, Tricycle, Beingwithoutself.org).
- Modern spread: Rick Fields et al., Chop Wood, Carry Water: A Guide to Finding Spiritual Fulfillment in Everyday Life (Tarcher, 1 December 1984), VERIFIED. One search result wrongly said "1970s." Jack Kornfield, After the Ecstasy, the Laundry (Bantam, 2000), VERIFIED. Van Morrison's 1990 album Enlightenment opens with "chop that wood, carry water," VERIFIED.
- I could not locate an earliest English appearance of the two line form. It is probably 20th century, but that is unproven.
- Safest print wording: "a saying that circulates in Zen circles in several versions." Optional anchor: a Tang dynasty layman named Pang called drawing water and carrying firewood his supernatural power.
- Other real Chan sources for "ordinary life is the point" (PLAUSIBLE, from knowledge not search): Nanquan's "ordinary mind is the Way" (Gateless Gate, case 19) and Dazhu Huihai's "when hungry, eat; when tired, sleep."

3. FIRST OPENING VERSUS FULL AWAKENING
Your draft: "Most traditions that speak of awakening separate a first opening from the full thing." This is a mild overclaim. "Most" cannot be shown, and some strands push back. Use "Several."
- Zen: kensho, "seeing one's nature," followed by long practice. Korean Seon master Chinul (12th century) taught "sudden awakening followed by gradual cultivation," drawing on Zongmi (780 to 841). Robert Buswell translated Chinul in The Korean Approach to Zen: The Collected Works of Chinul (1983). VERIFIED. Hakuin also stressed continuing training after awakening (PLAUSIBLE). Counter: Dogen and Soto treat practice and realization as one and treat kensho as passing scenery, and Chan subitists reject partial awakening. VERIFIED (Treeleaf, Tricycle summaries).
- Early Buddhism: four stages, stream enterer, once returner, non returner, arahant. Stream entry is the first, when nibbana is first taken as an object, and it drops the first fetters, including identity view. Final liberation takes more stages. VERIFIED (Wikipedia Sotapanna, Anagami, Britannica "arhat").
- Sufism: fana then baqa. Also the older distinction between hal (a passing state given by God, "descends into a man's heart without his being able to repel it or attract it") and maqam (a stable station earned through effort), per al-Hujwiri's Kashf al-Mahjub. VERIFIED (Wikipedia Maqam, Gibb Trust). The hal/maqam pair fits "glimpse versus settled" better than fana/baqa does.
- Christian three ways: purgative, illuminative, unitive (from Pseudo-Dionysius, systematized by Aquinas and later writers). VERIFIED (Catholic Encyclopedia). Warning: these are stages of a whole path for beginners, proficients and the perfect. They are not "glimpse versus full," and the first way is purification, not an opening. A closer Christian parallel is Teresa of Avila's spiritual betrothal followed by spiritual marriage in the Interior Castle (PLAUSIBLE).
Safest wording: "Several traditions separate a first opening from a settled realization, and they treat the first as the start of the work."

4. THE FELT SENSE OF A SEPARATE SELF
Your draft: "Many traditions point at the same suspect: the sense of being a separate thing." Fair in spirit; "the same suspect" overstates. Use "several contemplative traditions" and name the differences.
- Buddhism, anatta: strongest support. Stream entry drops "identity view" and the arahant drops the last conceit "I am." Scholars debate whether the Buddha taught "there is no self" or declined to assert either way. In SN 44.10 he stays silent when asked, and Thanissaro reads not-self as a strategy of letting go of identification, while others read it as a plain denial. VERIFIED (SN 44.10 summaries, Bodhi versus Thanissaro debate, Tricycle).
- Advaita Vedanta: strong, but with a different answer. The sense of "I am this body and mind, this is mine" is adhyasa, a superimposition on the true Self, and the Self (atman) is identical with Brahman, the whole. So "no separate self" here means the real Self is the whole. VERIFIED (Wikipedia Adhyasa, advaita.org.uk).
- Sufism: moderate to strong. Fana is the passing away of the self's claims, but mainstream Sufis keep the creator and creature distinction and reject literal union. Hallaj and Bastami are the exceptions. VERIFIED.
- Christian mysticism: weaker and differently framed. Eckhart's "ground of God is my ground" and detachment (gelâzenheit, "letting go") come closest; Heidegger later borrowed the word. VERIFIED that gelâzenheit means releasement or letting go and that Heidegger credited Eckhart. Eckhart's teaching was condemned in 1329 (In agro dominico, 28 propositions, 17 heretical and 11 suspect) VERIFIED. In theosis and in most Christian writing, the problem is self will and pride, and the cure is union by grace with a God who stays distinct. Gal 2:20 ("it is no longer I who live") is a Pauline anchor (PLAUSIBLE).
Differences to state: no self (Buddhism), true Self is the whole (Advaita), self emptied and united with God without becoming God (Sufism, Christianity).
Note for the essay: "letting go" has real historical weight in Eckhart's gelâzenheit.

5. KATZ AND THE PERENNIAL PHILOSOPHY
- Katz: Steven T. Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism," in Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (London: Sheldon Press; New York: Oxford University Press, 1978), pp. 22 to 74. One line: Katz argued that there are no pure, unmediated mystical experiences, so what a mystic experiences is shaped by the concepts and training of the tradition, and the experiences should not be assumed to be the same across traditions. VERIFIED (philosophyofreligion.org, Cambridge reviews, BU Wildman review). His own words are reported as "there are no pure experiences"; check the exact phrasing on p. 26 before quoting.
- Perennial philosophy: Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (New York: Harper & Brothers, 1945; London: Chatto & Windus, 1946). One line: Huxley argued that mystics across religions point to one underlying reality, "a divine Reality substantial to the world of things and lives and minds," and differ mainly in the language they use. VERIFIED. The term philosophia perennis predates him (Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia, 1540, then Leibniz). VERIFIED.
- Fairness extras: W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960), is the classic "common core" case. Robert K. C. Forman (ed.), The Problem of Pure Consciousness: Mysticism and Philosophy (Oxford University Press, 1990) is the main pushback to Katz. VERIFIED. Most scholars now sit in the middle.

6. THE AWAKENED ACT IN THE WORLD
Supported in several places. It is not universal, because renunciation is also a strong ideal in many of these traditions. Do not say "rather than withdrawing."
- Bodhisattva ideal: Shantideva, Bodhicaryavatara 10.55: "For as long as space exists and sentient beings endure, may I too remain to dispel the misery of the world." VERIFIED (Tricycle and Tsadra summaries; exact wording varies by translator, so name the translator in print). Also the Buddha's decision to teach after awakening, Ariyapariyesana Sutta (MN 26), where Brahma Sahampati asks him to teach. VERIFIED.
- Zen ox herding pictures: the tenth picture, "Entering the marketplace with bliss-bestowing hands" (also "helping hands"), in the 12th century version by Kuoan Shiyuan (Kakuan). The verse says he comes into the marketplace "barefooted, unadorned, smeared with mud, covered with dust, smiling." The tenth picture was added after the empty circle to show the realized person in the world. VERIFIED (terebess.hu, spiritualityandpractice). Earlier versions ended with the empty circle (PLAUSIBLE).
- Sufi: baqa as return to ordinary life (Junayd's sober school). VERIFIED. The Naqshbandi motto "khalwat dar anjuman" ("solitude in the crowd") is attributed to Baha al-Din Naqshband and the order rejected asceticism for active social life. VERIFIED (Wikipedia, eurasia.travel, New Age Islam). I could not confirm the Persian phrase "dast ba kar, dil ba yar."
- Christian: Teresa of Avila, Interior Castle, Seventh Mansions, ch. 4: the purpose of spiritual marriage is good works. In the Peers translation: "this is the aim of prayer: this is the purpose of the Spiritual Marriage, of which are born good works and good works alone." VERIFIED (IntraText Peers translation). Other translators phrase it differently, so name the translator. Eckhart, Sermon 86, ranks Martha, the active sister, above Mary. VERIFIED (Dionysius journal and other summaries).
- Hindu: Advaita's jivanmukta lives and acts without craving (VERIFIED), but Shankara himself favored renunciation, so it is not a clean case.

======== SAFE SENTENCES FOR PRINT ========
Terms
- Samadhi is a Sanskrit word for deep, steady absorption, in which the mind stops its usual chatter and the sense of a separate watcher loosens.
- Nirvana is the Buddhist word for the going out of the fires that keep suffering going, usually named as greed, hatred and confusion.
- Moksha is the Hindu word for liberation, release from the cycle of rebirth and from the ignorance that keeps a person bound to it.
- Satori is the Japanese Zen word for sudden insight, a moment of waking up to how things are.
- In Sufism, fana is the passing away of the ego's sense of being the centre of things, and baqa is the lasting that follows, a return to ordinary life with that recognition intact.
- In Eastern Orthodox Christianity, theosis is the lifelong process of coming to share in the life of God by grace, without ceasing to be a creature, and its best known Bible anchor is the phrase "partakers of the divine nature" in 2 Peter 1:4.
- Luke 17:21 says the kingdom of God is "within you" in the old King James and "among you" or "in your midst" in most newer Bibles, because the Greek can mean either.
- The Gospel of Thomas, a collection of sayings from the second century found in Egypt in 1945 and not part of the Bible, has Jesus say that the kingdom is inside you and outside you.
- In Daoism the Dao, or Tao, is the Way, the unnameable source and pattern of how things go, and wu wei, often translated as effortless action, means acting in tune with that pattern without strain or forcing.
- In Genesis 28:12 Jacob dreams of a ladder set up on the earth, with its top reaching to heaven and angels going up and down it.
- Stairway to Heaven is a Led Zeppelin song from their untitled fourth album, released in November 1971.
- Teachers in several traditions, most often in Vedanta, picture a person as a wave and the whole as the ocean, and "ocean of consciousness" is a modern English phrase for that old image.
Items 1 to 6
1. William Blake wrote on plate 14 of The Marriage of Heaven and Hell that if the doors of perception were cleansed, everything would appear to man as it is, infinite. Aldous Huxley took the title of his 1954 book The Doors of Perception from that line, and the band The Doors took its name from Huxley's book.
2. A saying that circulates in Zen circles, in several versions, says that before enlightenment you chop wood and carry water, and after enlightenment you chop wood and carry water. It echoes a verse by a Tang dynasty layman named Pang, who called drawing water and carrying firewood his supernatural power.
3. Several traditions separate a first opening from a settled realization and treat the first as the start of the work. Zen speaks of kensho followed by long practice, early Buddhism calls stream entry the first of four stages, and Sufis speak of fana followed by baqa.
4. Several contemplative traditions treat the ordinary sense of being a separate, sealed off "I" as the main thing to see through, though they disagree about what is left. Buddhism says no fixed self can be found, Advaita Vedanta says the true Self is the whole, and Sufi and Christian writers speak of the self being emptied so that God can fill it.
5. In 1978 the scholar Steven Katz argued that mystical experiences are shaped by the tradition of the person having them, so they should not be assumed to be the same everywhere. Aldous Huxley argued the opposite in The Perennial Philosophy in 1945, saying mystics of many religions point at one underlying reality and differ mainly in the words they use.
6. Several traditions describe the end of the path as a return to ordinary life and service. In Mahayana Buddhism the bodhisattva stays in the world to help others, the last of the Zen ox herding pictures shows the realized person walking back into the marketplace, and Teresa of Avila wrote that the purpose of spiritual marriage with God is good works.
