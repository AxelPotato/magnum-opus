1. BOTVINICK & COHEN 1998, rubber hand illusion
Citation: Botvinick M, Cohen J. Rubber hands 'feel' touch that eyes see. Nature 391(6669):756, Feb 1998. doi:10.1038/35784 [S]. A one-page letter.
Found: 10 subjects sat with the left arm hidden and a rubber hand in view. The experimenter stroked both hands with brushes for about 10 minutes, then a 9-statement questionnaire was given [S]. Only the three illusion statements (felt touch where the rubber hand was touched, touch seemed caused by the brush on the rubber hand, felt as if the rubber hand were mine) were endorsed, and only after synchronous stroking. Asynchronous stroking was the control. The summary I found says 8 of 10 spontaneously described the rubber hand as their own [S, secondary, check].
Speed: the 1998 paper used long stroking, so it does not give onset time. Later timing studies: Kalckert & Ehrsson 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 8:344 (moving version, n = 117), mean onset about 23 s, 90th percentile about 50 s [S]. A search summary says Ehrsson et al. 2004 reported a mean of 11.3 s (SD 7.0) [S, weak, one source]. Rough statement: most people within half a minute to a minute, some never.
Limits: experimental, but tiny sample, and the effect was measured mostly by self-report. Susceptibility varies: about 16 to 28 percent of participants did not respond in several studies [S]. Criticism: Lush and colleagues, Collabra: Psychology 2020 (n = 353), found that people who only read about the procedure and imagine being a participant expect the same pattern of answers, for both "illusion" and "control" conditions. They argue the questionnaire effect is partly suggestion [S]. Lush et al., Nature Communications 2020 (doi 10.1038/s41467-020-18591-6), found response to the illusion correlates with trait suggestibility in samples of 156, 404 and 353 [S]. A preregistered study (Royal Society Open Science 2021, n = 140 and n = 45) found that hypothesis awareness confounds the asynchronous control for indirect measures too [S]. A Bayesian meta-analysis (Tosi, Mentesana & Romano, QJEP 2023) found the two classic measures, felt ownership and proprioceptive drift, correlate only about r = .35 [S]. The illusion is not a fake effect, but "it feels like your hand" is partly a report that depends on suggestion.
Safe sentence: "In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen showed that when a rubber hand is stroked in time with your own hidden hand, most people soon feel the touch on the rubber hand and feel that it belongs to them."

2. LENGGENHAGER, TADI, METZINGER & BLANKE 2007, full-body illusion
Citation: Lenggenhager B, Tadi T, Metzinger T, Blanke O. Video ergo sum: Manipulating bodily self-consciousness. Science 317(5841):1096-1099. doi:10.1126/science.1143439 [S].
Found: volunteers wore a 3D headset showing live video of their own back from behind (about two metres away). Their real back was stroked in time or out of time with the strokes they saw on the virtual body. Under synchronous stroking they felt the virtual body was theirs, felt "located" toward it, and when blindfolded and walked back, they stopped closer to where the virtual body had stood [S].
Limits: small samples. A secondary source gives 14 participants for the main study. I could not retrieve per-experiment Ns [S, weak]. Mostly questionnaire and a modest position-drift measure. I found no preregistered direct replication. Follow-ups by the same Lausanne group extended it with other tasks (for example Ionta et al., Neuron 2011) [M]. The same suggestibility concern as item 1 plausibly applies, but I did not find it tested for this paradigm. It shows the felt location and ownership shift a little, not that the self "leaves the body."
Safe sentence: "In 2007 Bigna Lenggenhager and colleagues had people watch a video of their own back from behind while it was stroked in time with real strokes, and many reported that the body in front of them felt like theirs and that they felt located closer to it."

3. BLANKE & METZINGER 2009, and Metzinger's Being No One
Citation: Blanke O, Metzinger T. Full-body illusions and minimal phenomenal selfhood. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13(1):7-13. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2008.10.003 [S].
Found: a review, not an experiment. The abstract [S] asks what the minimally sufficient conditions are for a phenomenal self (the basic experience of being someone), reviews neurological and full-body-illusion evidence, and proposes this as an entry point for studying global self-consciousness. The three ingredients they name, self-identification with a body, self-location and first-person perspective, are from my memory of the paper [M].
Being No One, MIT Press 2003 (paperback 2004, ISBN 9780262633086) [S]. One fair sentence: Metzinger argues there are no self-things in the world, only a process that builds a model of the organism, and that model is experienced as a self because the system cannot see it as a model (he calls this "transparency") [S].
Limits: it is a theoretical framework and philosophical theory, informed by neuroscience, not an empirical finding. Metzinger is not denying that people exist. The claim is about what the felt self is. It is contested by philosophers.
Safe sentence: "The philosopher Thomas Metzinger argues that the felt self is a model the brain builds of the body and then cannot see as a model, and with Olaf Blanke he proposes that its basic ingredients, a body that feels like mine, a felt location and a point of view, can be pulled apart in experiments."

Next, as returned: "Common psychological constructs that contain a self-transcendent aspect include mindfulness, flow, peak experiences, mystical-type experiences, and certain positive emotions (e.g., love, awe)." The abstract goes on to propose mechanisms drawn from social psychology, clinical psychology and affective neuroscience [S]. Check the exact wording against the publisher page before you put it in quotation marks.

5. BREWER et al. 2011, default mode network
Citation: Brewer JA, Worhunsky PD, Gray JR, Tang YY, Weber J, Kober H. Meditation experience is associated with differences in default mode network activity and connectivity. PNAS 108(50):20254-20259, published 23 Nov 2011. doi:10.1073/pnas.1112029108 [authors, title, date: S; DOI and pages: M].
Found: fMRI of 12 experienced meditators and 13 matched meditation-naive controls doing three meditations (concentration, loving-kindness, choiceless awareness) [S]. Main default mode nodes (medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortex) were relatively deactivated in the experienced meditators across all three. They also showed stronger coupling between posterior cingulate, dorsal anterior cingulate and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, at baseline and during meditation [S]. Experienced meditators reported less mind-wandering during meditation [S, one summary]. I did not retrieve the hours of practice.
Limits: cross-sectional and correlational (practice was not assigned), 25 people in all, self-selected groups. It shows brain differences associated with experience, not that meditation caused them. The default mode network is not "the self". Calling it that is reverse inference. The paper measures no behavior toward others. A later meta-analysis (Fox et al. 2016, Neurosci Biobehav Rev 65:208-228; 78 studies) found different meditation styles activate different regions and convergence was "the exception rather than the rule" [S].
Safe sentence: "In a small 2011 Yale study, 12 experienced meditators showed less activity than 13 beginners in brain regions linked to mind-wandering and self-related thinking while they meditated, which fits their reports of less mind-wandering but cannot show that meditation caused the difference."

6. CONDON, DESBORDES, MILLER & DeSTENO 2013, seat study
Citation: Meditation increases compassionate responses to suffering. Psychological Science 24(10):2125-2127. doi:10.1177/0956797613485603 [title, journal, year, volume, issue and DOI: S; pages: M].
Found: 39 participants (29 female, mean age 25.2) [S]. Randomly assigned to an 8-week meditation course (mindfulness or compassion version) or a wait-list group. The test was a staged waiting room: three chairs, two confederates ignoring a third confederate who arrived on crutches and in a walking boot, looking in pain. The measure was whether the real participant gave up the seat. Reported result: meditators gave up their seat more than wait-list controls, chi-square(1, N = 39) = 5.13, p = .02, phi = .36, and it did not differ between the two course types [S].
Percentages: press and secondary summaries say 50 percent versus 16 percent (some say 15) [S]. My arithmetic: 10 of 20 versus 3 of 19 reproduces the reported chi-square 5.13 and phi .36 exactly, so those counts are very likely right, but they are inferred, not read from the paper. Group sizes of 20 and 19 are from a summary [S].
Limits: very small, a single yes/no behavior, a wait-list control (so no control for attention, group contact, teacher or expectation), and the effect did not differ between mindfulness and compassion training, which hints at a general effect of taking a class. Replications and follow-ups: same lab, Lim, Condon & DeSteno 2015 PLoS ONE 10(2):e0118221, found that a 3-week app-based mindfulness course beat an active cognitive-skills control on the same seat test [S, details unchecked]. Weng et al. 2013 Psychological Science 24(7):1171-1180 (n = 41, 2 weeks of compassion training versus reappraisal training) found more giving to a victim in a redistribution game [S]. Berry et al. 2018 J Exp Psychol Gen 147(1):93-112 reported medium effects of brief mindfulness on helping an ostracized stranger [S]. See extra 1 for the meta-analytic counterweight.
Safe sentence: "In a small 2013 experiment, about half of the people who had taken an eight-week meditation course gave up their seat to a woman on crutches, against about one in six on the wait-list."

7. PIFF, DIETZE, FEINBERG, STANCATO & KELTNER 2015, awe
Citation: Awe, the small self, and prosocial behavior. J Pers Soc Psychol 108(6):883-899. doi:10.1037/pspi0000018 [S].
Found: five studies, N = 2,078 in total [S]. (1) Dispositional awe predicted more generosity in an economic game, above and beyond other prosocial emotions such as compassion. (2 to 4) Induced awe, compared with various control states, increased ethical decision-making, generosity and prosocial values. (5) Standing in a grove of tall eucalyptus trees for about a minute led to more helping (picking up dropped pens) and less felt entitlement than looking at a tall building. Mediation analyses suggested that feeling like a "small self" explained part of the effect [S].
Limits: the mediation is statistical, with the small-self measure collected after the induction. Awe is hard to separate from positive mood. Replication concerns: Graziosi, Chirico, Clifton, Rohde, Chhabra & Yaden (2026), "A series of replication attempts of experimental awe-induction effects", PLoS ONE 21(9):e0356728. Per the search summary, conceptual replications (video, writing, VR inductions) did not generally reproduce earlier awe effects (agency detection, prosocial behavior, well-being), and three online direct replications at 2.5 times the original sample sizes also did not [S, from one summary of an article dated Sept 2026, which I could not open]. A student replication (Nguyen & Quinn, DePaul) reportedly did not replicate Piff 2015 [S, weak]. Meanwhile a 2024 meta-analysis (extra 3) finds a moderate positive association overall. So the causal "awe makes you more generous" claim is the weakly supported part.
Safe sentence: "In 2015 Paul Piff and colleagues reported that people who had just felt awe, for example among very tall trees, felt smaller and acted more generously, though later attempts to reproduce effects of this kind have not always succeeded."

8. CIALDINI et al. 1997, oneness; ARON, ARON & SMOLLAN 1992
Citation: Cialdini RB, Brown SL, Lewis BP, Luce C, Neuberg SL. Reinterpreting the empathy-altruism relationship: When one into one equals oneness. J Pers Soc Psychol 73(3):481-494 [S]. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.73.3.481 [M, pattern fits].
Found: in 3 studies, the link between empathic concern and willingness to help disappeared once perceived oneness (self-other overlap) was taken into account. Path analyses indicated empathic concern raised helping only through its tie to perceived oneness. The authors suggest empathic concern works mainly as a signal of oneness [S]. In plain terms: people help more the more they feel "one" with the person, and plain sympathy did not add anything on top.
Limits: correlational and path-analytic, mostly student samples. I could not verify per-study Ns or whether helping was hypothetical or real in each study. The interpretation is disputed. In the same issue, Batson et al. (1997, JPSP 73:495-509, "Is empathy-induced helping due to self-other merging?") ran two experiments and found the empathy-helping link could not be accounted for by any merging measure [S]. Maner et al. 2002 (JPSP, "Still no evidence for altruism") found oneness and negative affect, not empathic concern, mediated helping [S]. So the debate is open.
Aron, Aron & Smollan 1992 [S]: Inclusion of Other in the Self scale and the structure of interpersonal closeness. J Pers Soc Psychol 63(4):596-612. (DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.596 [M].) A one-item scale: seven pairs of circles overlapping more and more, and you pick the pair that best shows you and another person. In two studies it showed good reliability and predicted whether romantic relationships were still intact 3 months later [S].
Safe sentence: "In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how much people said they would help someone in need tracked how 'one' they felt with that person, and once that was counted, plain sympathy added nothing, a reading other researchers dispute."

9. KOLTKO-RIVERA 2006 AND MASLOW
Citation: Koltko-Rivera ME. Rediscovering the later version of Maslow's hierarchy of needs: Self-transcendence and opportunities for theory, research, and unification. Review of General Psychology 10(4):302-317 [S]. DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.10.4.302 [M].
What it argues [S, as quoted by search summaries of the abstract]: "The conventional description of Abraham Maslow's (1943, 1954) hierarchy of needs is inaccurate as a description of Maslow's later thought." Maslow amended his model, placing self-transcendence as a motivational step beyond self-actualization. A secondary source says Maslow was convinced "probably in October 1966" and first drew the distinction publicly in a lecture, "The Farther Reaches of Human Nature", San Francisco, 14 Sept 1967 [S, secondary citing Koltko-Rivera, check]. Another secondary summary says he never formally revised the diagram [S, weak].
Maslow primary texts I could confirm by quote, via search summaries [S], not in the books themselves:
- Maslow 1968 preface to the 2nd edition of Toward a Psychology of Being: "I consider humanistic, Third Force psychology, to be transitional, a preparation for a still 'higher' Fourth psychology, transpersonal, transhuman, centered in the cosmos rather than in human needs and interests, going beyond humanness, identity, self-actualization and the like." (Check "interest" versus "interests" and the page.)
- Maslow 1969, Various meanings of transcendence, Journal of Transpersonal Psychology 1(1):56-66 (Spring 1969; existence and start page confirmed [S]). Definition quoted by the summary: "Transcendence refers to the very highest and most inclusive or holistic levels of human consciousness, behaving and relating, as ends rather than means, to oneself, to significant others, to human beings in general, to other species, to nature, and to the cosmos." (It was reprinted in The Farther Reaches of Human Nature, Viking 1971, published after his death in 1970 [M]; I have not confirmed the reprint page.)
- Maslow 1969, Theory Z, Journal of Transpersonal Psychology 1(2):31-47 [M]. Summaries say it separates "transcenders" from healthy non-transcending self-actualizers [S]. This matters for wording: in Theory Z, transcendence is described as a way some self-actualizers live, not only as a new rung above.
The pyramid: Bridgman, Cummings & Ballard 2019, Academy of Management Learning & Education 18(1):81-98, "Who built Maslow's pyramid?" [S]. They searched the Maslow archives, found no sign that Maslow framed his ideas as a pyramid, and traced the shape to management writing: a stepped form in Keith Davis 1957 and a pyramid in Charles McDermid 1960 [S, summary]. DOI 10.5465/amle.2017.0351 [M]. So: yes, the familiar pyramid stops at self-actualization. It follows the five-need version from 1943, and it is not Maslow's drawing.
Limits: "above" is Koltko-Rivera's reading of Maslow's late prose. Maslow died June 1970 [M] with no finished revised diagram.
Safe sentence: "Late in his life Maslow wrote that there is something beyond self-actualization, which he called transcendence, and the familiar five-step pyramid, which he never drew himself, leaves it out."

10. EXTRAS (three, each a meta-analysis)
Extra 1 (for helping): Kreplin U, Farias M, Brazil IA. The limited prosocial effects of meditation: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Scientific Reports 8:2403 (2018). doi:10.1038/s41598-018-20299-z [S]. Randomized trials of healthy adults. Overall prosociality effect was moderate (mean r = .26 [S, secondary]). Compassion and empathy improved (r about .37 and .44 [S, one secondary source, check]). It found no significant effect on aggression, prejudice or social connectedness. The compassion gains appeared mainly where the control group was passive (wait-list) and where the meditation teacher was a co-author of the paper [S]. Treat the effect as weaker than headlines. Luberto et al. 2018 (Mindfulness 9:708-724; 26 studies, 1,714 people) found small to moderate effects on empathy, compassion and prosocial behavior against blank or wait-list controls [S].
Safe sentence: "A 2018 meta-analysis of randomized trials found that meditation training nudges compassion up modestly, but the effect mostly vanished with active control groups and when the teacher was not an author, so it is smaller than the early headlines."
Extra 2 (for self-focus and distress): Mor N, Winquist J. Self-focused attention and negative affect: A meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin 128(4):638-662 (2002). doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.638 [S]. 226 effect sizes. Self-focus overall went with more negative affect, more strongly in clinical samples and in women, and most strongly for rumination. Private self-focus went with depression and generalized anxiety, public self-focus with social anxiety. Self-focus on positive self-aspects, or after a positive event, went with lower negative affect [S]. A mean d of about .51 was cited by one secondary source [S, weak]. Correlational, so it does not show that less self-focus reduces distress, and "self-focus" is not always bad.
Safe sentence: "A large 2002 meta-analysis found that being turned inward on the self goes with more anxiety and low mood, especially when the inward looking is rumination."
Extra 3 (for awe): Li R, Hou Z, Zhang C, Xu Q, Nie A. A meta-analysis examining the relationship between awe and prosocial behavior. Current Psychology 43:24702-24711 (2024). doi:10.1007/s12144-024-06039-9 [S]. 38 articles, 57 independent effect sizes, N = 20,997. Dispositional and state awe both correlated moderately and positively with prosocial behavior, moderated by age and culture [S]. I could not get the pooled r value. Many included studies are correlational, and it predates the 2026 replication paper in item 7. A second three-level meta-analysis (Basic and Applied Social Psychology 2025, doi 10.1080/01973533.2024.2433721) exists [S, not read].
Safe sentence: "A 2024 meta-analysis of 38 papers found that people who feel more awe tend to behave more prosocially."
I did not find a strong preregistered or meta-analytic source specifically for "the felt boundary of me is a flexible construction". The rubber hand and full-body work above is the mainstream evidence, with the Lush suggestibility critique as the live caveat.

OVERCLAIM CHECK ON YOUR FOUR DRAFT CLAIMS
1. "The sense of where you end and the world begins is something the brain builds, and it can be moved in under a minute with a trick."
Mostly supportable, with three softenings. (a) What moves is the felt ownership of a hand (rubber hand), or the felt location of the body (full-body illusion), not the broad psychological boundary of "me". (b) "Under a minute" fits onset times: mean about 23 s and 90th percentile about 50 s in a 117-person moving-hand study [S], about 11 s in an older study [S, weak]. It holds for most people, not all (roughly 16 to 28 percent show no illusion [S]). (c) A good part of the reported effect is open to suggestion (Lush 2020). Suggested: "The felt edge of your body, the sense that this hand is mine, is something the brain works out from the senses, and for most people a rubber hand can take it over within about half a minute."
2. "People who have trained attention or compassion for a few weeks help more."
Overclaims. Small trials (2, 3 and 8 weeks, n = 39 to 41, and Lim's 3-week study with an active control) show more helping in staged tests. But the 2018 meta-analysis found the compassion effect depended on passive controls and on a teacher who was also an author, and it found no effect on aggression, prejudice or connectedness. Also "a few weeks" understates Condon (8 weeks), and "attention" training has no separate evidence of its own here. Suggested: "In some small experiments, a few weeks of meditation training went with more helping in a staged test, though later reviews find the effect modest and weaker with better controls."
3. "Awe makes people feel smaller and more generous."
Overclaims as a causal statement. Piff 2015 reported both effects in five studies (N = 2,078), but a 2026 replication series reports that awe-induction effects were generally not reproduced, including direct replications with 2.5 times the sample [S], and the small-self mediation is described as inconsistent [S]. The 2024 meta-analysis supports a moderate association with prosocial behavior. Suggested: "In some studies, people who had just felt awe reported feeling smaller and acted more generously, though recent replication attempts have not reproduced the experimental effects."
4. "Maslow put self-transcendence above self-actualization; the popular pyramid dropped it."
Close, but "dropped" implies someone removed a rung. The pyramid is not Maslow's (he never drew one, per Bridgman et al. 2019). It stems from the 1943 five-need version, which stops at self-actualization. Maslow's late writing (1967 to 1970, JTP 1969, Farther Reaches 1971) puts transcendence beyond ordinary self-actualization, but he left no finished revised diagram and in Theory Z he treated "transcenders" as a subset of self-actualizers. "Above" is Koltko-Rivera's reading. Suggested: "In his last years Maslow wrote about a kind of living that goes beyond self-actualization, which he called transcendence; the familiar pyramid, which he never drew, stops short of it."
