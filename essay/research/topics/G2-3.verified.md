## 1. The picture in brief

The essay says an illusion is a wrong reading of something real, and the wall between programs is real inside the system. Every tradition below has a technical version of that sentence. They differ on what the real thing is and what exactly is misread.

Shankara says the real thing is the substrate (Brahman) and the error is adhyasa, superimposition: the traits of the not-self laid over the Self and the reverse. He calls the habit natural, beginningless and a cause of suffering (anartha-hetu) that knowledge of the Self removes, and the thing superimposed neither real nor unreal. [Fact-check: "cause of the round of births" went beyond the passage as I recall it; see log C12.] Later Advaita adds three orders of reality (dream objects, the shared practical world, Brahman), which give "real inside the system" its source. The ranking test is sublation: does a later knowing cancel the earlier?

Yogacara Buddhism puts the real in the dependent flow of events and the error in an imagined split into grasper and grasped. The perfected nature is the absence of the second in the first. Madhyamaka says nothing has a nature of its own, that the conventional is the only place anything is found (Garfield's gloss: ultimate truth is that the conventional is conventional; "all there is" is too loose), that its word also means "concealing", and that conventional truth comes in a sound kind and a false kind. Ibn Arabi says the cosmos is "He and not He", an imagination that is real as self-disclosure. Plato, Plotinus and Bradley give degrees of reality and the line that an image is really an image.

The closest fits to the essay are Yogacara's three natures (structure), Shankara's superimposition (claim) and Candrakirti's two kinds of conventional (for "real inside the system"). Pali, Yogacara and Gelug texts also split the error into a theoretical part that argument removes and an innate part that only practice wears down, which is the rubber hand point. The fits fail in four places: maya is not the Yogacara imagined nature, Madhyamaka and Theravada have no One, strict Advaita says no one was ever bound, and Ramanuja asks the hardest question, whose ignorance it is.

## 2. Findings

### A. Advaita Vedanta

1. Adhyasa defined (the Adhyasa Bhashya)
Claim: Shankara opens his Brahma Sutra commentary by saying the object ("you", the not-self) and the subject ("I") are as opposed as darkness and light, so neither can really be the other. Yet people lay the traits of each on the other. That is adhyasa. His working definition is the appearing, in the manner of memory, of something seen earlier in another thing. He lists rival definitions and says all schools agree that something appears with another thing's traits. He calls it natural, beginningless and endless, a false cognition that drives agency and enjoyment, known to everyone. The wise call it avidya, and fixing the real nature of the substrate is vidya. All Vedanta texts exist to remove this cause of suffering and give knowledge of the oneness of the Self. His examples: shell taken for silver, the double moon, space taken to be blue.
Who: Shankara (8th century CE), Brahma Sutra Bhashya, introduction to 1.1.1. Translations I know: Gambhirananda (Advaita Ashrama, 1965), Thibaut (SBE 34, 1890, public domain; his second volume SBE 38, 1896, carries the later adhyayas, so 4.1.15 would be there [U]), Apte (1960) [U]. The brief names a "Sharma" translation; I cannot place it. Search strings, from memory: smrtirupah paratra purvadrstavabhasah; naisargiko 'yam lokavyavaharah. [Fact-check, memory only: the second string closes the sentence "aham idam mama idam iti naisargiko 'yam lokavyavaharah" ("I am this, this is mine: this everyday dealing is natural"). Both strings agree with my memory of the text. Not searched, tier unchanged.]
Traditions cited: none; other Indian schools are his rivals on the definition.
Tier: [M]. The definition string and the three claims are secure; the space example and the list of rival definitions are [U].
Use: Closest classical wording for "a wrong reading". One difference: Advaita holds the silver is neither real nor unreal, while the essay's wall is a real fact.

2. Why it feels real: naturalness, the substrate and the sublation test
Claim: The Advaita answer to "why does it feel so solid" has three parts. First, the superimposition is natural and beginningless, so nobody has an earlier moment of clear sight to compare with. Second, an error needs a substrate that is partly known: you see the "this" and miss the "what", so the shell's brightness really is there. Third, appearances are ranked by whether a later knowing cancels them (badha, sublation). A lamp cancels the rope-snake. No ordinary knowing cancels the world, only knowledge of Brahman. An older criterion, in the Gita (2.16) and Gaudapada (Karika 4.31), is constancy: what is absent at the start and end is absent in the middle.
Who: Adhyasa Bhashya; Gita 2.16; Mandukya Karika 2.6 and 4.31 [locators M, check]. Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.2.28 to 32 answers the Buddhist idealists with the sublation point: waking differs from dream because waking objects are not cancelled while one is awake.
Tier: [M]; sutra numbers [U].
Use: The Theosophical Glossary definition (what changes is maya) follows the constancy criterion, not Shankara's sublation test. One clause, if Alex quotes the Glossary.

3. Three orders of reality (pratibhasika, vyavaharika, paramarthika)
Claim: Pratibhasika: dream, rope-snake, mirage, cancelled within ordinary waking life. Vyavaharika: the shared, stable world of daily transactions, cancelled only by knowledge of Brahman. Paramarthika: Brahman, never cancelled. The wall between programs is vyavaharika: not a hallucination, not the processor's view.
Who: Shankara uses the pair vyavaharika and paramarthika (Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.1.14 and elsewhere [U]; my memory puts there his line that all dealings are valid until the knowledge of the Self, as dream dealings are until waking, but that is not checked [U]). The clean three-tier scheme with the name pratibhasika is post-Shankara: the Vedanta Paribhasa of Dharmaraja Adhvarindra (17th century [M]) states it plainly, and the scheme may be older than that work, so do not call it that book's invention [U]. Handbooks: Chandradhar Sharma, A Critical Survey of Indian Philosophy (1960); Eliot Deutsch, Advaita Vedanta (1969) [M]. Schuon's degrees of reality are the modern parallel.
Tier: [M].
Use: The source for "real inside the system": vyavaharika, the shared working level. Scholars warn against reading the three tiers back into Shankara himself.

4. Vivarta against parinama
Claim: Parinama is a real change of the cause into the effect (milk into curd). Vivarta is an appearance of the effect with no change in the cause (rope appearing as snake). Advaita says Brahman is the material cause of the world only in the second sense. Later Advaita adds that maya, as a power, does transform really, while Brahman as substrate does not. The doctrine rests on Brahma Sutra 2.1.14 (the effect is non-different from the cause) and Chandogya 6.1.4 (the modification is a name arising from speech; only clay is real).
Who: Brahma Sutra and Bhashya 2.1.14; Chandogya 6.1.4 to 6; a later verse in the Vedantasara of Sadananda (locator unknown [M]); Paul Hacker, Vivarta: Studien zur Geschichte der Illusionslehre des Advaita-Vedanta (1953) [M title].
Tier: [M]. Hacker's conclusion, that the strict contrast belongs to Shankara's successors, is [U].
Use: Closest classical match to "one machine, many windows", with a care. A window on a real screen is a real change in the hardware (parinama). The essay's point is vivarta-like: the machine has never heard of the distance, so the distance is a trait laid on the machine. Say that and the picture holds.

5. Anirvacaniya and the map of error theories
Claim: Advaita says the snake is neither real (it is cancelled) nor unreal (it was seen and caused fear), so it is indescribable as either, anirvacaniya. Shankara has a phrase of this kind for name and form ("not definable as the same as or other than the real"; my memory places it in Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.1.14, [U]); the formal doctrine was built by later Advaitins (Padmapada, Prakasatman, Sriharsha and Chitsukha are the names usually given [U]), and the five definitions of mithya are set out in Madhusudana Sarasvati's Advaitasiddhi (c. 1600), drawing on earlier authors. Which author gave which of the five, I cannot confirm [U]. Sriharsha (Khandanakhandakhadya) defends the indescribability of the world, but he is not, as I recall, one of the sources of the five definitions; the colleague's list is downgraded. Indian philosophers put the same test case, shell-silver, to every school (khyativada):
Advaita: anirvacaniyakhyati, the silver is made by avidya and is neither real nor unreal.
Nyaya: anyathakhyati, a real silver elsewhere is wrongly attached to the shell. Kumarila's variant is usually named viparitakhyati and often grouped with it [M]. The nearest to "a wrong reading of something real" taken literally.
Prabhakara Mimamsa: akhyati, two true cognitions (this, and a memory of silver) are not told apart; no error as such.
Ramanuja: satkhyati, the shell really holds a little silver, since all things share all elements.
Yogacara: atmakhyati, the mind's own form is projected outward.
Madhyamaka: asatkhyati, a non-existent appears.
Tier: [M] for the list; [U] for the exact Shankara locator.
Use: A one-paragraph device. "Illusion as wrong reading" is one answer among six, and the Nyaya one (a real thing misplaced) suits the wall better than Advaita's indescribable silver.

6. Avarana and vikshepa
Claim: Ignorance has two powers. Avarana veils the substrate (the rope is not seen as rope). Vikshepa projects the appearance (the snake).
Who: Post-Shankara Advaita: Vivekachudamani (attributed to Shankara, widely judged later, roughly verses 108 to 143 [U]), Drig-Drishya-Viveka, Panchadasi, Vedantasara.
Tier: [M] for the pair; [U] for locators and attribution.
Use: Not seeing the machine (avarana), then the screen picture on top (vikshepa). Credit the vocabulary to later Advaita.

7. The analogy set, what each is for, and the limit of analogy
Claim: Each analogy carries one point.
Rope-snake and shell-silver: a real substrate misread, and the lamp that cancels it.
Dream: a world made by the experiencer, ranked by sublation.
Clay, gold, iron: effect not different from cause (Chandogya 6.1.4 to 6).
Pot-space: space is one, and the pot only appears to cut it. Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 3.3 to 8: individual selves are like pot-spaces. When one pot is filled with dust or smoke the others are not, which answers why pain stays private (as I recall the verses: 3.3 the pot-space analogy, 3.4 the pots dissolve into space, 3.5 the dust and smoke verse, 3.6 forms and functions differ but space does not, 3.7 the jiva is not a part or a modification of the Self, 3.8 the sky looks dirty to children; all [U], my memory only).
Reflection: the sun in water. Brahma Sutra 2.3.50 (abhasa eva ca, "only an appearance", search string). The Vivarana school reads the individual as a reflection (bimba-pratibimba), the Bhamati school as a limitation (avaccheda).
Wave and ocean: Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.1.13 (sea, foam, wave); Ramakrishna and Vivekananda use it for the many in one.
Limit: the tradition teaches by superimposition and then retracts it (adhyaropa and apavada; Gita Bhashya 13.13, Vedantasara). An analogy holds for one point and is dropped after it has done its work.
Tier: [M]; verse numbers [M] for Karika 3.3 to 8, [U] elsewhere.
Use: Pot-space is the nearest classical match to private memory in a shared machine. Advaita says the jiva is not a part of Brahman, which has no parts, and reads Gita 15.7 ("an eternal portion of me") as a part "as it were". The "fingers of one hand" language fits Ramanuja better than Shankara.

8. Ramanuja's seven untenabilities
Claim: In the Sri Bhashya on Brahma Sutra 1.1.1, Ramanuja argues that avidya cannot be made coherent. The seven: it has no possible locus (not the individual, whose existence depends on it; not Brahman, who is knowledge); it cannot veil Brahman's nature; its own nature (neither real nor unreal) is incoherent; "indescribable" is an empty category, since all cognition is of something existing or not; no valid means of knowledge establishes it; the knowledge that removes it would itself be unreal; and its cessation is either real, which makes a second reality, or unreal. His own view: all cognition is veridical (satkhyati), and world and selves are real, as the body of God. Vedanta Desika (Satadushani, the same Sri Vaishnava school) presses the same attack at length. Vyasatirtha (Nyayamrta) is a Dvaita (Madhva) author who makes a related attack on the Advaita doctrine of illusion, and Madhusudana's Advaitasiddhi (c. 1600) is the Advaita reply to him, not a direct reply to Ramanuja; earlier Advaitins (Chitsukha is the name I recall) answer the Ramanuja-style objections to avidya [U]. [Fact-check: colleague wrote that the Advaitasiddhi answers Ramanuja's attack; corrected, log C3.]
Who: Ramanuja (traditionally 1017 to 1137, which scholars shorten), Sri Bhashya on 1.1.1; Thibaut, SBE 48 (1904, public domain); Carman (1974); Lipner (1986) [M].
Tier: [M] for the seven and their gist; the order and Sanskrit names are [U].
Use: The first objection, "whose ignorance?", is the essay's open question: if there is only the whole, who is fooled? Same shape as the decombination problem (G2-2). Advaita replies that avidya is beginningless and the question arises inside it. One honest sentence will do.

9. Gaudapada's caveat on the third clause
Claim: Gaudapada says that from the highest view there is no origination and no dissolution, no one bound, no seeker, no one released (Karika 2.32, "na nirodho na cotpattih", search string). Bondage and liberation are within maya.
Who: Mandukya Karika, ajati teaching, 2.32 and 3.48 [M, numbers U].
Tier: [M].
Use: The third clause, "the separation ends", holds for the individual's experience (jivanmukti); strict Advaita adds that at the highest level it never began. Say "ends, in experience" or flag the other reading.

### B. Buddhist theories

10. Yogacara: the three natures (trisvabhava)
Claim: Imagined (parikalpita), dependent (paratantra), perfected (parinishpanna). Vasubandhu's Trisvabhavanirdesa says: what appears is the dependent, since it arises from conditions; how it appears is the imagined, since it is only imagined; and the perfected is the constant non-existence of that "how" in the "what". Madhyantavibhaga 1.1: the imagination of what is unreal exists; duality is not found in it; emptiness is found in it, and it in emptiness. Example (Trisvabhavanirdesa, the verses on the magician; verse numbers [U]): a magician charms a piece of wood so it looks like an elephant. The appearance (the wood as it looks under the spell) is the dependent, the elephant is the imagined, the absence of any elephant in that appearance is the perfected. The wood itself is not one of the three natures, and the colleague's phrase "the wood-appearance" blurs that [M, wording U]. The seventh consciousness, manas, constantly takes the base consciousness for a self and carries four afflictions (self-view, self-delusion, self-conceit, self-love; Trimsika 5 to 7).
Who: Vasubandhu, Trisvabhavanirdesa (4th or 5th century) and Trimsika; Maitreya or Asanga, Madhyantavibhaga; Samdhinirmocana Sutra ch. 6 and 7. Translations: Anacker, Seven Works of Vasubandhu (1984); Kochumuttom (1982); Hopkins (1999, via Tsongkhapa's line) [M].
Tier: [M] for the structure and the Madhyantavibhaga line; [U] for Trisvabhava verse numbers.
Use: Best structural match to "wrong reading of something real": a real flow, a misreading laid on it, and freedom as the misreading's absence. It also fits letting go: the perfected nature is not added, the imagined is subtracted.

11. Madhyamaka: two truths and samvrti as concealing
Claim: Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.8 to 10: the Buddha's teaching rests on two truths, the worldly conventional and the ultimate. Those who do not see the difference do not see the Buddha's teaching. Without the conventional the ultimate cannot be taught, and without the ultimate nirvana is not reached. 24.18 equates dependent arising, emptiness and designation. Candrakirti, commenting on 24.8, derives samvrti in three ways: as covering (ignorance covers the real nature of things), as mutual dependence, and as worldly usage. In the Madhyamakavatara (ch. 6, around verses 23 to 28 [U]) he distinguishes two kinds of conventional: the sound kind (what a healthy faculty perceives and the world can act on) and the false kind (the hairs a cataract shows, mirage, double moon).
Who: Garfield, Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way (1995); Siderits and Katsura (2013); Huntington (1989) for Candrakirti [M].
Tier: [M] for 24.8 to 10, the three senses and the two kinds; [U] for verse numbers beyond 24.
Use: "Real inside the system" is Candrakirti's sound conventional: not a mirage, still empty of inherent nature. The caveat is large: Madhyamaka has no substrate. What is misread is a dependent thing taken to have its own nature. Never use it to say Madhyamaka teaches oneness.

12. Innate against conceptual: the rubber hand in three Buddhist schools and in Advaita
Claim: Four traditions separate a belief that argument can remove from a habit that only practice wears down.
Pali: the stream-enterer drops identity view, but "I am" conceit remains until arahatship. In the Khemaka Sutta (SN 22.89) Khemaka says he regards nothing among the five aggregates as self or as belonging to self, yet the conceit "I am" has not been uprooted in him. The elders' answer is a simile: a cloth washed with salt earth, lye or cow dung is clean but keeps a residual smell until it is put in a sweet-scented casket. The sutta itself does not name his attainment; the commentary calls him a non-returner [M; Bodhi's translation as I recall it, wording not checked].
Yogacara: the Cheng Weishi Lun (compiled and translated by Xuanzang, 659, on Vasubandhu's Trimsika) divides self-clinging into co-born (sahaja) and discriminated (parikalpita); the second goes at the path of seeing, the first only by long cultivation.
Gelug: Tsongkhapa (Lamrim Chenmo, 1402) distinguishes innate (lhan skyes, sahaja) from acquired (kun btags, parikalpita) self-grasping. The innate is shared with animals and does not leave when the argument is understood. After insight the appearance of inherent existence continues, but assent is withdrawn, "like an illusion".
Advaita: badhitanuvritti, the continuing of a cancelled appearance, as the double moon does after one knows. The jivanmukta continues like a potter's wheel after the push (Samkhya Karika 67; the image is used by Advaitins too, and my memory puts it in Shankara at Brahma Sutra Bhashya 4.1.15 [U]).
Tier: [M] for Khemaka, Trimsika, the Gelug split and Samkhya Karika 67; [U] for the Cheng Weishi details.
Use: The clean answer to the rubber hand: the feeling of a wall does not respond to being told it is a model. The Western parallel is the Muller-Lyer illusion, where the lines stay unequal after you measure them. State one difference: the traditions claim practice can wear the habit down, which the cognitive science does not show.

13. Buddhist wave and ocean (the Awakening of Faith, the Lankavatara)
Claim: The Awakening of Faith in the Mahayana (Dasheng qixin lun, a Chinese text of the 6th century, traditionally credited to Asvaghosha) says wind moves ocean water into waves that are not apart from the water; when the wind stops the waves stop and the water stays. Ignorance is the wind, the mind's nature the water. The Lankavatara has the base consciousness as an ocean stirred by the wind of objects.
Who: Awakening of Faith, tr. Hakeda (1967); Lankavatara Sutra, tr. Suzuki (1932) [M].
Tier: [M].
Use: A Chinese synthesis (Tathagatagarbha with Yogacara). Theravada and Madhyamaka do not accept a One Mind; say so if it is quoted.

### C. Islamic

14. Ibn Arabi: He and not He, imagination, barzakh
Claim: Creation is "He/not He" (huwa la huwa). Saying God is only incomparable (tanzih) restricts Him; saying He is only similar (tashbih) limits Him; whoever holds both is right (Fusus, chapter on Noah [M]). The cosmos is imagination (khayal): real as a self-disclosure (tajalli) of God, and an error only when taken as a separate thing. The barzakh (Quran 55:19 to 20: two seas, between them a barrier) is what is neither of two sides and joins them, for Ibn Arabi the pattern of the imagination, the cosmos and the human being. Corbin called the realm of images "imaginal", so that it would not be heard as unreal.
Who: Ibn Arabi (d. 1240), Fusus al-hikam, Futuhat al-Makkiyya. Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge (1989), Imaginal Worlds (1994); Corbin, Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn Arabi (French 1958, English 1969); Izutsu, Sufism and Taoism (1983) [M].
Traditions cited: Chittick and Schuon set "He/not He" beside maya as neither real nor unreal; Izutsu sets Ibn Arabi beside Laozi and Zhuangzi.
Tier: [M]; the Fusus and Chittick chapter titles are [U].
Use: The best Islamic witness for "real, but not what it looks like", and a safe one, since it keeps both statements true. "Unity of being" (wahdat al-wujud) was a later label that critics attacked. Ahmad Sirhindi (1564 to 1624) offered "unity of witnessing" (wahdat al-shuhud): the oneness is in the seer's experience, not in being [M]. The formula goes back, as I recall, to Ala al-Dawla Simnani (d. 1336), whom Sirhindi developed [U]. That reading is closer to "the separation ends in experience".

### D. Western

15. Plato and Plotinus: degrees of reality, the image that really is an image
Claim: Republic 509d to 511e (divided line): the visible splits into images and things, the intelligible into reasoning and direct insight, in the same proportion. Cave: 514a to 517a for the image, with its reading running on to about 521b [M; the colleague's 520e is a near miss]. Timaeus 27d to 29c: what always is, and what only becomes. Sophist 240a to b: a likeness is not the real thing it likens, but it really is a likeness [M]. So an image is not nothing. Plotinus: Intellect, Soul and the sensible world descend as images of images, matter last and barely real (Enneads I.8, III.6 [M]), yet the cosmos stays a good image, against the Gnostics who called it a prison (II.9 [M]).
Who: Plato (Jowett, Grube); Plotinus (MacKenna, Armstrong).
Tier: [M]; Plotinus sub-chapters [U].
Use: The Sophist line is the nearest Western wording of "real, in its own order". Plotinus kept the world good: one sentence.

16. Bradley, Kant, Schopenhauer: the Western path of the word
Claim: F. H. Bradley, Appearance and Reality (1893, 2nd ed. 1897): appearance is not nothing, because it appears; it belongs to reality, which is one; things come in degrees of truth and reality (a chapter by that name [M]). Each finite centre of experience is closed to the others. A passage T. S. Eliot cites in his note to The Waste Land (line 412, Bradley p. 346) says each person's experience falls in a circle closed on the outside, every sphere opaque to the rest [M, wording U]. My recollection of the standard text, do not print until checked against Eliot's note or Bradley: "My external sensations are no less private to myself than are my thoughts or my feelings. In either case my experience falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside; and, with all its elements alike, every sphere is opaque to the others which surround it." [U] Kant (Critique of Pure Reason, B69 to 71) separates appearance (Erscheinung) from illusion (Schein). Schopenhauer brought the Upanishads' "veil of Maya" into European philosophy (World as Will and Representation, vol. 1, sections 3 and 63 [U]) through the Latin Oupnek'hat of Anquetil-Duperron (1801 to 1802). Paul Deussen, The System of the Vedanta (1883), read Shankara's two standpoints as Kant's empirical and transcendental.
Tier: [M].
Use: Bradley's closed circle is a public-domain sentence for the memory wall; Kant's Erscheinung against Schein defends the essay's care with "illusion". Halbfass and Hacker say Schopenhauer and Deussen shaped the English sense of maya as "illusion".

### E. The comparers

17. Guenon, Schuon and Coomaraswamy on maya
Claim: All three reject "mere illusion". Guenon: maya is the relative nature of manifestation, real in its own degree (Introduction a l'etude des doctrines hindoues, 1921; L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta, 1925; a chapter "Maya" in Etudes sur l'hindouisme [C per P02]). Schuon: maya is the possibility of the relative inside the Infinite, neither being nor non-being, tied to the Sufi "He and not He" (see P05). Coomaraswamy: maya is the divine power that measures the one out into forms (root ma), the artist's skill, and the error is taking the made thing as self-existent (see P03). Textual root: Rigveda 6.47.18, used in Brihadaranyaka 2.5.19 (Indra goes in many forms through his mayas), and Svetasvatara 4.9 to 10 (maya is nature, the Lord the maya-wielder) [M]. Gita 18.61 has the Lord turning all beings by maya "as if mounted on a machine" [M].
Tier: [M] for the gist; [U] for specific works on maya.
Use: The Vedic sense (power, craft) beside Shankara's (superimposition) shows "illusion" as a late narrowing. The Gita machine line suits a machine metaphor.

18. Radhakrishnan and Vivekananda: "not illusion"
Claim: Radhakrishnan (Indian Philosophy vol. 2, 1927; The Hindu View of Life, 1927) argues that Shankara does not teach that the world is simply unreal. It is real as the manifestation of the Absolute, without independent reality, and maya is a principle of relativity. Vivekananda (Jnana Yoga, "Maya and Illusion", London 1896) calls maya a statement of the facts of experience, not a theory (my recollection of the sentence: "Maya is not a theory for the explanation of the world; it is simply a statement of facts as they exist", [U], do not print until checked). Halbfass, Richard King and Rambachan class both as Neo-Vedanta: partly faithful, partly a reply to missionary charges of world-denial. Hacker called the habit of absorbing other religions into one's own frame inclusivism. [Fact-check: the colleague's "(1983)" is the date of the Vienna volume Inklusivismus: Eine indische Denkform, ed. Oberhammer, which appeared after Hacker's death in 1979; the term itself is from his earlier work (my memory: a 1957 essay on religious tolerance and intolerance in Hinduism); English selection in Philology and Confrontation, ed. Halbfass, 1995. [M for the book and year, U for the 1957 essay]. Log C2.]
Tier: [M] for gist; locators unknown.
Use: Quote Radhakrishnan as one voice, and keep Shankara's mithya in view: he did rank the world lower.

19. The Theosophical Glossary, Blavatsky and the sheaths
Claim: The Theosophical Glossary (1892, edited by G. R. S. Mead) defines Maya in outline as illusion, the cosmic power that makes phenomenal existence possible, with only the changeless counted real (wording [U]; see T03). My recollection of the opening, do not print until checked: "Illusion; the cosmic power which renders phenomenal existence and the perceptions thereof possible." [U]. The Glossary was assembled from Blavatsky's notes and published after her death (1891), so treat it as a Theosophical house voice more than as her own sentence-by-sentence writing [M]. The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, has a sentence that maya is an element in all finite things, since everything has only relative reality, depending on the observer's power of cognition [M, wording and page U]. My recollection of the wording, do not print until checked: "Maya or illusion is an element which enters into all finite things, for everything that exists has only a relative, not an absolute reality, since the appearance which the hidden noumenon assumes for any given observer depends upon his power of cognition." I place it in the Proem of vol. 1 (around p. 39 of the 1888 edition) [U]. The Theosophists read the Vedic sheaths (annamaya, pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, anandamaya) and the three bodies as steps in a human constitution of seven principles, with after-death stages. T. Subba Row, a Madras Advaitin in the Society, proposed a Vedantic classification (three upadhis plus Atma) and resisted Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism [M]. In Advaita the sheaths are layers of misidentification to be discriminated away (neti neti), though later Advaita also has a subtle body that transmigrates. Mulaprakriti as the veil over Parabrahman is, to an Indologist, a mix of Samkhya prakriti and Advaita maya [M].
Tier: [M] for structure; [U] for exact Glossary wording and page numbers.
Use: Quote the Glossary as one voice only. The sheaths are real overlap with later Advaita, but Guenon (Le Theosophisme, 1921) and Indologists object to the septenary count and the after-death map.

## 4. How they compare

Level matching. Guenon and Schuon set esoteric beside esoteric: Shankara with Ibn Arabi and Eckhart (the pairing is Schuon's more than Guenon's; Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence, 2006, is by a later writer of Schuon's school and works in that spirit, so credit the method to Schuon and the book to Shah-Kazemi [M]), never a catechism beside the Gita. The equating happens at the top of each tradition, and the terms are placed on a ladder (Atma with Intellect with Spirit).

Structural comparison. Izutsu (Sufism and Taoism) maps each thinker's key terms inside his own system first, then compares the shapes. This is the careful method: find the word that carries the claim and compare its job (see G1-5).

The shared test case. Indian khyativada put one example (shell-silver, rope-snake) to every school and asked what each calls real in it. Alex can borrow the device and line up the traditions on one case, such as a dream, a mirage or a program's memory wall.

Equation by word. Maya with parikalpita, samvrti with vyavaharika: quick, and where specialists object most.

Explaining differences. Guenon: deformation and modern error. Schuon: providential perspectives on one truth. Theosophists: one teaching, partly garbled. Hacker: absorbing the other into one's own frame. Specialists answer that this word does a different job here.

## 5. What scholars say

Hacker (a 1950 article in the ZDMG on Shankara's terms avidya, namarupa, maya and isvara [M for the title, U for the pages]; Vivarta, 1953) found that Shankara's own working vocabulary is adhyasa, avidya and namarupa, that maya is secondary in him, and that the strict vivarta contrast grew in his successors [M for the direction, U for detail]. Shankara does use maya (my memory: Brahma Sutra Bhashya 2.1.14 calls name and form the maya-power of the Lord), so "maya grew in his successors" can only mean the maya doctrine as a system [U]. Halbfass, India and Europe (1988), traces how the West and Neo-Vedanta read maya. Hacker's inclusivism (coined before his death in 1979, published as a volume in 1983, see log C2) and Halbfass warn against any "all traditions say the same" frame led from one tradition. Lusthaus (2002), Kochumuttom and Gold hold that Yogacara is not simple idealism; Gelug scholars hold that Madhyamaka rejects paratantra as ultimately real [M]. Chittick says Ibn Arabi is not a monist in the plain sense and "unity of being" is a later school's label [M]. Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) is the standard history of the Traditionalists. Specialists reject equating maya with the Yogacara imagined nature or the Madhyamaka conventional: the Advaita illusion has a real substrate, and the Buddhist ones deny any.

## 9. Added in the fact-check: the perennial and Traditionalist comparers beyond Huxley (answer to Alex's remark)

All [M] or [U], from memory, none searched. Alex is right that Huxley is neither the only nor the strongest source, and this file already treats him as not a source. See also P06, P07, P09, P11 and G2-2 section 9 for the longer maps.

The phrase. "Philosophia perennis" is older than the movement. Agostino Steuco published De perenni philosophia in 1540, and Leibniz used the phrase in a letter of 1715 and gave it currency [M]. Behind them stands the Renaissance idea of a prisca theologia (Ficino, Pico) [M].

The school that compares religions systematically (called Traditionalist or Perennialist). Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951), Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947) and Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998) are the core [M]; Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), is the standard history [M]. A second generation: Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis, Whitall Perry, Lord Northbourne, Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Huston Smith [M for the names, U for who belongs where].
- Guenon: Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921); La Crise du monde moderne (1927); Le Regne de la quantite et les signes des temps (1945) [M].
- Schuon: De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953, reissued 1975 with an introduction by Huston Smith) [M]. Its thesis is that religions are different on the exoteric surface and meet at the esoteric level. That is the closest published support for Alex's own position of 2026-10-07 (the popular face of a tradition often does not carry the idea, its inner teaching does), and it can be credited to Schuon by name [M].
- Coomaraswamy: The Transformation of Nature in Art (1934); Hinduism and Buddhism (1943); Time and Eternity (1947) [M].
- Smith: Forgotten Truth (1976; later retitled The Common Vision of the World's Religions) and Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981, Gifford Lectures) are the best-known university-level restatements [M for titles, U for the later title of Smith's book].
- Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is an anthology with commentary. It is read as a popularising book, and my recollection is that Huxley himself drew on Guenon and Coomaraswamy [U]. Say "the perennial philosophy", then name Schuon, Guenon and Coomaraswamy for the method, and Huxley only as a popular door in if at all.

Traps for the essay.
- Guenon wrote against Theosophy (Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion, 1921), so the two movements the essay leans on are rivals, not allies. Credit each only with its own views, and do not write "perennialists and Theosophists say" as one voice [M].
- Do not credit Schuon's esoteric-unity thesis to Huxley's book, or Huxley's temperaments to Schuon [M].
- The comparers themselves are open to the charge that they read every tradition through one frame (Hacker's inclusivism; Halbfass). One honest sentence is enough, and it fits Alex's rule that the essay is not a proof [M].
