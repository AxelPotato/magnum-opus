## 1. The picture in brief

Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951) is the founder of what is now called the Traditionalist or Perennialist school; the two other men usually counted as its founders are Coomaraswamy (born 1877, nine years older, an established scholar who came to share his positions in the 1930s [M]) and Schuon (a younger disciple). Guenon was a French metaphysician who began in the Paris occult milieu, broke with it, wrote in 1921 an Introduction to the Study of the Hindu Doctrines that made Hindu Vedanta the master key for reading all religions, and spent his last twenty years or so (1930 to 1951) as a Sufi in Cairo.

His central claim is that behind all genuine religions stands one primordial Tradition. Its metaphysical core is the same everywhere. The outer forms differ because they are adapted to different peoples and ages. Each tradition has a public face (exoterism) and an inner face (esoterism), and only the inner face carries the metaphysics. His key doctrine is that a being has many states, of which the human, bodily state is only one, and that the aim is to realize the Supreme Identity: the Self was never really separate.

He is useful to Alex in two opposite ways. His metaphysics of the Infinite, Universal Possibility and the Self gives the sharpest philosophical form of Alex's axiom that I found in this slice. But he rejected reincarnation as the West understands it, rejected karma as Theosophy understands it, and wrote a whole book against the Theosophy Alex leans on. He was the strictest comparer in the movement: he disliked mixing traditions, required initiation, and was openly anti-modern. Alex's method (many witnesses, no lineage, no initiation) is not his. In one respect he is a model: late in life he changed his mind about Buddhism, after Coomaraswamy and Pallis pressed him.

## 2. Findings

1. Life and milieu.
Claim: Guenon came out of the occult scene and then turned against it. Who: Wikipedia and Sedgwick summaries; paraphrase. In 1905 he left the elite-school preparation for health reasons and moved into Papus's circles (Martinist Order) [C]. He joined the Gnostic Church of Fabre des Essarts, ran the review La Gnose on Hindu, Taoist and Sufi themes, and entered the Thebah lodge of the Grande Loge de France [C]. Ivan Aguéli brought him into the Shadhili Sufi order; results give 1910, 1911 and 1912 [C, conflicting; 1912 is the date I remember as the usual one, [M]]. He took the name Abd al-Wahid Yahya, as I remember at that initiation and not on arrival in Cairo [U]. In 1917 he taught philosophy at Setif [C]. Sylvain Levi, an Indologist, rejected the Introduction as a thesis [C]. He moved to Cairo in 1930 and died there on 7 January 1951 [C]. His wife's death (1928 or 1930) and his Egyptian marriage (1934 or 1937) are dated inconsistently [C, leave out]. Use for the essay: two sentences are enough. What matters is that he himself practised one tradition, Islam; Sedgwick notes he is "not known ever to have recommended anyone to become a Hindu, whereas he introduced many to Islam" [C, as quoted in a result].

2. The books and their dates.
Introduction to the Study of the Hindu Doctrines, 1921 [C]. Theosophy: History of a Pseudo-Religion (Le Theosophisme), 1921, from articles in the Revue de Philosophie [C; the journal is [U]]; English only in 2003 [C]. The Spiritist Fallacy, 1923 [C]. East and West, 1924 [C]. The Esoterism of Dante, 1925 [C]. Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta, 1925 in two results, 1927 in another [C, conflicting; 1925 is also my memory, [M]]. The Crisis of the Modern World, 1927 [C]. The Symbolism of the Cross, 1931 [C]. The Multiple States of the Being, 1932 [C]. The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times, 1945 [C]. 3. The primordial tradition and the unity of the traditions.
Claim: all regular traditions descend from one primordial Tradition; they agree in principle and differ in form. Who: Guenon, from the Introduction on. Paraphrase of Wikipedia's summary: Traditionalism posits a metaphysical unitary source called Tradition that forms the basis of the major religions in their "orthodox" forms [C]. Traditions cited: Hindu first, as the oldest and most complete [C]; then Sufi, Taoist, Christian, Kabbalistic, Masonic. Use for the essay: the closest thing to Alex's sentence that the traditions describe the same principles in different words. Guenon adds two claims Alex should not take over: that the agreement comes from one historical source, and that only lineages with regular transmission count.

4. Exoterism and esoterism; metaphysics against theology and philosophy.
Claim: a tradition has an outer, religious, theological side open to everyone, and an inner, initiatic, metaphysical side. Metaphysics is not philosophy, because it is intellectual intuition and not reasoning. Who: Introduction (1921). Chapter headings in a bookseller listing include Tradition and Religion; Relations between Metaphysics and Theology; Metaphysical Thought versus Philosophical Thought; Esoterism and Exoterism; Metaphysical Realization [C; not checked against a contents page by me]. A line shown as a quotation: "Metaphysics, because it opens out a limitless vista of possibilities, must take care never to lose sight of the inexpressible, which indeed constitutes its very essence." [C, Goodreads quotes via search summary; attributed to the Introduction; locator unknown; quote sites often misplace such lines, so the book is [U]]. Traditions cited: the distinction is applied to Hinduism, Islam (Sufism), China (Taoism inner, Confucianism outer), Judaism (Kabbalah), Christianity. Use for the essay: the twentieth-century origin of the public-face against inner-face idea that Alex's Part two already uses, and the reason Guenon would say a religion's popular teaching may not carry the doctrine.

5. Orthodoxy and initiation.
Claim: spiritual realization needs a regular initiatic line: someone qualified, a rite, a chain back to the origin, and then personal work. Mysticism without that chain is a different, lesser thing. Who: Perspectives on Initiation (1946). Paraphrase: initiation is the transmission of a spiritual influence, only in a regular line, by someone qualified, and the initiatic path differs from the mystical one; "virtual" and "effective" initiation are distinguished [C, book summaries]. The triad "qualification, transmission, work" is [M]. Traditions cited: Masonic and compagnonnage as Western survivals, Sufi orders, Hindu lineages [S]. Use for the essay: the chief difference from Huxley and from Alex. Wikipedia says Traditionalists differ from other perennialists such as Huxley in their anti-modernism and their insistence on esoteric initiation, in which Huxley had no interest [C].

6. The multiple states of the being, the Infinite and Universal Possibility.
Claim: the Absolute or Infinite is Universal Possibility, which cannot be limited, because a limit on possibility would be an impossibility. Being (manifested existence) is not the same as the Infinite. The human state is one of indefinitely many states of the total being. Who: The Multiple States of the Being (1932), with The Symbolism of the Cross and Man and His Becoming as a trilogy [C]. Paraphrase. A result shows, unsourced, the idea that Being, as the principle of manifestation, is not infinite because it does not coincide with total Possibility [C as paraphrase; no wording]. Chapter titles shown include Infinity and Possibility; Possibles and Compossibles; Being and Non-Being; Relationships of Unity and Multiplicity [C, first thirteen]. Traditions cited: Brahma and Ishvara, matched with his technical pair Non-Being and Being [M]. The step "so the possibility of manifestation must be included in the Infinite" is [M]. Use for the essay: an answer to the question a reader will ask of the axiom, "why would a whole experience separation at all?". Guenon's answer: nothing real can be excluded from the Infinite, so manifestation is included. Check the first three chapters before using.

7. The Self and the individual; the Supreme Identity; liberation in this life.
Claim: the true Self (Atma) is not individualized. The "I" of ordinary life is the individual, a modality of one state of a larger being. Liberation is realizing an identity that was always there, not acquiring something new. Who: Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta (1925). Paraphrase of the publisher's description: he leads the reader to the Supreme Identity and to the possibility that a being in the human state might in this very life attain liberation, where all separateness ceases [C, publisher text, not Guenon]. A study summarized in a result says he distinguishes Self (Soi) from Ego (Moi), and Personality from Individuality, the second of each pair existing only because of the first [S]. Traditions cited: Hindu (Atma, Brahma, Purusha, jivan-mukta) [M; the publisher text supports only the idea of liberation in this life]. Use for the essay: a careful form of Alex's third clause, "the separation ends". In Guenon's idiom nothing returns, because the Self never left; the individual realizes what he is. That sits well with the one hand and its fingers.

8. Why separateness feels real: individualism and the cycle.
Claim: the modern West is an anomaly because it denies any principle higher than the individual; the Kali Yuga makes everything harden. Who: The Crisis of the Modern World (1927), The Reign of Quantity (1945). Individualism is "the negation of any principle higher than individuality, and the consequent reduction of civilization, in all its branches, to purely human elements" [C, shown in quotes in a result as from the Crisis; locator unknown; my memory is that the chapter is called "Individualism" and that the sentence has a lead-in such as "By individualism we mean ..."; translations differ, so quote only from the printed book, [U] for the lead-in]. The Hindu cycle: four yugas in descending order, the present one the Kali Yuga. Guenon's own figure, as the summary of a Matheson Trust paper has it, is that it has lasted more than six thousand years [C, Guenon's figure; the standard Puranic reckoning has the Kali Yuga lasting 432,000 years and beginning in 3102 BCE [M], so do not say the Hindu tradition says six thousand]. A summary of Traditional Forms and Cosmic Cycles says that in the Golden Age space predominates over time, and later time dominates [S]. The Reign of Quantity moves from solidification to dissolution and "inverted spirituality" or counter-initiation [C]. Traditions cited: Hindu yugas, with the Greek and Roman ages of gold, silver, bronze and iron [C; Hesiod's Works and Days has five ages (gold, silver, bronze, heroes, iron) and the four-age sequence is Ovid's, Metamorphoses I [M]]. Use for the essay: a ready historical answer to why separateness feels so real: a civilization organized around denying the higher principle. Be careful: his cycles describe the decline of a whole age and run opposite to the personal ascent of Alex's arrow. His "time over space" and Alex's speed and space image only share words; do not link them.

9. Reincarnation: what he rejected.
Claim: (a) Reincarnation in the Western sense, the same individual coming back to the earthly human state, is not a doctrine of any traditional teaching. (b) The traditional doctrine is transmigration, which means a change of state. (c) Reincarnation is a modern idea.
The words: transmigration is "the passage of the being to other states of existence, which are defined by entirely different conditions from those to which human individuality is subject" and "transmigration essentially means change of state" [C, shown as quotes in several results; locator unknown]. The date claim: "the idea of reincarnation, like that of evolution, is a very modern idea; it appears to have materialized around 1830 or 1848 in certain French socialist circles" [C, shown as a quote in two results; locator unknown; "materialized" is odd, so check the translation]. A third result gives "an exclusively modern idea" [C, weaker]. Result summaries add that he held the Orientalists read reincarnation into texts where there is no such content, and that the distinction between transmigration and reincarnation holds even in Buddhism, which he then regarded as heterodox [C].
His argument, as summarized in results [S]: a total being cannot occupy the same state twice, so a being cannot return to the corporeal state. The reason, which I give from memory [M], is that each possibility of manifestation is realized once, and repetition would limit Universal Possibility. It is a corollary of finding 6. What then explains claimed memories and the Hindu rebirth texts? A result gives his answer: psychic elements of the dead individual separate after death and can pass into other beings, human or animal, which matters no more than the fact that the matter of a dissolved body can build other bodies [S]. In The Spiritist Fallacy he adds that spirit messages mirror the milieu of the sitters: "English spirits" deny reincarnation and "French spirits" accept it [C; the quotation marks are probably his irony around the phrase, not a full quotation]. Traditions cited: Hindu samsara and transmigration, Buddhism, with the Theosophists and Kardec's Spiritism as the target. Tier: [C] for the quotes, [S] for the argument.
Use for the essay: the big caution for the lifetimes section. The founder of the movement Alex relies on denied that the traditions teach lifetimes in the Theosophical sense. He accepts survival, many states, and transmigration as change of state, but not return to this world. Alex should not claim that the perennialists agree on reincarnation; better to say what the school teaches and why, and put lifetimes down to his axiom.

10. Reincarnation: what scholars say about his claim.
Historians place the modern Western doctrine with Leroux, Reynaud, Fourier and Kardec between the 1830s and 1860s [C, results from Kardec sources]. Theosophy's own historian shows that Blavatsky taught two different doctrines, a metempsychosis without earthly return in Isis Unveiled (1877) and earthly rebirth of the individuality in The Secret Doctrine (1888) [C, results on "Recycled Lives" (OUP) and Theosophy Forward; author of that book is Julie Chajes [M]]. That partly vindicates Guenon on Theosophy. The harder claim is about India. The Upanishads (for example Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5, Chandogya 5.10), the Gita (2.22, the person who changes clothes) and the Pali canon speak of rebirth in human, animal and other forms; most specialists read these as rebirth in the ordinary sense [M]. Guenon needs these texts to mean change of state; I found no result showing how he read the Gita verse [U]. Use: Alex's essay already gives the Gita clothing image; if he cites Guenon, he should say it is a Traditionalist reading and that Indologists mostly read the texts the other way.

11. Karma.
Claim: Theosophy made karma into a moral law of retribution tied to repeated lives, and that is not the Hindu or Buddhist teaching. Who: Theosophy: History of a Pseudo-Religion (1921), probably. The words: "the word 'karma' quite simply means 'action' and nothing else. It has never had the sense of causality" [C, shown in quotes in one result; locator unknown; I could not confirm which book]. A review says he shows that popular ideas of karma and reincarnation owe more to Theosophy than to Hinduism or Buddhism [C, ICSA Today review by Szimhart]. Traditions cited: Hindu, Buddhist. Tier [C].
Scholars: the word karma means action, yes. But the idea that actions have consequences for the agent is classical (Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5, "as one acts, so one becomes", from memory [M]) and central in Buddhism (for example Anguttara Nikaya 6.63: "it is intention (cetana) that I call kamma", from memory [M]). His sentence is about the word, and overstates if read as denying moral consequence. Use for the essay: do not cite him as a witness for karma as "something I did to another part of myself". He is a witness for the opposite caution, that popular karma talk is a modern overlay.

12. Buddhism.
Claim in 1921: Buddhism is a heterodox deviation. Why: Hindu orthodoxy is defined by acceptance of the Veda, and Buddhism rejects it. The Introduction has a chapter "Concerning Buddhism" under "Orthodoxy and Heterodoxy" [C, listing]. He wrote that Europeans exaggerate Buddhism, "the least interesting of all the Eastern doctrines", which "constitutes a deviation and anomaly for the East" and so looks easier to Westerners [C, quote fragments from a longer sentence shown in a result, which attributes it to the Theosophy book; locator unknown; fragments, so do not quote; the book may be the Introduction or the Theosophy book, [U]]. A result says he followed Shankara in condemning Buddhism as a heresy [C]. The Buddha's Kshatriya birth is mentioned in a result, and the reading of Buddhism as a Kshatriya reaction against Brahmin authority is my memory of Guenon [M]. A critical statement says he relied on Hindu informants who, like Hindu Traditionalists, are "acutely anti-Buddhist" [C, quote shown in a result; source not shown].
The reversal: Coomaraswamy's Hinduism and Buddhism (1943) argued that Buddhism agrees with the essence of Hinduism and is orthodox. Pallis, a Buddhist practitioner, and Schuon pressed the same point. A result drawing on Oldmeadow's biography says Guenon "authorised amendments" to the references to Buddhism in his earlier works [C]. He also reviewed Hinduism and Buddhism in Etudes traditionnelles in 1946 and called it an important work [C]. Traditions cited: Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist. Tier [C].
Use for the essay: an honest example of the movement correcting itself, in line with the rule not to say a tradition "fits badly". Check Guenon's own later notes before quoting.

13. The Theosophy book: arguments and what it found.
Claim: Theosophism is a counterfeit, a pseudo-religion. Who: Guenon, 1921; asked in September 1920 to write against the Theosophical Society [C]. The arguments, as reported:
(a) A name: "theosophisme", to show it has nothing to do with real theosophy in the Western esoteric line [C, Smoley in Quest].
(b) Biography and fraud: Blavatsky's antecedents, the Mahatmas, the Society for Psychical Research (Hodgson, 1885) [C, chapter titles]. Sinnett, who spread Theosophism in Europe, was "genuinely fooled by all of Mme Blavatsky's tricks" [C, fragment shown in a result; fragment, so do not quote]. The Hodgson Report itself has been contested (Harrison, Journal of the SPR 53, in the repository's notes [M]), so Guenon's use of it was already one-sided in a way a Theosophist would point out.
(c) Esoteric Buddhism (Sinnett, 1883) and "Esoteric Christianity" (chapter titles [C]; Besant wrote a book of that name [M]) are misnamed. Besant's version he called pseudo-Christianity or neo-Christianity [C]. Contemporary critics of Esoteric Buddhism said it contained neither esotericism nor Buddhism [C, result on Sinnett].
(d) Karma and reincarnation are modern fabrications (findings 9 and 11).
(e) Politics: the early Society's bid for political power and its role as an agent of British imperialism in India [C, ICSA review]. This is Guenon's accusation. The record is mixed: Annie Besant founded the Home Rule League in 1916 and presided over the Indian National Congress in 1917 [M].
(f) A shared enemy: spiritualist seances do not reach the dead but only leftover astral shells; Guenon and Blavatsky agree [C, Smoley in Quest].
The agreement: Smoley notes that Guenon's thought resembles Theosophy in important ways. Both hold that a central esoteric teaching underlies all religions, and he differs about which lineage is genuine [C].
Scholars: Georges Meautis answered in 1922 that Guenon used his sources selectively and lumped Blavatsky's Theosophy with the later Besant and Leadbeater "Neo-Theosophy" [C]. Leslie Price said Guenon "was given a dossier, but employed it not as a historian, weighing up the contents, but as a polemicist" [C, shown in a result; source work unknown]. The ICSA reviewer says scholars, Eliade among them, still count it an essential expose [C, second-hand].
Use for the essay: it gives Alex's Theosophy paragraph a precise sentence: the school's founder wrote a whole book against the Theosophists while holding their central idea.

14. How Guenon compares: the Universal Man example.
Claim: one doctrine, many names. The cross is the traditional symbol of Universal Man, the being in full communion with all states. The concept comes from Muslim esoterism (al-Insan al-Kamil), and he finds it under other names: the Self or Purusha in the Hindu Vedas and Upanishads, Adam Kadmon in Kabbalah, the King (Wang) in the Far East, and Christ in the Christian world [C, result summarizing The Symbolism of the Cross; the Christian name is the summary's, possibly not his]. The vertical axis is the hierarchy of states, the horizontal the extent of one state; Universal Man stands at the crossing [C]. Use for the essay: a short model of an "equation of terms" in the strictest comparer.

15. Other comparisons.
The Esoterism of Dante (1925) reads the Divine Comedy as an initiatic map, comparing it with Rosicrucian, Masonic and Hermetic symbolism, the Fedeli d'Amore and the Grail [C]. Insights into Islamic Esoterism and Taoism treats Sufism as the heart of Islam and Taoism as the inner side of Chinese tradition, with Confucianism outside [C]. The King of the World has chapters on Shekinah, Metatron and Melki-Tsedeq beside Agarttha [C]. On Judaism he recognized Kabbalah as genuine esoterism but wrote little on it [C, Matheson Trust]. On Christianity he held that the sacraments were lowered to an exoteric level and the esoteric rites kept apart; Schuon repudiated this, and it was the central point of their split in 1948 to 1950 [C; finding 17 gives a second reason, so "central" is the summary's]. Use for the essay: "the same in different words" always came with a claim about each tradition's inner layer, which matches Alex's correction of 2026-10-07 (public face against inner face).

16. His rule against mixing.
He taught that a seeker should follow one regular tradition and not mix forms [C, one result; [M] more broadly]. He blamed his early occult experience, where ill-assorted doctrines could not make a stable edifice [S]. Comparison "from above", at the level of principle, was his; comparison by historians of religion he scorned: the Introduction has chapters on "Official Orientalism" and "The Science of Religions" as targets [C, chapter list]. Use for the essay: Alex's method of quoting many traditions as witnesses is what Guenon opposed in practice. A fair sentence would say so.

17. Influence.
Coomaraswamy corresponded with him and called him not an Orientalist but what "the Hindus would call a guru" [C, fragment in a result; second-hand]. ## 4. How they compare: the method

1. He starts from a metaphysical principle, not from a comparison of texts. The doctrine is stated first in Sanskrit vocabulary, because he thinks Hindu doctrine is the most complete, and the other traditions are then read as saying the same in other terms. Critics point at this (Legenhausen: tenuous comparisons based on a prejudiced selection of materials; Sedgwick: his approach was theological, not historical) [C].
2. Symbols carry the equation: the cross and its axes are the connecting material between traditions (finding 14).
3. He divides each tradition into outer and inner, and compares inner with inner, looking for the inner face in the initiatic or mystical stream (Sufism, Kabbalah, Taoism, Hermeticism) [C].
4. He applies tests: the term in its original language, a regular lineage, metaphysical and not psychological content. The Theosophy book uses them negatively: Blavatsky fails on lineage (the Mahatmas) and on content (karma and reincarnation).
5. He explains differences by adaptation and decline. Forms are adapted to a people and an age; a tradition may lose its inner side (his view of the Christian West). The real opposition is not East against West but the traditional world against the modern West [C, East and West summary].
6. He lets unity stand at the level of principle and tells the practitioner to stay in one form (finding 16).

## 5. What scholars say, short

Sedgwick, the standard historian of the school (Against the Modern World, 2004 [M]), says Guenon made "no pretense of following the standard scholarly methods of Indology", and that for him Hinduism was a repository of spiritual truth, not a body of beliefs that changed over time [C]. Sylvain Levi rejected the thesis for ignoring history and historical criticism, among other reasons [C]. Legenhausen, a Muslim philosopher, finds the movement too reactionary and nostalgic, and its comparisons tenuous [C]. Price and Meautis criticize the Theosophy book's method (finding 13). Poznanovic (Kom, 2014, https://doaj.org/article/fe4115badf0d437389068d829d26edd2) and Dugin (1998) wrote critical studies [C, titles only]. Publishers praise the Introduction as an authentic statement of Hindu metaphysics [C, marketing, not scholarship]. Both can hold: a weak historian of religion, and a coherent system read for its metaphysics.
