## 1. The picture in brief

An anthology of the perennial philosophy does one thing. It takes a short passage from a scripture or a mystic, files it under a theme, and files a passage from another tradition under the same heading. The reader does the equating by reading the passages in a row, and the anthologist's commentary says what the row shows. The device is strong, and its weakness is built in: the headings are the anthologist's, the excerpts are cut from their context, and the translations often carry the equation already. For a metaphysical claim such as Alex's axiom the row of passages is an illustration and not a proof: it shows that the wording can be set side by side, not that the writers meant the same thing.

The books differ in how much they claim. Huxley (1945) is a popular, commentary-heavy anthology of mostly Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist and Sufi mystics under about two dozen themes. It is one book in a long line: the Traditionalist school began with Guenon in 1921, before Huxley, and went on after him with Schuon (1948), Perry (1971) and Smith (1976). Perry (1971) is the Traditionalist answer: over a thousand pages and a stricter frame (outer forms differ, inner truth is one). The Traditionalist school, from Guenon on, is hostile to Theosophy and to Western-style reincarnation; whether Perry's own text says so is not checked (corrected C6). Huston Smith supplies the map the anthologies fill in: four levels of reality, four of selfhood. Wilson (1991) and Novak (1994) are interfaith projects with less metaphysical commitment. Wilber and Campbell compare structures. Hastings's encyclopedia and Max Muller's Sacred Books of the East are not perennialist, and are the right tools for checking a quotation.

Three points matter for Alex. The movement is not one voice, and its founders reject the Theosophical reincarnation his Part two leans on. The Traditionalist rule that outer layers differ while the inner layer agrees is his own policy, and he can credit it. Scholars object strongly to the anthology method, and one honest sentence would protect the page. A fourth, from his own remark: Huxley is the best known of these books in English and not the only one, and several of the others (Perry, Stace, Needleman, Smith) are better suited to the essay's purpose (section H).

6. The rule for differences: outer and inner.
Claim: each religion has an outer layer (law, ritual, dogma, story) and an inner layer (metaphysics and way). Outer layers differ and may contradict each other. Inner layers agree. Schuon's image: radii of a circle, far apart at the rim and meeting at the centre. He also argued that the formal contradictions are not accidents, since each form must be exclusive to keep its integrity.
Who: Frithjof Schuon, De l'unite transcendante des religions (Gallimard, 1948); The Transcendent Unity of Religions (English 1953; revised 1975 with a Foreword by Smith, per P04 [U]); Esoterism as Principle and as Way.
The words: paraphrase. T. S. Eliot praised the book (P04 says "praised by Eliot" is safe in gist; his exact words are unchecked, do not quote them).
Tier: [M].
Use for the essay: this is Alex's rule ("the popular understanding often does not carry the idea, but the inner teaching does") in source form. Credit it in one sentence, and say it is a theory about religions that some scholars reject.

Tier: [M] for the school's position and the Guenon titles and years; [U] for Perry's own treatment.
Use for the essay: the most useful fact in this file. The essay calls the perennial philosophy and Theosophy the two movements that compared religions systematically. True, but they disliked each other, and the Traditionalists reject the reincarnation the essay needs. One sentence belongs in the paragraph: lifetimes come from India, Plato and the Theosophists, and the perennial school's founders kept survival and states of being but not a return to this world. ### C. Huston Smith

9. Forgotten Truth.
Claim: Smith's Forgotten Truth: The Primordial Tradition (Harper and Row, 1976; reissued by HarperSanFrancisco in 1992 as Forgotten Truth: The Common Vision of the World's Religions; corrected C8) sets out a shared map: four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite) and four of selfhood (body, mind, soul, spirit), with consciousness mapped on waking, dreaming, deep sleep and the fourth state (turiya). It leans on Schuon. Following him, it argues that an Infinite without the possibility of expressing itself as finite would not be infinite, so maya is the Infinite's own self-limitation. Guenon's Universal Possibility (P02) is the same argument.
Who: Smith 1976; chapter order and wording [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: Hindu states of consciousness; Christian body, soul, spirit; Platonic chain of being; Islamic and Native American cosmologies; Advaita, Sufi self-disclosure, Kabbalistic tzimtzum, Plotinian procession for the argument.
Tier: [M] for the levels; [U] for the argument's wording in Smith.
Use for the essay: a readable staged map beside the arrow, and a philosophical answer to "why would the whole do this?" that the Safed, Plotinus and lila paragraph lacks: the whole cannot exclude any possibility, and separation is one. It is a metaphysical argument and not evidence.

### E. Structural comparers

13. Ken Wilber.
Claim: three kinds of work. Anthologies he edited: Quantum Questions (Shambhala, 1984), mystical writings of Heisenberg, Schrodinger, Einstein, Planck, Eddington, Jeans, de Broglie and Pauli; and The Holographic Paradigm (1982). A stage map: The Atman Project (1980), Up from Eden (1981), Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995), with stages up the Great Chain toward non-dual unity and substitute goals on the way; his first book, The Spectrum of Consciousness (1977), is the earliest statement of the perennial-philosophy scheme (added [M]). A method: in Eye to Eye (1983) valid knowledge in any field has an injunction (do this), an apprehension (see what comes) and a confirmation (compare with others who did it); later, Integral Spirituality (2006) splits a religion's translative function (meaning at your stage) from its transformative one (moving you on).
Who: the books named; contents [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for titles and gist.
Use for the essay: Quantum Questions is where many readers met Schrodinger as a mystic, so Alex's Schrodinger paragraph can point to it. The injunction scheme matches "an experiment you can run on yourself". The translative and transformative split explains why a religion's public face and inner teaching differ. Critics call Wilber's stages loose science and say he assumes a perennialist metaphysics.

14. Joseph Campbell.
Claim: Campbell compares stories. The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949) finds one shape in hero myths: departure, initiation, return. Later he separates "elementary ideas" (shared patterns, from Adolf Bastian via Jung) from "folk ideas" (local costume). He edited The Portable Jung (Viking, 1971; translated by R. F. C. Hull). He often cited the Rigveda sentence that the real is one and the sages name it variously.
Who: Campbell 1949; Masks of God (1959 to 1968); Portable Jung 1971. Where he quotes Rigveda 1.164.46: [U].
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for titles and gist.
Use for the essay: return as a shape stories keep supports the third clause only as a pattern, since stories do not prove metaphysics. Jung, in his commentary on the Tibetan Book of the Dead, warned against transplanting Eastern ideas.

## 4. How they compare: the method

Six moves recur.

1. Theme first. The anthologist picks a heading (the One, charity, silence) and collects passages by what they say, not where they come from. Huxley's chapter 1 sets a Chandogya line beside Christian and other passages and lets the commentary say they match (which passages are in chapter 1 is [U], C2). Each excerpt was chosen for fit, so the agreement looks strong.

2. A translation key. One vocabulary becomes the key and the others are translated into it: Huxley's Ground and Godhead, Smith's four levels, Perry's Traditionalist set (Principle, manifestation, Intellect). The equation is made at the translation stage, before the reader sees it.

3. Witnesses and experience. Huxley, Stace and Underhill treat mystics as reporters and ask whether the reports agree. This is closest to Alex's "witnesses and not authorities". It needs a further step, showing the reports are of the same thing, which Zaehner and Katz dispute.

4. Outer and inner layers. Perry, Smith and Schuon sort every religion into exoteric and esoteric, so disagreement at the surface does not count against agreement at the centre. Powerful and hard to test: a text that disagrees can always be assigned to the outer layer.

5. Structure over content. Campbell and Wilber compare shapes (departure, initiation, return; stages from body to non-dual unity). The risk is that a shape fits almost anything.

6. Many hands. Wilson, Novak, Hastings and Muller let specialists speak for their own tradition and give agreement and disagreement equal room.

How the movement explains differences: temperament and path (Huxley, Ramakrishna); idolatry, the hardening of a symbol (Huxley); the necessary exclusivity of each form (Schuon); local costume over an elementary idea (Campbell); different stages of development (Wilber).

A worked example in the anthologists' manner, for the essay's karma step. Heading: the other is oneself. Passages: Isha Upanishad 6 and 7, Gita 6.29, Leviticus 19:18, Matthew 22:39, Shantideva on exchanging self and other, the hadith of the one body. Commentary: the ethic follows from the metaphysic. What the row hides is that Leviticus and Matthew ground love in a shared status under God, Shantideva in the lack of a fixed self, the Isha in identity. The essay already says this ("Most of these speak first of a community"), which is good practice.

## 5. What scholars say

Short, from memory, [M].

Steven Katz ("Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", in Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis, 1978) argues that no mystical experience is unmediated: a tradition's concepts shape what is experienced, so cross-tradition agreement is less than it looks. Robert Forman (The Problem of Pure Consciousness, 1990) replied that some experiences are contentless. The debate is unsettled. Zaehner argued against Huxley that mystical types differ.

Stephen Prothero (God Is Not One, 2010) is the best-known popular case against "all religions are the same": they ask different questions and offer different cures. Jonathan Z. Smith wrote that comparison always involves choosing what to compare and why, and that the resemblance is made by the comparer. Richard King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) argues that the Western image of a mystical East was partly made by anthologists of this kind. Wouter Hanegraaff (1996) and Mark Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) give the historians' account: a modern esoteric current with its own rivalries, and Traditionalism as a distinct, often anti-modern school.

Textual scholars add a quieter charge: the anthology removes a passage from its argument. A line of Eckhart read alone sounds like Advaita and, read in its Dominican sermon, belongs to a Christian theology of the soul.

In fairness, the movement's best authors (Coomaraswamy, Schuon, Smith, Shah-Kazemi) know the specialist objections and answer them, usually by appeal to the inner and outer layers. An anthology has no room for that argument.

## 5b. Traps for this slice (added; the usual hunt)

The file contains no direct quotation of Blavatsky, Einstein, Rumi or Eckhart, so those traps did not bite. Traps that do apply to this theme, all from memory, none searched:

1. "The phrase 'perennial philosophy' was coined by Leibniz" is Huxley's own claim, copied into many web pages. Steuco's De perenni philosophia (1540) is earlier (C3).
2. "The Doors of Perception" is Blake's phrase, not Huxley's sentence (C5).
3. "Truth is one, the sages call it by many names" is often credited to Gandhi or Vivekananda or "the Vedas". The source is Rigveda 1.164.46; they quoted it (C16).
4. "He who knows one religion knows none" is Max Muller's line (Introduction to the Science of Religion, 1873 [U for the date]), which echoes Goethe's remark about languages [M]. P01 records it.
5. A Huxley-via-Pfeiffer Eckhart line can be spurious (C4). Check the critical edition.
6. A Traditionalist credited with another's view: Guenon's attack on Theosophy and reincarnation (1921, 1923) belongs to Guenon; do not credit it to Perry, Smith or Coomaraswamy without a text. Coomaraswamy, for his part, accepted a form of rebirth ("transmigration of character", not of a soul; P03's wording from memory).
7. "Perry's Treasury is by Huston Smith": Smith wrote the Foreword to a Perry reissue; the compiler is Perry. "Forgotten Truth" is Smith's own book.
8. Eliot's praise of Schuon: the gist is safe, the exact wording is not (P04).
9. Schuon's Transcendent Unity was reissued by a Theosophical press (Quest Books) [U, P04]. A small irony, not a claim to use.
