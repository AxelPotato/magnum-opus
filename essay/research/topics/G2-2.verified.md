## 1. The picture in brief

The trouble that line 17 names, "if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing", has a name in analytic philosophy of mind: the decombination problem. It mirrors the combination problem, which James posed in 1890 against the idea that many small feelings add up to a big one. Cosmopsychism says the cosmos as a whole is the one basic subject. Its frame is Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism (2010): the whole is basic, the parts real but derived. Shani, Nagasawa and Wager, and Goff defend versions. Kastrup's analytic idealism adds a mechanism by analogy, dissociation. Chalmers (2019) and Miller (2017) press the objection that a subject containing subjects is no easier than subjects adding up. Miri Albahari (2019) is one of the few analytic authors who builds on the mystics on purpose (Stace did it in 1960).

The problem is older than all of them. Advaita (Gaudapada, Shankara) says the question belongs to the level of appearance and that ignorance has no beginning. Ramanuja, about nine hundred years ago, gave the sharpest form of the objection: where does the ignorance sit? Western idealists asked it as the problem of finite centres, and James made it Lectures IV and V of A Pluralistic Universe.

Clause three of the axiom (the separation ends) has almost no analytic literature. It is the religious clause. Most of these authors do not claim that religions agree; they sort theories by logical form. The comparing is done by the perennialists, Schrödinger, Albahari and Kastrup.

Claim: existence monism says there is one concrete thing. Priority monism says there are many but the cosmos is the one basic thing and the parts depend on it.

## 4. How they compare

Analytic philosophers sort; perennialists match.

Schaffer, Nagasawa and Wager, Shani and Chalmers classify. They lay out the logical possibilities (existence or priority monism; micro or cosmic; Berkeleyan or Kantian) and slot historical figures in: Spinoza as a monist, Fechner as a cosmopsychist, Bradley as an absolute idealist. The comparison is by logical form and never claims two religions say the same thing. A Vedantin and a Neoplatonist can sit in one box and mean different things.

Goff compares explanatory hypotheses: in Why? theism, axiarchism and cosmic purpose compete. Religions appear as rivals.

Albahari takes mystical reports as data: she groups reports from contemplative traditions around witness-consciousness and asks which philosophy of mind they favour. Differences between traditions are mostly doctrinal overlay. This is the nearest to the perennial method.

Kastrup reads religions as allegory: myths are symbolic reports of one metaphysical reality, with Schopenhauer and Jung as translators. Schrödinger starts from a puzzle (one world, many minds) and cites the Upanishads and the mystics as precedent, not proof. Ramanuja and the Advaitins compare from inside, as rivals reading the same scripture; Ramanuja's question separates the schools more than any survey unites them.

How differences are explained: Albahari and Kastrup say surface differences are clothing; Goff and Chalmers treat them as real rivals; Ramanuja says the question is whether the many are an error or a real dependence, and Advaita declines it. A fair essay can say all three are live. The Traditionalist perennialists (section 9) say a fourth thing: the religions differ at the outer, exoteric level and agree at the inner, esoteric one. That is closest to Alex's own correction about popular versus inner teaching.

## 5. What scholars say

Priority monism is a respected minority position in metaphysics. Critics doubt the gunk argument and say entanglement does not force holism. Cosmopsychism is a minority within panpsychism, itself a minority. The standing objection is decombination (Miller 2017); another is that a cosmic mind either knows everything we know or explains nothing about why the world has the shape physics finds.

Kastrup is widely read and seldom taken up in academic journals [U]; the dissociation analogy is the weak point. Hoffman's mild thesis is accepted, his theorem has been challenged by simulation work (Berke and others 2022), and the step from a non-veridical interface to consciousness-first is one even sympathisers do not treat as following.

The standard challenge to common-core arguments such as Albahari's is constructivism: Steven Katz (1978; the volume is Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis, his chapter "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism") argues that mystical experience is shaped by tradition and shows no common core; Robert Forman's pure consciousness event (the 1990 edited volume The Problem of Pure Consciousness) argues the opposite [M].

## 9. Perennial philosophy beyond Huxley (added in the second pass, in answer to Alex's remark)

Nothing here was searched. All [M] unless marked [U]. The point: the movement is much bigger and in several ways better than the one anthology that most people know, and some of its best authors are exactly the ones who say what Alex says.

- The name. "Philosophia perennis": Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540) [M]; Leibniz used the phrase in a letter to Nicolas Rémond in 1714 [M for the fact, U for the date]. Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is an anthology with commentary and drew on Coomaraswamy among others [M]. The phrase "Mind at Large" is Huxley's, from The Doors of Perception (1954), borrowed from C. D. Broad [M].
- The Traditionalist or perennialist school proper: René Guénon (Introduction générale à l'étude des doctrines hindoues, 1921; L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vêdânta, 1925; English Man and His Becoming According to the Vedanta); Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (Hinduism and Buddhism, 1943; Time and Eternity, 1947); Frithjof Schuon (De l'unité transcendante des religions, 1948; English The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953); Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis; later Huston Smith (Forgotten Truth: The Primordial Tradition, 1976) and Seyyed Hossein Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, the Gifford Lectures, 1981) [M for authors and titles, U for exact dates of the English editions].
- Their core thesis, which is the one Alex needs: the religions differ at the outer, exoteric level (dogma, ritual, law) and agree at the inner, esoteric or metaphysical level. This is Schuon's "transcendent unity of religions" [M]. It is closer to Alex's correction of 2026-10-07 (popular understanding versus inner teaching) than Huxley's anthology is. Caveat for fairness: the Traditionalists insist that one must follow one orthodox tradition and they oppose mixing traditions [M]; Alex does not say that.
- Trap, a Traditionalist credited with another's view: Guénon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon are the Traditionalists; Huxley is not one in the technical sense. Guénon wrote against Theosophy (Le Théosophisme: histoire d'une pseudo-religion, 1921; L'Erreur spirite, 1923) [M]. So "perennial philosophy and Theosophy as the two comparing movements" is true as a description of what they do, but they are not allies, and the Traditionalists would object to being put beside Blavatsky.
- Comparative scholars of mysticism who are not Traditionalists: W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960), with his extrovertive and introvertive types and a "common core" thesis; Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926, English 1932); William James, Varieties (1902).
