## 1. The picture in brief

This slice is a different branch from the perennial movement's doctrinal books. The movement is wider than one anthology: Guenon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon state the doctrine, Burckhardt, Lings and Pallis read it in Sufi and Buddhist vocabulary, Perry collects passages, Smith and Nasr draw the maps, and Huxley (1945) is the best known popular anthologist in that line and not its centre (section 9). Those books compare metaphysics and doctrine by level, and the movement's strict core says psychological study of mystical states misses the point. The comparers of mysticism compare experiences and the paths to them. They are psychologists, theologians and philosophers of religion, writing between 1901 and 1990, and they disagree with each other more sharply than the perennialists do. Huxley's book overlaps them, because many of his quotations are mystics' own reports.

One line says there is a shared core: James (1902, with qualifiers), Underhill (1911), Bucke (1901), Stace (1960). Mystics of different traditions report that the barrier between self and whole thins or falls, that this feels more real than ordinary life, that it resists description, and that it brings joy and a change of character. Differences are put down to interpretation, vocabulary or temperament.

A middle position keeps both parallels and differences: Otto (1926) sets Eckhart beside Shankara term by term and finds both, and Smart (1965) says doctrine is part of the experience.

The other line says the differences are real: Zaehner (1957) finds at least three kinds of experience and ranks them, and Katz (1978) says no experience is unmediated, so a Buddhist and a Christian never have the same one. Forman (1990) answers Katz with contentless "pure consciousness events".

For Alex the useful things are: James's filmy screen and his reason for modesty; Underhill's five stages as a human-scale ladder to set beside the arrow; Bucke's three levels as an early evolutionary scheme; Stace's two types of unity experience; and the Katz debate, which he should know about and answer. Almost nothing here concerns lifetimes or karma, because these authors compare experience, not rebirth doctrine (section 7).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 James: the four marks and the "unanimity"
Claim: James gives four marks of mystical states (ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity); the first two suffice to call a state mystical. Across Hindu, Neoplatonic, Sufi and Christian sources, and what he calls Whitmanism, he finds one recurring note, the overcoming of the barrier between the individual and the Absolute, and says the agreement should make a critic stop and think.
Who: William James (1842 to 1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), Gifford Lectures, Edinburgh, 1901 to 1902. The marks open Lecture XVI. The unanimity passage: Lecture XVI, near its end, by my recall, [U] (the colleague said XVII; see C1); the three closing conclusions are at the end of Lecture XVII. Safe locator until checked: Lectures XVI and XVII ("Mysticism").
The words (paraphrase): the state cannot be adequately reported; it feels like knowledge of depths the intellect misses; it passes in an hour or two (his phrase, as I recall it, is half an hour or at most an hour or two); the person feels held by a greater power. Recalled wording of the passage, [M, not cleared for quotation]: "In mystic states we both become one with the Absolute and we become aware of our oneness. This is the everlasting and triumphant mystical tradition, hardly altered by differences of clime or creed. In Hinduism, in Neoplatonism, in Sufism, in Christian mysticism, in Whitmanism, we find the same recurring note, so that there is about mystical utterances an eternal unanimity which ought to make a critic stop and think, and which brings it about that the mystical classics have, as has been said, neither birthday nor native land." The essay's paraphrase (line 53) is right in gist and leaves out Whitmanism. His three conclusions: mystical states are authoritative for the person who has them; they give outsiders no duty to accept them uncritically; they break the claim of the sense-bound, rationalistic mind to be the only kind. He puts differences into the "over-beliefs" added afterward (the term is most at home in Lecture XX and the Postscript), and allows a dark counterpart he calls diabolical mysticism [wording unchecked, Lecture XVII by my recall].
Traditions cited: Hindu yoga and Vedanta (Vivekananda on samadhi), Buddhist dhyana, Sufism (Al-Ghazali), Christian mystics (Ignatius, Teresa, John of the Cross), Plotinus, Tennyson, Whitman, Symonds; he also cites Bucke (the lecture is [U]).
Tier: [M] for marks, lectures XVI and XVII as a pair, and list; [U] for which of the two lectures holds the unanimity passage; [M, not cleared] for the recalled wording.
Use: his second conclusion is the best possible authority for Alex's stance that the essay is not a proof. The essay's current sentence on James is right in gist; add Whitmanism and the three limits.

### 2.2 James: the filmy screen, transmission, the wider self
Claim: ordinary waking consciousness is one type among others that lie right beside it, parted by the filmiest of screens. Separately, the brain may transmit consciousness without producing it.
Who: Varieties, Lecture XVI (the screen, after the passage on alcohol and nitrous oxide); the keynote of mystical states is reconciliation, as if the opposites that make our trouble melted into unity (lecture [U], probably XVI, see C3); Lecture XX and the Postscript (the conscious person is continuous with a wider self through which help comes, "the more"); and Human Immortality: Two Supposed Objections to the Doctrine (the Ingersoll Lecture for 1897, published 1898; see C4).
The words (paraphrase): in 1898 James proposed that brain function can be permissive or transmissive, like a lens or coloured glass, so that mind's dependence on brain does not prove the brain makes the mind.
Tier: [M] for content; [U] for the exact lecture of the reconciliation line and for the delivery year of Human Immortality.
Use: a respectable reply, from 1898, to Alex's first worldview (consciousness depends on the brain), and a one-line veil image. Caution: James offered it as a possibility, not a demonstration.

### 2.3 James: surrender, saintliness, fruits

Traditions cited: Christian and Protestant revival cases mostly; Hindu and Buddhist saints in the saintliness lectures; the mind-cure movement (lectures IV and V), which taught relaxing and letting go.

### 2.4 Underhill: definition, method, sources
Claim: mysticism is the art of establishing a conscious relation with the Absolute, an organic process of growth, with love and will as method, and sharply distinct from magic and occultism.
Who: Evelyn Underhill (1875 to 1941), Mysticism: A Study in the Nature and Development of Man's Spiritual Consciousness (London: Methuen, 1911). Opening chapters and the chapter on mysticism and magic (numbers [U]).
The words (paraphrase): mysticism is the innate tendency of the human spirit toward harmony with the transcendental order, whatever theory the intellect holds about it. Her four tests: it is active and practical; its aims are wholly spiritual; its One is a living object of love and its method is love; and union is a definite state of enhanced life reached by a long process, the Mystic Way (C7). The magician wants to get; the mystic wants to give.
Traditions cited: overwhelmingly Western (Plotinus, then the Christian line from Pseudo-Dionysius through Eckhart, Ruysbroeck, Suso, Tauler, Julian, the two Catherines, Teresa, John of the Cross, Boehme, Blake and Jacopone); thinner Sufi and Hindu material. She also wrote the introduction to the Tagore and Underhill Kabir (C8). Before 1911 she had passed through occult circles (a short membership of a Golden Dawn order, as I recall) [U, check the Oxford DNB]; Mysticism draws a hard line against the occult.
Tier: [M] for gist, title and sources; [U] for chapter numbers.
Use: the one comparer of the stages, and a Christian (Anglican) writing from inside, so a good witness that the Christian inner layer matches the others.

### 2.5 Underhill: the five stages
Claim: the mystic way has five phases, in this order: the Awakening of the Self; Purification of the Self; Illumination; the Dark Night of the Soul (which she also discusses as surrender); Union (the Unitive Life).
Who: Mysticism (1911), Part Two, "The Mystic Way". Chapter order and numbers [U]. Some summaries name the fourth phase "Surrender" and others "the Dark Night"; the colleague's double name is right as far as I can tell, but check which word she puts in her own headings [U].
The words (paraphrase): she extends the old threefold scheme (purgative, illuminative, unitive, going back to Pseudo-Dionysius) with an awakening in front and a dark night before union. She says the stages overlap and the soul can swing back. Separate chapters on introversion (recollection, quiet, contemplation) and on voices and visions sit outside the five names; where they fit is [U].
Traditions cited: built from Christian mystics, with Teresa's seven mansions (the Interior Castle, 1577) and John of the Cross's night (the Dark Night, written about 1578 to 1579) as main maps. That the pattern is general is her claim, and not all specialists accept it.
Tier: [M] for the five names; [U] for details of placement.
Use: five stages with a clear low point before the top. Caution for Alex: her scale measures the person's relation to the Absolute, his measures how wide a piece of the whole the person is at home in. A one-to-one mapping would be forced. My own observation, not hers: the dark night is where what carried you so far stops working, which echoes the wall at the corner of the small square.

### 2.6 Underhill: union, ego, return
Claim: union is the self made complete, not annihilated, and the great mystics come back as agents.
Who: Mysticism (1911), the chapter on the unitive life (title and number [U]).

### 2.7 Bucke: three levels, and religions as residue
Claim: mental evolution has three levels: simple consciousness (animals), self consciousness (humans, with language and abstraction), and cosmic consciousness, a third level now appearing sporadically and due to become common. The founders of the great religions had it, and scriptures and creeds are what it left behind.
Who: Richard Maurice Bucke (1837 to 1902; born in England, raised in Ontario; Canadian psychiatrist; friend and biographer of Whitman), Cosmic Consciousness: A Study in the Evolution of the Human Mind (Philadelphia: Innes and Sons, 1901).
The words (paraphrase): the person feels the cosmos as a living presence, not dead matter. His marks as I recall them: a subjective light, moral elevation, intellectual illumination, a sense of immortality, loss of the fear of death, loss of the sense of sin, suddenness, onset usually between about 30 and 40, a charm others notice. His own illumination came in 1872, at about 35, in England after an evening of poetry (Wordsworth, Shelley, Keats, Browning and above all Whitman, as I recall) and a ride home in a hansom cab; he tells it in the third person [M for 1872 and the third person, [U] for the details].
Traditions cited: main cases (order [U]): Buddha, Jesus, Paul, Plotinus, Mohammed, Dante, Las Casas, John of the Cross, Francis Bacon, Boehme, Blake, Balzac, Whitman, Edward Carpenter, plus lesser cases. He equates the state with Nirvana and the Kingdom of Heaven, and I recall "Brahmic Splendor" for the Hindu name [U].
Tier: [M] for the three levels, author, date and thesis; [U] for exact marks, case order and locators.
Use: the earliest author in this slice with an arrow of stages and a next stage the species is moving toward, which is close to Alex's picture, and a historical version of "the traditions agree because people woke up". Caution: doubtful entries, almost all men. His term passed to Ouspensky's Tertium Organum [M] and, I believe, to Maslow [U]. "Cosmic consciousness" is Bucke's coinage and not an Eastern or Theosophical term; do not write "the Hindu term" for it (section 10).

### 2.8 Otto: Eckhart and Shankara
Claim: Eckhart and Shankara show striking parallels and also deep differences that come from their religions.
Who: Rudolf Otto (1869 to 1937; Lutheran theologian; author of The Idea of the Holy, 1917, on the numinous), West-oestliche Mystik (Gotha, 1926); English Mysticism East and West, trans. Bertha Bracey and Richenda Payne (New York: Macmillan, 1932).
The words (paraphrase): parallels: the divine ground or spark of the soul in Eckhart and the atman in Shankara; the Godhead above God and the nirguna Brahman above Ishvara; the creature as nothing without the divine; knowing that is a becoming one. The pairing is also recorded as Otto's formula by a sibling's search result (C17) [C]. Differences as I recall them [U]: in Eckhart the Godhead is dynamic and overflowing, with the birth of the Son in the soul, and the creature is a real creation; in Shankara the Absolute is static being and the world is maya. Otto treats the two as comparable types stamped by their religions, not as identical.

### 2.9 Stace: the universal core
Claim: all mystical experience contains a universal core in two types; cultures differ in the interpretation added afterward.
Who: Walter T. Stace (1886 to 1967; British civil servant in Ceylon, then Princeton philosopher), Mysticism and Philosophy (Philadelphia: Lippincott, 1960), and The Teachings of the Mystics (New York: New American Library, 1960). The chapter on the core of mysticism (title [U]).
The words (paraphrase), my recollection of his table, which matches the colleague's. Extrovertive: (1) the unifying vision, all things are One, seen through the objects of sense; (2) a more concrete sense of the One as an inner life in all things. Introvertive: (1) the unitary consciousness, pure consciousness emptied of sensory and conceptual content, the One or Void; (2) non-spatial and non-temporal. Both share marks 3 to 7: a sense of objectivity or reality; blessedness, peace, joy; a feeling of the holy or divine; paradoxicality; alleged ineffability. Both types occur in Eastern and Western sources, in his account (C18).
Traditions cited: Upanishads (the Mandukya's fourth state is the natural case [U]), Buddhist nirvana and the Void, Taoism, Plotinus, Eckhart, Ruysbroeck, Boehme, Sufis, Ramakrishna, modern Western cases. I take his extrovertive cases to include Bucke's [U].
Tier: [M] for the two-type structure and shared marks; [U] for exact wording and cases.
Use: the extrovertive type, the world seen as one living whole, fits Alex's axiom of a whole experiencing separation; the introvertive type fits "the separation ends". He can name both and say they are two doors to one claim. Check the table before quoting the seven marks.

### 2.10 Stace: method and the self
Claim: separate experience from interpretation, and ignore the cause of the experience (his principle of causal indifference). The empirical self falls away, and what remains is the universal Self.
Who: Mysticism and Philosophy (1960).
The words (paraphrase): a drug-induced, ascetic or spontaneous experience of one type counts as the same experience. "I became one with God", "I am Brahman" and "I saw the Void" differ only in doctrine. He treats personal union with God as an interpretation of the introvertive experience, which is a move against Zaehner [M].
Tier: [M] for the method terms; [U] for his argument on the self.
Use: it answers "but the traditions disagree", and Zaehner's and Katz's replies are the whole debate in miniature.

### 2.11 Zaehner: three types, then fana and baqa
Claim: there are at least three kinds of mystical experience: nature mysticism (all in one, one in all; his coinage "panenhenic"), monistic mysticism (the soul isolated and finding itself, as in Samkhya-Yoga and Advaita), and theistic mysticism (loving union with a personal God). He argued they cannot be merged, and that Huxley's merging of them, in the mescaline of The Doors of Perception, was a mistake. He ranks the third highest.
Who: R. C. Zaehner (1913 to 1974; Spalding Professor of Eastern Religions and Ethics at Oxford, 1952 to 1974; Catholic convert, 1946), Mysticism Sacred and Profane: An Inquiry into Some Varieties of Praeternatural Experience (Oxford: Clarendon, 1957); Hindu and Muslim Mysticism (London: Athlone, 1960); Concordant Discord (Gifford Lectures, 1970), which is more conciliatory; a commentary on the Bhagavad Gita (1969) that reads it as theistic; and Drugs, Mysticism and Make-Believe (1972), a later attack on drug mysticism (C16).
The words (paraphrase): he reported his own mescaline trial as at most nature-mystical, not religious [M]. He drew nature cases from Jefferies, Rimbaud and Proust, monism from the Upanishads and Samkhya-Yoga, theism from John of the Cross, the Gita and Ramanuja. In the 1960 book he argues that the early Sufi Bistami's "annihilation" (fana) was monistic and Indian-influenced, and that Junayd moved to a sober, theistic form in which the self returns and abides (baqa); later scholars contest the Indian-influence thesis [M].
Tier: [M] for the books, three types, the Huxley quarrel and fana and baqa; [U] for chapter structure and how much of the Bistami thesis stands.
Use: the best educated opponent of "they all say the same". Fana then baqa is the Sufi form of union then return, a Muslim counterpart to Underhill's active unitive life.

### 2.13 Smart: doctrine is part of the experience
Claim: phenomenology alone cannot compare mystical experiences, because each tradition's doctrine helps define what the experience is. Buddhist nirvana and Vedantic Brahman are not the same without the doctrines around them. ### 2.14 Katz, the foil, and Forman's reply
Claim: there are no pure, unmediated mystical experiences. Tradition, concepts and training shape the experience before, during and after, so the same words from a Buddhist and a Christian do not name the same experience. Forman replies that some events have no content at all, so nothing is there for tradition to shape.
Who: Steven T. Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", in Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (Sheldon Press and OUP, 1978); Mysticism and Religious Traditions (1983, with his essay "The Conservative Character of Mystical Experience"); Mysticism and Language (1992); Mysticism and Sacred Scripture (2000). He treats Stace's split of experience from interpretation as untenable, and argues that the Jewish goal, devekut (cleaving to God), differs from Christian union and from Buddhist emptiness, and that such goals are trained for.

## 4. How they compare: the method

1. James collects first-person reports into one pool and sorts them by features, not tradition, then builds the marks by induction. Differences go into "over-beliefs". The test of value is fruits.
2. Underhill reads the Christian mystics as a body, extracts a path from their lives, and then holds that other traditions fit it.
3. Bucke lists the people he judges to have had the same awakening, tabulates their marks, and derives an evolutionary story. Selection is his weak point.
4. Otto takes one pair, Eckhart and Shankara, and compares term by term in two parts, parallels and differences, explaining differences by the religion each stands in.
5. Stace defines shared marks in a table, splits them into two types, separates experience from interpretation, and argues that differences fall in the interpretation column.
6. Zaehner sorts by type, ranks the types, and uses cases that will not merge, with his own theology as the yardstick.
7. Smart maps the dimensions of each tradition and compares across the grid; experience is one row of several.
8. Katz starts from one tradition's training and shows how it shapes the experience, and asks for each claim of sameness what concepts were already present.
9. Happold anthologises [U].

How differences are explained: over-beliefs (James), temperament (Underhill), wrappers around one event (Bucke), the stamp of a religion (Otto), interpretation (Stace), difference in kind (Zaehner), doctrine inside the experience (Smart), mediation all the way down (Katz).

## 5. What scholars say

- James: praised for starting from experience, criticised for a Protestant, individual, private view of religion. Wayne Proudfoot (Religious Experience, 1985) argued that descriptions are shaped by the describer's concepts and that ineffability claims shield experience from question [M].
- Underhill: still the standard English handbook of the stages, built from Western Catholic sources, so treating them as universal is her assumption. Grace Jantzen (Power, Gender and Christian Mysticism, 1995) and Michel de Certeau (The Mystic Fable) argue that "the mystic" as a private inner experience is a modern construct [M].
- Bucke: a pioneer with dated evolutionism and retrospective, mostly male case selection.
- Otto: respected as a comparer, criticised for a Lutheran yardstick that makes Eckhart the fuller form. Eckhart's own theology was in part condemned in 1329 (the bull In agro dominico, issued after his death), so equating him with the Upanishadic view is itself a claim [M].
- Stace: widely used and widely criticised for circularity (cases that do not fit are called not mystical) and a hidden monist bias. His work underlies the questionnaires used in modern psychedelic research [M].
- Zaehner: his confessional ranking is judged a bias and the Bistami thesis is contested, but his warning against merging unlike experiences has been absorbed.
- Katz: dominant from about 1978 to 1990, then challenged by Forman and by Ferrer's participatory view (2002) [M]. Richard King (1999) and Robert Sharf (1995 and 1998, C13) question "experience" as a category [M].
- Overall: most specialists reject a strong common-core claim, many also reject "everything is different", and a middle view of similar structures with different content is common [M, a summary of the field and not a count].

## 10. Traps for this slice (added)

Quotations and attributions that circulate wrongly, or that a careless essay could get wrong. None of these was checked by search; each is from memory, [M] unless marked.

1. "If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, infinite" is Blake (The Marriage of Heaven and Hell), not Huxley's sentence and not Jim Morrison's. Huxley took the title of The Doors of Perception (1954) from it.
2. James's screen is "the filmiest of screens". Online it is often "the thinnest" or "the flimsiest". Quote it only after the page is open.
3. "The mystical classics have neither birthday nor native land" is James (Varieties, in the unanimity passage), written with "as has been said", so he is passing on a saying. It circulates in perennialist writing without his name.
4. "Mysticism is the art of union with Reality" belongs, as I recall it, to Underhill's Practical Mysticism (1914). The 1911 Mysticism uses the longer form about establishing a conscious relation with the Absolute. Do not credit the short line to the 1911 book.
5. "Cosmic consciousness" is Bucke's term (1901). Ouspensky, Maslow and many later writers reuse it. It is not a Sanskrit or Theosophical term; Bucke himself matched it to Hindu words.
6. Underhill's stages are her reading of Christian mystics. Do not present them as "the stages of all mystics" without her own caveat, and do not map them onto the arrow (section 2.5).
7. Katz is quoted in shortened form online ("there are no pure experiences"). The sentence has a parenthesis: "There are NO pure (i.e. unmediated) experiences." Katz does not deny that traditions use similar words; his claim is about the experience, not the vocabulary.
8. Stace's "introvertive" is not "Eastern" and "extrovertive" is not "Western": he finds both in both.
9. Eckhart, three traps. (a) "The eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me" is genuine Eckhart (Sermon 12, "Qui audit me", as I recall) [M for the sermon, [U] for the number]. (b) "If the only prayer you ever say in your entire life is thank you, it will be enough" is widely credited to Eckhart; I know of no source for it in his works and treat it as unsourced [U]. (c) Anthology lines from Pfeiffer's 1857 edition may not be Eckhart's own; check against Quint's critical edition [M, from P07 C4].
10. "Huxley coined the perennial philosophy" and "Leibniz coined it" are both wrong; Steuco used it in 1540 [C, P01]. The quotation traps for Huxley, Einstein, Rumi and Blavatsky belong to other slices (P08, P12 and the T files).
