## 1. The picture in brief

The BE half of Part two is well covered. The DO half is where the traditions are most insistent and the draft is thinnest. The same position appears in every layer: release is the ground of action, not a substitute for it. The Gita says it in the god's own voice (3.22), Rabbi Tarfon as an instruction (Avot 2:16), Teresa as the purpose of the highest prayer, Zen in a story about a fox, Wang Yangming as the unity of knowing and acting, the Stoics as a named discipline.

Each tradition also names the failure of the person who uses stillness to avoid acting: the Gita's tamasic abandonment (18.7), the Church's quietism (1687, 1699), the fox of Mumonkan 2, Mark's withered hand, the Stoic idle argument, Cassian's acedia, Ghazali on trust without means. So the vegetable and bypassing worries (essay line 149) have answers inside the traditions.

Stillness and work also sit on a timetable (Benedict ch. 48, Zen samu, Seneca on alternating solitude and company, the Sabbath, the Naqshbandi solitude in the crowd), which is the material for the hand-off to Part three (line 161). The held body appears mostly as hard heart, stiff neck and knot, an ethical condition in body words; Daoist and Buddhist texts are the most body-literal. None lists Gennady's three words; the match is thematic.

Honest limit: no tradition equates release with the BE axis exactly, and the standard Advaita reading ranks knowledge above action.

## 2. Findings

A. The Gita and its readers

1. Gita 2.47 to 2.50. Claim: a right to the act and none to its fruit, with no attachment to inaction (2.47). Established in yoga, act; yoga is evenness in success and failure (2.48) and skill in action (2.50). Handles: "ma te sango 'stv akarmani", "yogasthah kuru karmani", "yogah karmasu kausalam" (Zaehner and Miller render the last as skill in action [U on exact English]). Shankara, as I remember, reads kausalam as the skill of acting so that the act does not bind, i.e. action done in evenness [M, gist]; so "skill" is a gloss with more than one reading. Layer: scripture. [M]. Use: the one-line answer to the vegetable worry, and a definition of high agency (evenness is BE, skill is DO).

2. Gita 3.4 to 3.9. Claim: abstaining from works does not bring freedom (3.4); no one stands without acting for an instant (3.5); one who restrains the organs of action but sits brooding on sense objects is a deluded hypocrite, "mithyacara" (3.6); do the required work, since even the body's upkeep fails without it (3.8). Layer: scripture. [M]. Use: 3.6 is the Gita's own diagnosis of the outwardly quiet, inwardly gripping person, near Part one's cold observer.

3. Gita 3.19 to 3.25. Claim: Krishna has nothing to gain in the three worlds, yet acts untiringly (a-tandrita, 3.23), because if he stopped the worlds would fail (3.22 to 3.24). Janaka and others reached perfection by action, with loka-sangraha in view (3.20). The wise should act unattached as the ignorant act attached, wanting loka-sangraha (3.25), the holding together of the world (translators: welfare, maintenance, cohesion). The word occurs at both 3.20 and 3.25. Shankara's and Aurobindo's glosses [U]. Layer: scripture. [M] verses. Use: the strongest text for a reason bigger than yourself in the DO half: the one who needs nothing still acts.

4. Gita 4.18, 5.2, 6.1, 6.16, 18.5 to 18.11, 18.23 to 18.28. Claim: the wise sees inaction in action and action in inaction (4.18). The yoga of action beats renunciation of action (5.2). The true renouncer does the work that must be done without leaning on its fruit, not the one who merely stopped (6.1). Yoga is not for those who eat or sleep too much or too little (6.16). Dropping required work from delusion is tamasic (18.7); dropping it because it hurts or from fear is rajasic and earns nothing (18.8); no embodied being can drop action entirely (18.11). The sattvic doer has steadiness and energy (dhriti, utsaha) (18.26); the tamasic doer is stiff (stabdha), lazy (alasa), despondent, a procrastinator (18.28). Layer: scripture. [M] content, [U] exact verse splits. Use: the direct answer to what the text tells someone who uses release to avoid acting: it calls that delusion. 6.1 is the verse for Drifting, 6.16 for Driven.

5. Gita 18.45 to 18.48. Claim: each finds perfection through their own work (18.45 to 18.46). One's own dharma, imperfectly done, beats another's done well (18.47; also 3.35). Do not abandon innate work for its flaws, since every undertaking is covered by fault as fire by smoke (18.48). Layer: scripture. [M]. Caution: svadharma is tied to varna (18.41 to 18.44); Ambedkar read it as a defence of caste [M]; Aurobindo re-read it as inner nature (svabhava) [M]; Gandhi's reading changed over his life and was for years a defence of hereditary calling, so do not group him with the aptitude readers [U]. Use: "do not drop the work because it is imperfect" answers Drifting; name the caste context.

6. Isha Upanishad 2, 9 to 11. Claim: doing works here, one should wish to live a hundred years (2). Those who pursue ignorance (avidya) enter blind darkness, those absorbed in knowledge (vidya) a greater darkness (9). Whoever knows both crosses death by one and reaches immortality by the other (11). Shankara reads avidya as ritual works and vidya as worship of deities, not as Self-knowledge [M, gist]; so he is not a witness for "knowledge and action together" from these verses. Layer: Upanishad. [M]. Use: the shortest scriptural statement that neither axis alone will do, with the Shankara caveat. The essay already uses Isha 6 to 7.

7. The readers. Shankara (8th century CE; traditional dates 788 to 820): both paths are taught, but liberation comes by knowledge with renunciation; he rejects combining knowledge and action. Ramanuja (11th to 12th century): action as worship is a stage toward devotion. Tilak (Gita Rahasya, written in prison 1910 to 1911, English by Sukthankar 1935): the Gita teaches karma-yoga against the renunciation reading. Gandhi (Anasakti Yoga, Gujarati; English by Desai, 1946): the core is non-attachment, and the war is inner. Aurobindo (Essays on the Gita, 1916 to 1920): works as sacrifice, joined to knowledge and devotion. Vivekananda (Karma-Yoga, New York 1895 to 1896): work as worship, detachment as mastery, the butcher who teaches a renouncer [M, gist]. The Ramakrishna Mission motto: for one's own liberation and the welfare of the world [M]. Layer: commentary and modern teaching. [M]; Gandhi's dates [U]. Use: honesty; Shankara is a prominent ally of release over action. Vivekananda also made the four yogas the Western template (Huston Smith: four temperaments) [M]. The Gita itself refuses the split (5.4 to 5.5).

C. Buddhist, Daoist, Confucian

13. Mumonkan 2 (1229), Hyakujo's fox. An old man, once an abbot, became a fox for 500 lives for saying that a person of great practice does not fall into cause and effect. Baizhang's answer, that such a person does not obscure cause and effect, frees him, and the fox gets a monk's funeral. Dogen revisits it in the Shobogenzo (fascicles Daishugyo and Jinshin Inga, [M ~80%]) [U on detail]; Heine 1999 is the study [M]. Layer: koan collection. [M]. Use: the Zen text against "the awakened are beyond consequences", the Drifting error in one story; it pairs with the karma section (line 101).

14. Ox-herding and Baizhang. In the ten pictures (Kuoan Shiyuan, 12th century) the eighth, the empty circle, is not the end; the ninth returns to the source; the tenth has the master enter the market with open hands [M]. Compare Mazu, "ordinary mind is the Way" [M]. "A day without work, a day without food" and the story of the old master refusing to eat when his tools were hidden are attached to Baizhang Huaihai (720 to 814); I cannot name the earliest source. Foulk, Poceski (2007) and Yifa (2002) treat his monastic code as lost, rebuilt from Song texts (Chanyuan qinggui 1103) [M picture, U earliest source]. Layer: Zen teaching, monastic tradition. Use: the last stage is work among people. Write "the Baizhang tradition says". "Before enlightenment chop wood, carry water; after, chop wood, carry water" has no classical source I know [U]. The nearest early source for the water-and-firewood image is the verse ascribed to Layman Pang (Pang Yun, d. 808) about marvelous activity being drawing water and carrying firewood [M ~85%].

15. Dogen, Tenzo Kyokun (1237). Dhammapada 276: you must make the effort, the Buddhas only point [M]. He moved from teaching quiet sitting to polishing oneself in affairs after students came to love quiet and dislike activity, and he put down a rebellion in 1519 [M]. Layer: philosopher-teacher. ## 4. How they compare: the method

Vivekananda sorts by temperament: jnana, bhakti, karma and raja yoga are four doors for four kinds of people (Karma-Yoga, 1896). Cost: the Gita itself refuses the split. The Traditionalists sort by rank and function: Guenon reads the Gita through the priest and warrior order and ranks knowledge above action; Coomaraswamy matches craft guilds, Hindu vocation and medieval Christian craft as one social form in which work carries meaning. Cost: a hierarchy and a politics the sources do not all share. The Theosophists sort by an ethical test: the Voice of the Silence judges the two paths by whether they leave the world to suffer. Cost: the Pratyekabuddha counter-type is polemic. Guenon and the Theosophists were enemies, so do not present them as one camp.

Scholars compare inside one tradition, by word: Slingerland follows wu wei, Hadot the Stoic disciplines, Idel corporeality, Heine the fox. That suits Alex: pick the word that carries the point (kausalam, horme, obras, wu wei, zhi xing he yi, avodah be-gashmiyut, kirat karni, tawakkul) and say what it does in its own argument. Comparers explain differences as temperament (Vivekananda), function (Guenon) or outer dress over an inner core (Schuon); specialists call them real differences of doctrine.

## 5. What scholars say, short

Gita: Davis (2015) and Malinar (2007) show a long fight over action against renunciation, and that each modern reader read it to suit a programme. Quietism: Cognet calls the label the opponents' and the process political; check Knox before pairing him [U]. Baizhang: Foulk and Poceski treat the work rule as partly a Song-period image. Zhuangzi: Slingerland argues for skill; Graham and Hansen read the book as perspectival. Traditionalists: Sedgwick (2004) shows the Guenon and Evola split and an anti-modern politics. Theosophy: Campbell (1980) and Godwin (1994) call it influential and mixed in reliability; the Voice has no known source text.

## 9. Perennial, Traditionalist and Theosophical authors beyond Huxley, for the DO half (new in this pass)
