## 1. The picture in brief

These passages do four jobs.

The one and the many. Brihadaranyaka 2.4.14, Katha 2.1.10 and Gita 13.16 each say the duality holds "as it were" (iva). Chandogya 6.2.3 has Being decide to become many; Brihadaranyaka 1.4 has the self alone, afraid, wanting a second. Plotinus V.1.1 and Marcus 8.34 add self-will and discontent as causes of separation, and Marcus adds that the cut-off part can rejoin (the Greek verb, as I recall it, is sumphunai, "to grow together again" [M, checker]).

Lives. Gita 6.40 to 45 and 7.19 say that one life is not enough and progress carries over; Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 5 has the caterpillar and the goldsmith.

The wall as an obstacle that is removed. Yoga Sutra 4.3, Mundaka 2.2.8, Tao Te Ching 48, Zhuangzi 6, Plotinus I.6.9, Tattvartha Sutra 10.7 and Wang Yangming all describe liberation by subtraction. This is the strongest cross-tradition structure here, and it is Part one's letting go.

The ethic of the fingers: Marcus 2.1, Gita 13.28 (13.29 where Arjuna's opening verse is counted [M, checker]), Dhammapada 129, Mencius 2A6, Wang Yangming, Zhang Zai.

Where a tradition does not say it. SN 12.48 names "all is a unity" an extreme; MN 22 calls one version of "the self is the world, after death I shall be permanent" a fool's teaching; DN 16 denies a closed fist; Gaudapada 2.32 says nobody is bound or freed; the Sutrakritanga refutes the single soul; Zhang Zai ends in death as rest.

## 4. How they compare

The anthologist sets short passages under a theme and lets the echo work (Huxley 1945; Perry 1971 [M]). Checker: the original put Huston Smith and Radhakrishnan in this group, which is a category mistake [M]. Smith's Forgotten Truth (1976) is an exposition of one metaphysical scheme and not an anthology, and Radhakrishnan's Gita (1948) is a translation with a commentary that reads the text through Advaita. Huxley is one anthologist among many (section 9), not the default. The equated terms are the anthologist's: Brahman, Godhead, the One, the Tao. Translators have often done the equating already: translators such as MacKenna and Waley render terms as "the One" and "the Way" with other traditions in their ears [U]. Checker: the renderings are as I recall them [M] (Waley's title is The Way and Its Power, 1934); "with other traditions in their ears" is a conjecture and not a finding.

The Traditionalist builds a metaphysical grid (Absolute, degrees of reality, form and essence) and reads each text through it; Coomaraswamy's "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (1944; Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3 [M, checker]) traces one doctrine through Indic, Platonic and Christian sources [details in G1-3]. Differences become differences of form or level.

The Theosophists assume an older Wisdom-Religion, concealed or corrupted. They cite Gita 2.22 as the standard reincarnation text (Judge, Besant) [U, checker lowered from [M]: I cannot place a passage in either]. Differences are loss and concealment.

Native comparers exist too: Cheng Yi's li yi fen shu (one and many), Ramakrishna's landing steps on one lake (the image is in the Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna as I recall it [M]; the grounding in Gita 4.11 and the contrast with 9.23 are the colleague's reading and not, as far as I know, Ramakrishna's own citation [U, checker]), Buddhist skilful means, Advaita's two standpoints.

Weak points recur: a metaphor lifted out of context (the elephant); terms equated that the traditions keep apart (Brahman, Dao, the One, sunyata; atman and anatta); texts read for what they do not claim (SN 12.48, MN 22, DN 16, Sutrakritanga 1).

A method that suits Alex: find the word that carries the claim in each passage (iva, tolma, amsa, svabhava, periagoge, buren), say what role it plays in its own argument, then compare roles. Plotinus says tolma, Brihadaranyaka fear, Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 and Shankara's gloss lila, Marcus discontent. Checker [M]: lila is in the sutra itself; Shankara adds the standpoint caveat.

## 5. What scholars say

Katz (1978) argues mystical experience is shaped by tradition; Forman (1990) argues for a "pure consciousness event" across traditions; Prothero (2010) argues the religions answer different questions. Olivelle treats rebirth and karma as late Upanishadic developments, Bronkhorst as non-Vedic in origin.

Gombrich (1996, 2009) reads the Buddha as answering Upanishadic ideas; Collins (1982) reads anatta as denying a permanent self. Dodds (1951) and Burkert (1972) trace Plato's soul lore to Orphic and Pythagorean sources. Graham, Hansen and Ziporyn differ on whether Zhuangzi 2 teaches one reality or perspectival freedom.
