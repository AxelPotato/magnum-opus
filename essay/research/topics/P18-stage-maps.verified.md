## 1. The picture in brief

Almost every tradition that describes a path describes it in steps, and the steps carry names that sound like distance: mansions, valleys, heavens, spheres, grounds, gates, rungs, worlds, planes. The comparers use these maps in four ways. Underhill (1911) lays Christian maps side by side and claims one human pattern. The perennialist and Traditionalist writers (Schuon, Nasr, Smith, Coomaraswamy, Guenon) treat the ladder of the soul and the ladder of the cosmos as one ladder seen from two sides. Wilber and Brown test it on meditation stages. The Theosophists build one numbered map from many sources.

Three things recur. The number of rungs varies (four, five, seven, nine, ten, fourteen, thirty, one hundred), and seven is frequent, which in Mediterranean and Near Eastern cases may come from the seven classical planets and not from a shared inner fact (the colleague's reasoning; it fits Hermes, Mithras, Hagigah and Dante and does not fit Teresa, the Jain fourteen or the Buddhist ten; C18). Most maps allow falls. And the end of the ladder is handled in four ways, each an interpretive grouping and not a doctrine of the traditions (C17): rest (Jain siddha, Theravada parinibbana as it is usually glossed), no end (Gregory of Nyssa, Ibn Arabi on one reading), return into action (bodhisattva, Teresa's seventh mansion, the tenth ox-herding picture, baqa on one reading, Theosophy's Nirmanakaya), and denial that there was ever a ladder (Gaudapada, sudden Zen, Wilber's "one taste").

Alex's arrow, with a stage called birth after plus four, fits the return family and the second-birth family. He can say honestly that the traditions disagree about what comes after the last rung.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Underhill's five stages
Claim: the mystic way has five stages: awakening of the self, purification, illumination, the dark night of the soul (which she also treats as surrender), union. Behind it is the older threefold way (purgative, illuminative, unitive) from Pseudo-Dionysius, with earlier forms in Origen and Evagrius; the Latin tags are scholastic (C6).
Who: Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism: A Study in the Nature and Development of Man's Spiritual Consciousness (London: Methuen, 1911), Part Two "The Mystic Way"; chapter numbers locator unknown. Do not confuse with her separate book The Mystic Way (London: Dent, 1913) (C6).
Words (paraphrase): the stages overlap and the soul can swing back. The unitive life is active, with Catherine of Siena, Teresa and Francis as models.
Cites: mostly Christian mystics, some Plotinus, Sufi and Hindu material.
Tier: [M] names and order; [U] her reading of the stages as the life of Christ re-enacted in the soul (possibly the 1913 book's thesis; C6).
Use: the one comparer with a short numbered ladder, a low point before the top, and an ending that is work in the world. A short witness that the top is not an exit. Overlaps P11.

### 2.2 Teresa's seven mansions and John of the Cross
Claim: Teresa of Avila, The Interior Castle (written 1577, first printed 1588; C4): a castle of one diamond or clear crystal, with seven groups of mansions (Spanish moradas, dwelling places; the English "mansions" echoes John 14:2 in the King James version). One, self-knowledge. Two, perseverance. Three, an orderly life that can go dry and proud. Four, the prayer of quiet, where she says the supernatural begins. Five, the prayer of union (the silkworm becomes a butterfly). Six, the betrothal, with raptures and trials. Seven, the spiritual marriage. She says the purpose of the marriage is the birth of good works (Seventh Mansions, chapter 4; the Spanish as I recall it ends "que nazcan siempre obras, obras", wording not cleared), and that Martha and Mary go together.
John of the Cross (the poem about 1578; the prose commentaries between about 1579 and 1585; C5): two nights, of the senses and of the spirit, each with an active and a passive side; beginners, proficients, the perfect; and in Dark Night Book II, chapters 19 and 20 (18 introduces the ladder) [M-], ten steps of the ladder of love, which John says he takes from Bernard and Aquinas.
Tier: [M] seven mansions and themes, ten steps; [M-] chapter numbers 19 and 20.
Use: the seventh mansion is a Christian text where arrival produces action and, in her wording, birth. It supports Alex's point that "birth" is a beginning in John and a state in Eckhart. Note the essay already cites Teresa without a date; if one is added, say "written 1577, printed 1588".

### 2.3 The ladder family
Claim: the ladder is the oldest cross-tradition image of staged ascent, and each version counts differently. Counts, all from memory:
Genesis 28:12 and 17, angels ascending and descending, the gate of heaven [M]. Plato, Symposium 210a to 212a, Diotima's ladder from one beautiful body to Beauty itself [M]. John Climacus, Ladder of Divine Ascent (about 600): thirty steps, the last four on stillness, prayer, dispassion, and faith, hope and love; the icon shows monks dragged off the ladder by demons [M]. Rule of Benedict ch. 7: twelve degrees of humility, introduced with Jacob's ladder [M]. Bonaventure, Itinerarium mentis in Deum (1259): six stages after the six wings of the seraph, then a seventh of rest [M]. Origen, Contra Celsum VI.22 (about 248), reporting Celsus on the Mithraic mysteries: a ladder with seven gates of seven metals tied to the planets, and an eighth gate above [M]. The seven Mithraic grades (Raven, Bridegroom, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-runner, Father) are NOT in Origen: they come from Jerome, Letter 107, and from the Santa Prisca and Felicissimus Mithraea; whether gates and grades were one scheme is debated (C1) [M-]. Corpus Hermeticum I (Poimandres): the soul climbs through the seven planetary zones, giving back one vice at each, reaches the eighth, and becomes divine [M]. Paul's "third heaven", 2 Corinthians 12:2 [M]. Hagigah 12b names seven heavens: Vilon, Raqia, Shehaqim, Zevul, Maon, Makhon, Aravot [M].
Tier: [M]; the order of the vices [U].
Use: one paragraph can call these a family. Climacus's falls match the Jain scheme (2.10). The Jewish ladder of Mesillat Yesharim is also worth a line (section 10).

### 2.4 Dante, the Mi'raj, and the scale of the heavens
Claim: Dante, Divina Commedia (about 1308 to 1320). Paradiso has ten heavens: Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the fixed stars, the Primum Mobile, the Empyrean. Purgatorio has seven terraces (pride, envy, wrath, sloth, avarice, gluttony, lust), Inferno nine circles. The Empyrean is outside space and time and has no "where" except the divine mind (around Par. XXVII, XXX) [M, lines U].
Words (paraphrase): in Paradiso I Beatrice says his rising should surprise him no more than a stream running down a mountain, and that the marvel would be if, free of hindrance, he stayed below, as a living flame would be if it stayed still on the ground [M, lines U: I.136 to 141]. In XXI, at Saturn, he sees a golden ladder going up past sight [M, XXI.25 to 30 U]. In XXVIII he sees a point of light ringed by nine circles of angels, and the circles nearest the point turn fastest, so speed rises with nearness to God [M, XXVIII.16 to 39 U]. In the last canto he sees the scattered leaves of the universe bound by love in one volume [M, XXXIII.85 to 87 U]. The nine angelic orders are Pseudo-Dionysius's, in three triads: Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones; Dominions, Virtues, Powers; Principalities, Archangels, Angels [M].
Cites: the Islamic Mi'raj is the other great graded journey: Muhammad ascends through seven heavens. The Qur'an gives the night journey (17:1) and the Lote tree vision (53:1 to 18); the seven heavens, one prophet in each, and Gabriel stopping at the Lote tree of the farthest boundary are from hadith and commentary (C2). The sequence in the hadith as I recall it: Adam; John and Jesus (two in the second heaven); Joseph; Idris; Aaron; Moses; Abraham [M; hadith numbers U]. Asin Palacios (1919) argued that Dante drew on the Mi'raj and Ibn Arabi; Cerulli (1949) added the Latin and French "Book of the Ladder" [M]. Most scholars call a direct source possible and unproven [M].
Tier: [M].
Use: the best cultural precedent for Alex's picture. The old maps were already scale maps with the planets as steps; his solar system, galaxy and universe are the modern version. Dante's natural rising and speed rising with nearness fit his speed and gravity picture. Check cantos before use.

### 2.5 Sufi stations and states, Ansari, Attar
Claim: Sufi writers separate stations (maqamat), won by effort and stable, from states (ahwal, singular hal), which are gifts and pass. Sarraj (d. 988), Kitab al-Luma: seven stations (repentance, scrupulousness, renunciation, poverty, patience, trust in God, contentment) [M] and ten states (watchfulness, nearness, love, fear, hope, longing, intimacy, tranquillity, contemplation, certainty) [U on the list and order]. Ansari of Herat (d. 1088 or 1089), Manazil al-Sa'irin: one hundred stations in ten sections of ten [M], section names [U] (see C8), ending in three grades of tawhid (of the common, the elect, the elect of the elect) [M].
Attar, The Conference of the Birds (about 1177): seven valleys, Quest, Love, Insight, Detachment, Unity, Bewilderment, Poverty and Annihilation [M]. Thirty birds arrive; "thirty birds" (si murgh) is the meaning of the king's name, the Simurgh, and they find that they are the one they sought [M]; the pun is Attar's wordplay, not the name's etymology (C8). Subsistence (baqa) follows annihilation.
Cites: Islamic only; survey: Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (1975) [M].
Use: Attar carries Alex's axiom in a story: many, a journey, a mirror, one. Valleys are a distance word. Thousands set out and thirty arrive. Baha'u'llah's Seven Valleys answers Attar (section 10).

### 2.6 Ibn Arabi, the four journeys, baqa
Claim: Ibn Arabi (1165 to 1240), al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya, 560 chapters in six parts (knowledge, practices, states, abodes, meetings or descents, stations) [M, parts U]. The highest rank is the station of no station (maqam la maqam), a person not fixed in any one station [M; Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge, 1989]. He also holds that the Real never shows itself twice in the same form, so no disclosure is last (the second half is an inference, C17) [M].
Mulla Sadra (died 1635 or 1640), The Four Journeys: from creation to the Real; in the Real with the Real; from the Real to creation with the Real; in creation with the Real [M]. The scheme is older than Sadra (C9) [U]. He taught that the soul keeps changing in substance after death [M; see part-2-notes].
Fana (passing away) and baqa (subsisting): baqa is usually read as return to life from the other side [M; P11]; one reading among others.
Use: the third and fourth journeys are the cleanest short formula for return as action. For "no last rung", pair Ibn Arabi with Nyssa (2.15).

### 2.7 Kabbalah
Claim: four worlds: Atzilut (emanation), Beriah (creation), Yetzirah (formation), Asiyah (action), the lower three tied to Isaiah 43:7 [M]. Ten sefirot: Keter, Chokhmah, Binah, Chesed, Gevurah, Tiferet, Netzach, Hod, Yesod, Malkhut, with Da'at sometimes counted for Keter [M]. Beyond them is Ein Sof, the Without End [M]. In Safed Kabbalah and Chabad the morning prayer is a climb through the four worlds [M]. Luria's scheme: withdrawal (tzimtzum), breaking of the vessels, repair (tikkun), in which scattered sparks are lifted back [M]. The Pardes story (Hagigah 14b): four entered the orchard, one died, one was harmed, one lost his faith, and Akiva entered and left in peace [M]. Survey: Scholem, Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism (1941), who notes Neoplatonic and Gnostic influence [M].
Tier: [M]; [U] when the four worlds became a fixed scheme.
Use: the Lurianic sparks are the closest Jewish match to one, separation, return. Pardes warns that ascent can hurt. Overlaps P14, P15.

### 2.8 Buddhist four stages, jhanas, 31 planes
Claim: the early path has four stages, each with a path moment and a fruit, so eight kinds of noble person: stream-enterer (sotapanna), once-returner (sakadagami), non-returner (anagami), arahant [M]. They are defined by the ten fetters. Stream entry cuts the first three (self-view, doubt, clinging to rites) and carries at most seven more lives; once-returner weakens desire and ill will; non-returner cuts them and is reborn in the Pure Abodes (five, the highest Akanittha) to finish there, though a non-returner may also finish in this life (C10); arahantship cuts desire for form, desire for the formless, conceit, restlessness and ignorance [M].
Jhanas: four form jhanas, four formless attainments (infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, neither perception nor non-perception), and the cessation of perception and feeling, nine abidings in all [M; Anupada Sutta, MN 111, locator U]. Cosmology has 31 planes: 11 sense-sphere, 16 form, 4 formless [M]. Sutras speak of thousandfold, thousand-thousandfold and thousand-thousand-thousandfold world systems, that is 1,000, 1,000,000 and 1,000,000,000 worlds [M, locator AN 3.80 U] (C10).
Use: the first formless rung is literally called infinite space, so a Buddhist stage name already is "space". The 1,000 by 1,000 world systems echo his zoom exactly as a number of worlds; present it as an echo, not a source.

### 2.9 The ten bhumis, and the bodhisattva who returns
Claim: ten grounds: Pramudita (Joyful), Vimala (Stainless), Prabhakari (Luminous), Arcismati (Radiant), Sudurjaya (Hard to Conquer), Abhimukhi (Facing), Duramgama (Far-going), Acala (Immovable), Sadhumati (Good Intelligence), Dharmamegha (Cloud of Dharma) [M]. Source: the Dasabhumika, part of the Avatamsaka Sutra and also circulating alone; Chandrakirti's Madhyamakavatara (7th century) is built on it [M]. They sit inside five paths (accumulation, preparation, seeing, meditation, no more learning), the first bhumi beginning at seeing [M]. Chinese schemes run to fifty-two stages, Tibetan lamrim (Atisha, Tsongkhapa) to three scopes of person [M].
Beyond the last: the vow to stay until all are free; non-abiding nirvana, where wisdom keeps one out of samsara and compassion keeps one out of nirvana [M]; the Heart Sutra mantra, gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond, awakening (Sanskrit "gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha"; earliest dated text Xuanzang's Chinese of 649; Nattier 1992 argues the Sanskrit is a back-translation) [M]. The ten ox-herding pictures (Kuoan, 12th century): seeking, tracks, first glimpse, catching, taming, riding home, ox forgotten, both forgotten (an empty circle), return to the source, entering the marketplace with open hands [M]. Earlier sets stopped at the empty circle, and the last pictures are the return [M].
Use: the tenth picture and non-abiding nirvana are the sharpest Buddhist answers to "what is after the last rung". Seventh bhumi is "far-going", a distance word. Shantideva: "born into the family of the Buddhas" is Bodhicaryavatara 3.25 [U numbering]; the stay-until-all-are-free vow is 10.55 in the numbering I recall [U] (C11).

### 2.10 Jain fourteen stages and where the freed soul goes
Claim: fourteen gunasthanas: 1 mithyatva (deluded view); 2 sasvadana (a taste of right view while falling); 3 misra (mixed); 4 avirata samyagdrishti (right view, no vows); 5 desavirata (partial vows, the householder); 6 pramatta-samyata (ascetic, lapsing); 7 apramatta-samyata (ascetic, vigilant); 8 apurvakarana (unprecedented purity); 9 anivrtti-karana; 10 suksma-samparaya (only subtle passions); 11 upasanta-kasaya (passions suppressed); 12 ksina-kasaya (passions destroyed); 13 sayogi-kevalin (omniscient, embodied, active); 14 ayogi-kevalin (activity stilled), then siddha [M; the English glosses are the colleague's]. A soul at stage 11 falls back because the passions were quieted and not destroyed; from 12 there is no return [M]. Sources: Shatkhandagama, Gommatasara, the Karmagranthas; survey: Jaini, The Jaina Path of Purification (1979) [M].
Where the freed soul goes: Tattvartha Sutra ch. 10, sutras 5 to 8 [U, my recall agrees, C12]: it rises straight to the end of the universe by its own nature, like a flame or a gourd freed of its clay, and stops because beyond is aloka, empty space with no medium of motion [M]. The cosmos is a person fourteen rajju tall [M]; a rajju is defined by a god flying at a stated speed for six months [M-, figure U].
Use: the clearest ancient match to gravity and escape velocity: karma as weight, release as natural ascent, a hard edge of the cosmos. It also shows that the Jain ladder ends in stillness at the boundary, not return. Jain souls stay many, so this is a witness to staged growth and not to the one whole (part-2-notes).

### 2.11 Yoga Vasistha, Patanjali, and the jivanmukta
Claim: Yoga Vasistha (Kashmir, built up over centuries, core perhaps 10th century [M-]): seven stages of knowledge: subheccha (good wish), vicarana (inquiry), tanumanasa (thinning of mind), sattvapatti (attaining clarity), asamsakti (non-attachment), padarthabhavana (non-perception of objects), turyaga (going to the fourth) [M]. The first three are described as still waking, the last four as the liberated; how they map to waking, dream, deep sleep and turiya varies by expositor [U]. It also lists seven stages of ignorance, so fourteen in two ladders [M; around sargas 117 and 118, U].
Patanjali: eight limbs (II.29: yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi); samadhi with seed (vitarka, vichara, ananda, asmita) and without (I.17 to 18, 41 to 51); a sevenfold last wisdom (II.27); the highest samadhi called dharma-megha (IV.29); kaivalya as the return of the gunas to their source (IV.34) [M].
The jivanmukta who stays: Advaita defends it (Vidyaranya, Jivanmuktiviveka, 14th century); Shankara allows that begun karma runs on until the body falls (locator U; candidate Brahma Sutra Bhashya 4.1.15, C13) [M]. The Gita says the realized person acts to hold the world together (III.20 to 25, Janaka as example) [M].
Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika II.32: from the highest standpoint there is no dissolution, no origination, no one bound, no seeker, no one freed [M, paraphrase].
Use: Gaudapada fits Alex's "treat the labels as labels". Patanjali's Yoga is dualist (purusha apart from prakriti), so equating kaivalya with Advaita liberation is the comparers' move, not Patanjali's.

### 2.12 Plotinus and Plato
Claim: Plotinus (Enneads, six groups of nine, edited by Porphyry about 301): three hypostases (the One, Intellect, Soul), matter as the lowest limit, procession out, conversion back [M]. The way up runs through the grades of virtue (civic, purificatory, then contemplative and exemplary in Porphyry, Sentences 32) and ends in the flight of the alone to the alone (VI.9.11, wording varies) [M]. Porphyry says Plotinus reached union four times in his presence and Porphyry once, at sixty-eight (Life 23) [M]. Plato: divided line, four grades (Republic VI, 509d to 511e); the cave, with the compelled return of the philosopher (VII, 514a to 520e); the place above the heavens (Phaedrus 247c) [M].
Use: Republic VII is the oldest "must go back down" text, a good partner to the bodhisattva. The Traditionalists equate the One with Brahman, Ein Sof and the Godhead. Scholars debate Indian influence on Plotinus [M].

### 2.13 Gurdjieff
Claim: seven categories of man. One, two and three are men of the moving-instinctive, emotional and intellectual centres, living by habit; four is balanced through work; five has a unified permanent I; six has objective consciousness, not yet permanent; seven has all a person can have [M; Ouspensky, In Search of the Miraculous, 1949, chapter U]. The ray of creation runs from the Absolute through all worlds, all suns, the Sun, all planets and the Earth to the Moon, with a number of laws at each step, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 [M, numbers U; my recall agrees, not raised]. The law of octaves says a seven-step process stalls at two intervals unless a shock is added [M].
Use: distance from the Absolute counts the steps, and a soul is made and not given. That differs from Alex's soul that continues; flag it. Man No. 4 is the middle of the seven and the first who is made and not born, a threshold scale (C18).

### 2.14 Theosophy: portals, two paths, initiations, planes
Claim: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (1889), three fragments: the Voice of the Silence, the Two Paths, the Seven Portals [M]. The portals are dana, shila, kshanti, viraga, virya, dhyana, prajna: the six Mahayana perfections with viraga (indifference) inserted [M]. The Two Paths set personal liberation against renunciation, whose master is a Nirmanakaya who stays to help [M]. The Secret Doctrine (1888) states as its third proposition the identity of all souls with the Over-Soul and the obligatory pilgrimage of every soul through the cycle of incarnation [M, paraphrase]. Seven principles of the human being (sthula sharira, prana, linga sharira, kama, manas, buddhi, atma), seven planes, seven rounds [M]. Besant and Leadbeater, then Alice Bailey, add initiations tied to the Christian life (Birth, Baptism, Transfiguration, Crucifixion or Renunciation, Resurrection) and, in Leadbeater, to the four Buddhist stages plus the Master [M first five, U rest]. The Christian link is in Besant's Esoteric Christianity (1901) and Leadbeater's The Masters and the Path (1925) [M-]; Bailey runs past five (C14). Bailey and Leadbeater set the human steps inside a stack of planetary and solar Logoi [M, detail U].
Tier: [M] portals, principles, two paths; [U] later initiations and the Logoi.
Use: the only existing map that puts human steps inside a cosmic hierarchy reaching the solar system and beyond, which is Alex's plus two to plus four. It is also where a "first birth" initiation appears. Caution: Buddhist scholars say the Theosophical Nirmanakaya and Pratyekabuddha are not the Buddhist ones; in the trikaya, nirmanakaya is the visible emanation body. Part one bans school vocabulary.

### 2.15 Birth, and what lies after it
Claim: Eckhart preaches the birth of the Son in the ground of the soul (main text the sermon on Wisdom 18:14, "while all things were in quiet silence", German Works no. 101 [U]), then a breakthrough (Durchbruch) into the Godhead beyond God, so the birth is not last (Sermon 52, "Blessed are the poor in spirit", where he prays God to rid him of God) [M]. John 3:3 and 3:7 use the Greek anothen, again or from above [M]. A dvija, twice-born, is one who has received the upanayana initiation [M]. Teresa's seventh mansion ends in the birth of works (2.2). Gregory of Nyssa, Life of Moses (390s), builds on Philippians 3:13 the idea of epektasis, a never-ending stretch toward God, so union does not stop the ascent [M]. Theosis (2 Peter 1:4) is the Orthodox word for the end [M].
Use: Alex's paragraph already says the traditions use the image differently. Add that Eckhart himself puts something after the birth. Epektasis is the best Christian authority for "even plus four may be a stage".

### 2.16 Aurobindo, Teilhard, Bucke
Claim: Aurobindo, The Life Divine (serialised 1914 to 1919, book 1939 to 1940): matter, life, mind, then above mind Higher Mind, Illumined Mind, Intuition, Overmind, Supermind [M]. Overmind still divides the aspects of the One into separate powers and Supermind sees them as one [M-]. The point is a descent that transforms life, not escape; the Mother announced on 29 February 1956 that the supramental had manifested [M]. Teilhard, The Phenomenon of Man (French 1955; English 1959): matter, life, mind, Omega, and union differentiates, so the end keeps persons [M]. Bucke, Cosmic Consciousness (1901): simple consciousness, self consciousness and cosmic consciousness, expected to become common, with Buddha, Jesus, Paul and Whitman among his examples [M, list U] (C15).
Use: the nearest precedents for a "new stage of evolution". Teilhard answers the worry that return means loss of self. Traditionalists reject all three (2.17).

### 2.17 The perennialist and Traditionalist comparers on levels
Claim: Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth: The Primordial Tradition (1976; later subtitle "The Common Vision of the World's Religions", 1992 [U]): four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite) matched to four of selfhood (body, mind, soul, spirit), the higher including the lower; he credits Schuon [M]. Schuon (The Transcendent Unity of Religions, French 1948, English 1953; Esoterism as Principle and as Way, French 1978, English 1981): degrees of Reality (Beyond-Being, Being, Existence), and for the Muslim path sharia, tariqa, haqiqa, matched with exoterism, initiation, gnosis [M, pages U]. Nasr, An Introduction to Islamic Cosmological Doctrines (1964) and Knowledge and the Sacred (1981): the cosmic hierarchy and the five Divine Presences (Hahut, Lahut, Jabarut, Malakut, Nasut) [M, naming M-]. Guenon, The Symbolism of the Cross (1931) and The Multiple States of the Being (1932): a vertical axis of degrees and a horizontal axis of range, crossing at the Universal Man [M]. Coomaraswamy: the sun as the single exit of the cosmos, in "Svayamatrnna: Janua Coeli" (1939, C3) and "The Sun-Kiss" (1940), with Mundaka Upanishad I.2.11 [M idea, U verse; my recall agrees on the verse, not raised]. Perry's Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971) is a themed anthology; stage chapters unknown [U]. Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945), is a thematic anthology with commentary, one popular voice in the movement and not its centre or its stage-mapper: no stage map of his own that I recall [U]. Where the movement's stage maps actually are: section 9.
Method: cosmos and person are one ladder, so distance words stand for degrees of reality, not kilometres [M].
Use: mapping stages onto space is a classical move, and Traditionalist readers will say "up" is symbolic. Guenon's cross, two axes meeting at a centre, is a coincidence with Alex's drawing, not a source. The strict school (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon, in good part Nasr) rejects evolution and reads history as decline, so "new stage of evolution" runs against it (Smith is softer, C16).

### 2.18 Wilber and Brown
Claim: Wilber, The Spectrum of Consciousness (1977): levels of identity from the whole universe down to persona and shadow, produced by drawing boundaries between self and not-self [M]. Later: matter, body, mind, soul, spirit; states of waking, dream, deep sleep, turiya and non-dual; the pre/trans fallacy, which warns against reading a return to unity as regression to infancy [M]. The non-dual is the wood the ladder is made of, not its top rung [M-]. Daniel P. Brown, "The Stages of Meditation in Cross-Cultural Perspective" (in Wilber, Engler and Brown, Transformations of Consciousness, 1986), compares Theravada, Mahamudra and Patanjali and argues for one deep structure under different surfaces [M].
Use: Wilber's boundary picture is Alex's axiom, one whole drawing lines inside itself. The pre/trans fallacy is a good guard sentence.

### 2.19 Daoism
Claim: inner alchemy is often given in four steps: essence into qi, qi into spirit, spirit into emptiness, emptiness into union with the Dao [M]. Zhuangzi ch. 1: the bird Peng rises ninety thousand li on a whirlwind while a cicada and a dove laugh, since the small cannot reach the great [M].
Use: the Peng is an old speed picture, useful beside run, bike, car, fly, orbit.

### 2.20 Beyond the last rung: four answers
Rest: Jain siddha; Theravada parinibbana as usually glossed; Yoga's kaivalya [M]. No end: Nyssa's epektasis; Ibn Arabi's no repetition (inference); Mulla Sadra [M]. Return: bodhisattva; tenth ox picture; jivanmukta and the Gita; Teresa's seventh mansion; baqa on one reading; Plato's cave; Theosophy's Nirmanakaya; Campbell's monomyth, seventeen stages in departure, initiation and return (his, 1949; Vogler's twelve is a screenwriting adaptation), with the refusal of the return as a stage [M, count M-] (C17). No ladder: Gaudapada; sudden Zen (the Shenxiu and Huineng verses, Platform Sutra; quote from a named edition, C17); Wilber's one taste [M].
Use: all four exist, and the teacher's "birth" belongs with return and new beginning.

## 4. How they compare: the method

Side by side by count and function. Underhill sets Teresa's seven, John's two nights and the old threefold way together and asks what each stage does to the self. The Theosophists map the four Buddhist stages onto initiations by position in the sequence. This strains for the Jain fourteen or the Sufi hundred.

By a shared term. Dharma-megha, at the end of both the tenth bhumi and Patanjali's last samadhi, is the best example. The Traditionalists do the same with Beyond-Being, the Godhead, Ein Sof, Brahman and the Tao.

By a shared structure. Campbell and the Neoplatonists use out, down, up and back. Wilber uses boundaries drawn in layers. Alex's axiom has the same shape, so the structural comparison is the one he wants.

By identifying the person's ladder with the cosmos's (Smith, Nasr), so the distance words are not measurements. By experience (James, Underhill, Brown), bracketing doctrine.

Differences are explained as temperament and vocabulary (Underhill, James), exoteric and esoteric layers (Schuon), or different surfaces over one deep structure (Brown). The critic Katz says they are real.

## 5. What scholars say

Katz (1978) argues that tradition shapes the experience from the start, so cross-tradition stage maps may match the writers' vocabulary and not events. Forman (1990) answers with contentless events. Zaehner says there are at least three kinds. Ferrer, Revisioning Transpersonal Theory (2002), says Wilber-style hierarchies assume one summit and argues for plural ends [M].

Repeated sevens are weak evidence of a shared inner structure, since planets and calendar explain them [M, the colleague's observation].

Theosophy: Hanegraaff and Godwin read it as part of the history of Western esotericism, a 19th-century synthesis; Lopez, Prisoners of Shangri-La (1998), is critical of how it reshapes Tibetan Buddhist terms; Johnson, The Masters Revealed (1994), argues the Masters were identifiable historical persons (C19) [M-]. Traditionalism: Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), places it historically [M]. Dante and the Mi'raj: possible, unproven (2.4).

## 9. The perennial philosophy movement beyond Huxley, as it bears on stage maps (added on Alex's steer)

The phrase: Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540); Leibniz, in a letter of 1714; Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945) [from P01's first pass for Steuco and Leibniz; [M] for Huxley's date; Huxley wrongly credits Leibniz with coining it, as the repo's part-2-notes says]. Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947), Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), Time and Eternity (1947), with essays gathered in Selected Papers (ed. Lipsey, 1977); Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998), The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953), Esoterism as Principle and as Way (1978, English 1981), Stations of Wisdom (French 1958, English 1961 [U]); Titus Burckhardt (1908 to 1984), Introduction to Sufi Doctrine (1959) and Mystical Astrology according to Ibn Arabi (French 1974, English 1977 [U]), which treats Ibn Arabi's planetary heavens as stages; Martin Lings (1909 to 2005), What Is Sufism? (1975); Marco Pallis (1895 to 1989), The Way and the Mountain (1960), A Buddhist Spectrum (1980).

The later authors and what they are for. Whitall Perry (1920 to 2005), A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a large anthology of passages arranged by theme [publisher and foreword U]. Huston Smith (1919 to 2016), Forgotten Truth (1976), the readable map of levels of reality and of selfhood. Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), Knowledge and the Sacred (Gifford Lectures 1981) and An Introduction to Islamic Cosmological Doctrines (1964), the cosmic hierarchy. Reza Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence (2006), Shankara, Ibn Arabi and Eckhart side by side from the school's side. About the school from outside: Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004); Harry Oldmeadow, Traditionalism (2000).

The experience and psychology line, outside the strict school: William James (1902), Underhill (1911), Walter Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy and The Teachings of the Mystics (both 1960), F. C. Happold, Mysticism (Penguin, 1963), Charles Tart (ed.), Transpersonal Psychologies (1975), Daniel Goleman, The Varieties of the Meditative Experience (1977; reissued as The Meditative Mind, 1988), which lines up meditation stage maps from several traditions [list of traditions U], Wilber (1977) and Wilber, Engler and Brown (1986). Zaehner (1957) and Katz (1978) are its best known academic critics.

What each is for, if the question is "who lays out stages". Underhill: the one numbered ladder from inside Christianity (2.1). Smith: four levels of reality and of selfhood, readable and systematic. Schuon and Nasr: degrees of Reality and the cosmic hierarchy, with sharia, tariqa and haqiqa as a three-step path in Islam. Guenon: many states of being and a vertical axis of degrees. Coomaraswamy: the sun door as the exit of the cosmos. Burckhardt: the planetary heavens in Ibn Arabi, the nearest Traditionalist treatment of "heavens as stages". Stace, Happold, Goleman, Wilber and Brown: stage maps from the side of experience and practice. Huxley: a thematic anthology; chapters follow subjects (love, silence, grace, time and eternity), not rungs; one voice among these, and for stages the weakest of the sources to cite (my reading, [U]).

How the strict school and the experience line differ, for an essay that wants both. The school says the criterion is doctrine and intellectual intuition, not experience, and that the traditions agree at the level of metaphysics while differing as forms. The experience line asks whether mystics' reports agree and why. An essay can set a doctrine voice (Schuon, Coomaraswamy, Smith) beside an experience voice (James, Underhill, Stace) and say it is doing so. Note that Guenon wrote against the Theosophical Society (Le Theosophisme, 1921) and rejected modern reincarnation, which matters for an essay that cites both movements as allies (P09, P02).

A suggestion, my reading and not a fact: if Part two's skeleton is meant to lean on the movement and not on one anthologist, Smith's four levels (for the arrow) and Schuon's exoteric against esoteric (for "same things in different words") are the movement's own frames, and either could stand beside or in place of Huxley's three-part definition; the repo's notes say that definition is verified only through secondary quotations.

## 10. Stage maps the colleague missed (added; all from recall)

1. Jewish ladder of rungs. Moshe Chaim Luzzatto (Ramchal), Mesillat Yesharim (1740), built on the baraita of Rabbi Pinchas ben Yair (Avodah Zarah 20b; a shorter, differently ordered form in Mishnah Sotah 9:15 [U]): Torah leads to watchfulness, zeal, cleanliness, separation, purity, piety, humility, fear of sin, holiness, the holy spirit, and the revival of the dead [M; order U]. A ladder whose last rung is resurrection, a return. Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed III.51: the parable of the king's palace, where people stand at different distances from the king, from outside the city to the inner chamber [M]; another distance image.
2. Sikh: the five khands of Japji (pauris 34 to 37): Dharam (duty), Gian (knowledge), Saram (effort), Karam (grace), Sach (truth) [M; English glosses vary]. P16 has Japji pauri 4 for rebirth.
3. Baha'i: Baha'u'llah's Seven Valleys (about 1860, Baghdad): Search, Love, Knowledge, Unity, Contentment, Wonderment, True Poverty and Absolute Nothingness [M; the English names vary by translation]. It answers Attar's scheme.
4. Vedanta: the five sheaths (Taittiriya Upanishad II): food, breath, mind, understanding, bliss, a ladder of selves and the closest Indian match to Smith's levels of selfhood [M]. The seven upper lokas (Bhu, Bhuvar, Svar, Mahar, Jana, Tapas, Satya) and seven lower are a Hindu "worlds as steps" scale [M].
5. Buddhist nine-step scales (C18): the nine stages of calm abiding (Tibetan manuals, from the Madhyantavibhaga tradition and Kamalashila) and the nine vehicles of the Nyingma school ending in Dzogchen [M].
6. Bernard of Clairvaux, On Loving God (De diligendo Deo): four degrees of love, from loving oneself for oneself to loving oneself for God's sake, the last rarely reached in this life [M]. John of the Cross says he takes his ten steps from Bernard and Aquinas (2.2).
7. Rumi, Masnavi III, about lines 3901 to 3906 (Nicholson's numbering as I recall [U]): the poem of dying as mineral, becoming plant, animal, human, then angel, then passing beyond the angel to "what no mind has conceived". It is a stage map with something after the top rung. It is often read as Darwinian evolution; the older reading is the arc of descent and ascent of the Islamic Neoplatonic cosmos [M]. Quote only from a named translation.
8. Hawkins, Power vs. Force (1995): a popular scale of levels of consciousness with a threshold at 200; its measuring method (muscle testing) is not accepted by scientists. Mentioned only because a reader may bring it up; not a tradition, not a witness [M].

## 11. Traps for this slice (added)

Quotations and attributions that circulate wrongly, or that a careless essay could get wrong. None was checked by search; each is from recall, [M] unless marked.

1. "Mysticism is the art of union with Reality" is Underhill's Practical Mysticism (1914) as I recall it, not the 1911 Mysticism (P11 has the same note).
2. Underhill's Mysticism, Part Two, is titled "The Mystic Way"; her 1913 book of that title is a different work (C6).
3. "Christ has no body now but yours" is widely credited to Teresa of Avila; I know of no source for it in her works and treat it as unsourced [U].
4. The Mithraic grades are Jerome's, not Origen's (C1).
5. The Mi'raj heavens are hadith, not Qur'an 17:1, and the second heaven has two prophets (C2).
6. "Eckhart: if the only prayer you say is thank you" has no source I know and is unsourced [U] (P11 C9); "the eye with which I see God" is genuine (P11).
7. Eckhart "asked God to rid him of God" is Sermon 52, "Beati pauperes spiritu"; the wording differs by translation (Walshe: "free of God") [M].
8. The Simurgh pun is Attar's, not the name's etymology (C8).
9. Huineng's "originally there is not a single thing" is from later editions of the Platform Sutra, not the Dunhuang text (C17).
10. The Heart Sutra's Sanskrit may be a back-translation from Chinese (C11).
11. Campbell's monomyth has seventeen stages; the twelve-stage version is Vogler's (C17).
12. Rumi's "mineral, plant, animal" poem is not a statement of Darwinian evolution (section 10, item 7).
13. Huxley did not coin "perennial philosophy" and wrongly credits Leibniz (Steuco, 1540; P01, P08).
14. "Seven is the number of rungs everywhere" is false: the numbers range from four to a hundred (section 1), and the planetary explanation covers only some of the sevens (C18).
