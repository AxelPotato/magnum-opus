## 1. The picture in brief

Both weak points have long Western histories, and the histories are better than the essay's current one-line answers. Huxley is a late voice in the one that concerns the brain, and the philosophical weight sits with James, Schiller, Bergson and Broad.

On the opening view, the older reply is a logical point and not a counter-fact. That mind and brain rise and fall together shows dependence. It does not show production. William James made this exact in 1898: a brain can produce thought, release it, or transmit it. Schiller (1891), Myers (1903), Bergson (1896 to 1913) and Broad (1925, 1953) restated it, Huxley's reducing valve (1954) made it famous, and Kelly and colleagues (2007) and Kastrup (2014 to 2019) revived it. Neuroscience grants that the brain restricts and denies that a Mind at Large sits behind it. The theory is logically open and has no decisive positive evidence, and that is the honest sentence for Alex.

On plural lives, the Western case is a set of reasons: the unequal start, the unfinished task, the prodigy, love, soul-making, and the symmetry of before and after. Plato supplies most of them. Origen adds pre-existence with a just God. Lessing, Kant, Goethe and Schopenhauer carry them through the Enlightenment and the generation after it, and Hume states the symmetry between before and after, though as a challenge to believers in survival and not as a case for return. French Spiritists and the Theosophists turn them into a rule between 1857 and the 1880s. McTaggart, Ducasse, Price, Weatherhead, MacGregor and Hick give the twentieth century versions.

S2 is half right that the dilemma is Lessing's and Hick's, and the "half" is smaller than the colleague first thought. The bad-designer form proper (a good maker, an unequal start) has earlier homes: Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36, Plato's blameless god (Timaeus 42d, Republic 617e) and Origen on the diversity of birth. Gita 6.37 to 45, Kant 1788 and Lessing 1780 belong to a second argument, that the road is longer than one life. Hick joins the two. There is also a third horn that Alex half states himself: growth continues without return (purgatory, Gregory of Nyssa, Hick's other worlds). The safe form is "the run is longer than one life, and the traditions differ on where the rest happens".

### A. Filter and transmission theories

1. James's three functions. Claim: the brain's dependence on mind can be productive (a kettle makes steam), permissive or releasing (a trigger frees a stored force), or transmissive (a lens, prism, stained glass or organ key lets something through). Only the first gives the materialist conclusion. Who: William James, Human Immortality: Two Supposed Objections to the Doctrine, Ingersoll Lecture, Harvard, given 1897, published 1898 (Houghton Mifflin), second edition with preface 1899; public domain; chapter locator unknown. Words: paraphrase; the images are recalled and need checking. He also cites Shelley's dome of many-coloured glass staining the white radiance of eternity (Adonais, stanza 52) [M, wording unchecked]. As I recall, the lecture's second supposed objection concerns the great number of souls that immortality would collect, which would give an 1898 reply to the population objection of finding 23 [U]. Parallels: little religious comparison here; Kant's transcendental self as ancestor; Fechner later in A Pluralistic Universe (1909, the chapter on Fechner). Tier: [M]. Use: the cheapest honest fix for the opening view. Quote after checking the text on a public-domain host. It restores logical room and adds no evidence, and the essay should say so.

2. Schiller and Myers, the first English statements. Claim, Schiller: matter limits and confines consciousness and does not generate it, so the body is an instrument of restriction. Who: F. C. S. Schiller, Riddles of the Sphinx (1891), published anonymously as by "a Troglodyte" (subtitle, as I recall, "A Study in the Philosophy of Evolution") and revised under his name in 1910 as "A Study in the Philosophy of Humanism" [M]; I do not trust my wording of the limiting sentence, and I think James credited it in his 1899 preface [U]. Myers, Human Personality and Its Survival of Bodily Death, two volumes, 1903, posthumous (he died in 1901) [M]. Tier: [M]; [U] for what Myers said about reincarnation (I think he left it open). Use: Schiller's "limitation" is close to Alex's "wall". The lineage runs James, Schiller, Myers, Bergson, Broad, then Huxley.

3. Bergson. Claim: the brain selects from a memory that lies beyond it and is the organ of attention to life, so damage blocks recall without destroying memory; the mind overflows the brain; survival is at least possible. Who: Matter and Memory (1896); "Brain and Thought: A Philosophical Illusion" (1904, in Mind-Energy, tr. Carr, 1920); "Soul and Body" (1912); the Presidential address to the Society for Psychical Research (1913). Words: paraphrase; the coat and nail image (pull the nail and the coat falls, yet the nail is not the coat) and the brain as an organ of pantomime are recalled, and which essay holds each is [U]. 4. Broad and Huxley's valve. Claim: the brain, nerves and senses mainly eliminate and do not produce; each person could in principle remember everything and perceive everything, and the system cuts this to what is useful for survival. Who: C. D. Broad, "Some Elementary Reflexions on Sense-Perception", Philosophy 27 (1952), reprinted in Religion, Philosophy and Psychical Research (1953); earlier the compound theory (a mind as brain plus a "psychic factor") in The Mind and Its Place in Nature (1925). Aldous Huxley, The Doors of Perception (1954), quotes Broad, takes "Mind at Large" from him, and adds the reducing valve; Broad took the idea from Bergson [M]. Whether Huxley also names Schiller or James is [U]; I think it is Broad who names them in the 1952 paper [U]. Huxley equates the mescaline experience with Eckhart's Istigkeit and the Buddhist Dharma-Body [M]. Use: the valve is a Broad-Bergson idea in Huxley's prose.

5. Modern restatements. Claim: Kelly and colleagues present Myers and James as the basis for a mind-first psychology. Who: Edward Kelly and others, Irreducible Mind (2007); Beyond Physicalism (2015) [M]. Claim, Kastrup: one universal mind; individual minds are dissociated parts, like alters in dissociative identity disorder; the brain is what dissociation looks like from outside, closer to an image of a mind than to a filter. Who: Bernardo Kastrup, Why Materialism Is Baloney (2014), Brief Peeks Beyond (2015), The Idea of the World (2019) [M]. Use: Kastrup's picture is the nearest academic neighbour of Alex's axiom. The standard objection is how one mind divides into many, the boundary or decombination problem (Chalmers, "Idealism and the Mind-Body Problem", 2019, in the Routledge Handbook of Panpsychism [M]). Priority monism (Schaffer, Philosophical Review, 2010 [M]) and cosmopsychism (Shani, Philosophical Papers, 2015 [U]; my recall of the journal agrees, the tier stays) are the analytic names for the axiom.

6. The older forms of the same picture. Plato: the body as tomb or prison (Phaedo 62b, where the word phroura can also be read as "guard post"; Cratylus 400c). Plotinus: the soul does not wholly descend, a higher part stays above (Enneads IV.8.8). Advaita: limiting adjuncts (upadhi) make the one seem many, as the space in a pot seems cut off from all space (ghatakasa, Gaudapada's Karika, chapter 3). Averroes: one shared intellect for all humans, attacked by Aquinas in De unitate intellectus (1270). Fechner: life before birth, on earth and after death as three stages, as the womb is to the world (Das Buechlein vom Leben nach dem Tode, 1836). All [M]. Use: the pot-space image is the old Indian filter theory and fits the essay's machine. Fechner's stages echo the child in the dark.

7. Objections from neuroscience and philosophy. (a) Dependence is fine-grained: lesions, stimulation, anaesthesia, dementia. Filter theorists reply that a damaged transmitter looks the same, and critics answer that then no brain result could ever count against the theory (Edwards 1996; Augustine, in Martin and Augustine, eds., The Myth of an Afterlife, 2015). (b) Memory: engram work (Liu et al., Nature 2012; Ryan et al., Science 2015) finds cell groups whose activation recalls a memory, even one lost to amnesia, and both sides use it. (c) Dying brains: a gamma surge after cardiac arrest in rats (Borjigin, PNAS 2013), with human reports later (Vicente et al., Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience 2022, one patient; Xu, Borjigin and others, PNAS 2023, a few patients) [M on the existence, U on the counts]. (d) Terminal lucidity in dementia (Nahm and Greyson, 2009; fuller review Nahm and others, 2012) is the filter side's best anomaly, and the cases are retrospective. (e) Physics: Sean Carroll argues that any signal into the brain must come through known fields, which leave no room (2011 blog post; journal version "Consciousness and the Laws of Physics", Journal of Consciousness Studies, 2021 [M, about 85 percent]). (f) The radio analogy has a transmitter we can find, and for mind there is none. (g) Disembodied persons are hard to make sense of (Strawson, Individuals, 1959; Flew, The Logic of Mortality, 1987). Tier [M] unless marked.

8. What neuroscience already grants. Thalamic gating, attention and predictive processing all describe the brain as a reducer of input. Psilocybin lowers activity in some regions (Carhart-Harris and others, PNAS 2012), which filter writers cite, and its own authors read in naturalistic terms (the REBUS model, Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019) [M]. So the valve metaphor holds as a description of the brain, and the extra claim is only that what is reduced is a pre-existing Mind at Large. Tier: [M]. Use: give the reader the two claims separately. The first is easy to grant and the second carries the weight.

### B. The Western case for plural lives

9. Plato. Meno 81: the soul has been born many times and has seen everything, so learning is recollection. Phaedo 70 to 77: opposites come from opposites (the living from the dead), and knowledge before experience shows pre-existence. Phaedrus 245 to 256: ten thousand years for the soul to regain its wings (three lives of a thousand years for the philosopher, 248e to 249b), and love as recognition of a beauty seen before birth. Timaeus 41e to 42d: the maker gave every soul the same first birth so that none would be disfavoured, and so is blameless for later evil. Republic 617e and 621: the chooser is responsible and god is blameless, and the souls drink forgetfulness. Tier [M]. Use: the oldest "not a bad designer" in the West. The designer is cleared by an equal start, and the unequal present is explained by earlier choices.

10. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa. Origen, De Principiis (c. 220 to 230), argues that the diversity of birth conditions (Greek or barbarian, rich or poor) would make God unjust unless souls pre-existed and earned their places (I.5 to 6, II.9) [M, locators U]. He also speaks of worlds before and after this one (II.3) and rejects transmigration. The work survives mostly in Rufinus's Latin (about 397 to 398), which softened some passages, so take any locator and wording from a critical translation [M]. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380) argues that souls unpurified at death are purified after it, because God's purpose cannot fail (On the Soul and the Resurrection), and taught endless progress, epektasis [M]. The Church defined purgatory at Lyon II (1274) and Florence (1439) [M]. Use: the Latin and Greek Christian solutions are purification and more time after death without return, which is the third horn. Do not repeat the Council of 553 story (P16).

11. The Indian receipts for the dilemma. Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36 (the section on partiality and cruelty): God is not partial or cruel, because creation is regulated by the souls' own earlier deeds, and since creation has no beginning the regress is no objection. Shankara's image is rain, a common cause of grain whose kinds differ by seed [M; numbering to within one sutra, and my recall agrees on 2.1.33 lila, 2.1.34 partiality and cruelty, 2.1.35 the objection from beginninglessness, 2.1.36 reasonable and found in scripture]. The essay already cites 2.1.33 (lila), so the next three sutras are adjacent. Gita 6.37 to 45: Arjuna asks what becomes of the seeker who fails, and Krishna answers that he is not lost, is born again, regains his earlier practice, and is perfected through many births [M]. This is the unfinished-road argument and not the dilemma. Majjhima 135, the Culakammavibhanga Sutta: a young man asks why humans differ in health, looks, wealth and wisdom, and the Buddha answers by action [M]. Weber called the karma doctrine the most consistent theodicy in history [M]. Use: these are the best receipts for the unfinished process and the unequal start. Check the sutra numbers in Thibaut or Gambhirananda.

12. Hume. "Of the Immortality of the Soul" (written about 1755, withdrawn in 1757 from the Five Dissertations, printed in 1777 after his death) argues against survival from the dependence of mind on body. In the part on metaphysical arguments he says that if the soul is immortal it must have existed before birth, since what cannot perish cannot have begun, and that metempsychosis is therefore the only scheme of its kind that philosophy could listen to (recalled wording, do not print; check the text) [M]. He does not endorse it. His point is that believers in survival owe an account of the soul before birth, and that we have no memory or other sign of one. Use: a strict sceptic saw that survival after death and existence before birth stand or fall together. Cite him for the symmetry and not as a supporter of return.

14. Kant. The planets of 1755 are a precedent for Alex's arrow mapped on space travel, as are Swedenborg's Earths in the Universe (1758) and Flammarion [M].

## 4. How they compare: the method

The filter authors rarely compare religions. They argue a gap in logic with a family of functional images (kettle, trigger, lens, valve, organ key, nail and coat), press with anomalies (mediumship, hypnosis, genius, dying), and then call mystics as witnesses to a wider self: James in the Varieties (the "More"), Huxley on Eckhart and the Buddha, Bergson in 1932 with a ranking that puts Christian action and love on top. The comparing comes last.

The lives authors use six moves. (1) Reason first: justice, development, love, the unfinished task, with traditions as later confirmation (Lessing, Kant, McTaggart, Hick). (2) The oldest-idea argument: Lessing treats the idea's age and spread as a sign that it fits the human understanding, Hume treats the symmetry as a challenge to believers in survival, Schopenhauer calls it the natural belief of Asia, T. H. Huxley ties it to heredity. (3) The catalogue: Walker and Head and Cranston sort short quotations by thinker and religion, which shows spread and hides context. (4) Home reinterpretation: Weatherhead and MacGregor read it into Christian terms (purgatory, sanctification, Elijah and John). (5) The word-equation: Theosophists match devachan with a Pure Land, karma with Nemesis. (6) Stages: Lessing explains the religions as stages in the education of the species, Kardec as grades of worlds, Schopenhauer explains rebirth against migration as truth against myth, and Hick as one Real met through different filters (An Interpretation of Religion, 1989).

## 5. What scholars say

Filter theory: philosophers call it coherent and not forced by any evidence, neuroscientists treat dependence as production, physicists such as Carroll find no room for the extra channel, and a minority (Kelly, Kastrup, Braude) keep it alive. Lessing: read mostly as philosophy of history, with the rebirth paragraphs tentative. The Theosophical rule: Chajes shows a Western and Spiritist history, which cuts against the story of an Eastern import. Stevenson: respected for care, criticised for retrospective testimony and cultural setting; the strongest cases have records made before verification (Keil and Tucker). Hick: the soul-making theodicy is the standard Irenaean version, criticised for the amount of suffering. Edwards: the main sceptical book, itself accused of a hostile reading of Stevenson. For history, Bremmer, The Rise and Fall of the Afterlife (2002), Segal, Life After Death (2004), and Obeyesekere, Imagining Karma (2002; subtitle Ethical Transformation in Amerindian, Buddhist, and Greek Rebirth), which finds similar ethical rebirth eschatologies arising in several regions and argues for independent parallels more than for one diffusion [all M].

## 9. Beyond Huxley: the wider perennial shelf, for the topic of this slice (new, added for Alex's remark)

9.1 The headline for the essay. The perennialists do not agree on lives. The Traditionalist school (Guenon and those who followed him) mostly rejects reincarnation as the West understands it, and attacked the Theosophy that Alex also leans on. The Theosophists, the other comparing movement, made return a rule in the early 1880s. So the two movements Alex relies on split on exactly the lives passage. A reader who knows only Huxley will not see that; a reader who knows Guenon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon will, and will count it against an essay that writes "the perennial philosophy teaches lifetimes".
