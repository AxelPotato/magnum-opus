## 1. The picture in brief

The comparers argue for an ethic in two different ways, and the difference matters for Alex's fingers.

The first is reciprocity: treat others as you want to be treated. Hillel, Confucius (Analects 15.24), Matthew 7:12, the Mahabharata, the Udanavarga, the hadith and the Jain verse all have it. It needs no metaphysics, which is why it appears almost everywhere, and why Hans Kueng's 1993 Declaration and Karen Armstrong's 2009 Charter can use it as a common floor.

The second is identity: the other is you, so harming them harms you. Isha Upanishad 6 and 7, Gita 6.29 and 13.28, Paul's members of one another, Matthew 25:40, Shantideva's limbs and the Neo-Confucian one body are of this kind. This is what the perennialists argue for. Schopenhauer made it the foundation of morals in 1840. Vivekananda and Deussen follow from the Indian side, Schuon and probably Coomaraswamy from the Traditionalist side, and Huxley's Charity chapter makes the same move as the third leg of his definition. In this reading the golden rule is the visible shadow of the metaphysics.

Those who draw the line between the two: Kant (rejects the rule as trivial), Schopenhauer (reciprocity is egoism in better clothes), Ricoeur (the rule is a hinge between exchange and gift), the rabbis Akiva and Ben Azzai (love your neighbour versus common descent from Adam), Shankara on Gita 6.32, and Wattles (unverified). Kueng and Armstrong do not, because they want a floor everyone can stand on. Critics of the identity route: Levinas, Buber, Zaehner, Nietzsche.

For Alex: the fingers sit on the identity rung. The draft already says most rest on reciprocity or empathy and a few go further. Below is material to turn that into a ladder of three rungs (reciprocity, analogy, identity), each with citations.

## 2. Findings

1. Schopenhauer: compassion as the one moral motive, grounded in tat tvam asi.
Claim: Morality is compassion (Mitleid), not duty or reciprocity. Compassion is explained by metaphysics: individuality is appearance (the principium individuationis, which he equates with maya), and the compassionate person has in practice seen through it. His content formula is to harm no one and help everyone as far as you can (neminem laede, imo omnes, quantum potes, juva). Two cardinal virtues: justice and loving-kindness. He holds that the golden rule and Kant's imperative smuggle in self-interest, since I agree to them by picturing myself on the receiving end.
Who: Ueber die Grundlage der Moral (written 1839 for the Danish Royal Society, which gave no prize; published 1840 in Die beiden Grundprobleme der Ethik). Payne translation, On the Basis of Morality (Berghahn, 1995, Cartwright introduction). The tat tvam asi passage: section 22, locator [U]. Fuller scene: World as Will and Representation vol. 1, section 66.
Traditions: Hindu (via Anquetil-Duperron's Oupnek'hat), Buddhist, Kant as foil.
Tier: [M] for argument and work; section number [U].
Use: the oldest Western statement of Alex's step; public domain in older translations. Add his own ending: his ethics leads to denial of the will, which is not return (P01 lead 5).

2. Neo-Vedanta takes the step: Deussen, Vivekananda, Radhakrishnan, Rambachan.
Claim: Paul Deussen (Schopenhauer's disciple, 1845 to 1919) took tat tvam asi as the Upanishadic basis of morals. Vivekananda argued that only Vedanta answers why to be unselfish: because the Self is one, while utilitarian and reciprocity grounds leave the question open. Radhakrishnan, An Idealist View of Life (1932), argues similarly. Anantanand Rambachan, The Advaita Worldview (SUNY, 2006), argues from inside Advaita that non-duality grounds ethics and social concern, against the charge that a world called illusion cannot motivate action.
Who: Deussen, Das System des Vedanta (1883), Die Philosophie der Upanishad's (1899); Vivekananda, Complete Works, Karma-Yoga and Practical Vedanta lectures (titles [U]).
Tier: [M] for claim and authors; [U] for exact lectures and chapters.

7. Hans Kueng and the 1993 Declaration toward a Global Ethic.
Claim: Kueng (1928 to 2021, Catholic theologian, Tuebingen) drafted it for the Parliament of the World's Religions, Chicago, 28 August to 4 September 1993; signed 4 September by well over a hundred leaders (143 to 200 in my memory [U]). Two principles: every human being must be treated humanely, and the golden rule, negative and positive form, called the irrevocable, unconditional norm for all areas of life. Four directives follow: non-violence and respect for life, solidarity and a fair economy, tolerance and truthfulness, equal partnership of women and men. It says we are interdependent. An endnote lists the rule across traditions: Analects 15.23, Hillel (Shabbat 31a), Matthew 7:12 and Luke 6:31, Nawawi's Forty Hadith 13, Sutrakritanga I.11.33, Samyutta Nikaya S V 353, Mahabharata XIII 114.8 [M that the list exists; numbers medium].
Who: Kueng and Kuschel (eds.), Erklaerung zum Weltethos (Piper, 1993); A Global Ethic (SCM, 1993). Earlier, Kueng, Projekt Weltethos (1990).
Tier: [M].
Use: Kueng seeks a minimal consensus on practice and avoids a shared metaphysics on purpose. That makes it the best foil: the rule is shared without the axiom, and the axiom is a candidate for why. Its ground is interdependence, weaker than oneness. P01 (finding 14, [V] there) reports that the 1893 Parliament's stated aim included making the golden rule the basis of union.

10. Judaism: Hillel, Akiva, Ben Azzai, Tanhuma.
Claim: Shabbat 31a: a would-be convert asks for the whole Torah on one foot; Hillel says what is hateful to you do not do to your fellow, that is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary. That is the negative form and a reciprocity rule. The better find is the rabbinic argument about the same verse. Sifra, Kedoshim 4:12, has Akiva call love your neighbour as yourself the great principle of the Torah; Ben Azzai answers that this is the book of the generations of Adam (Genesis 5:1) is greater, since all descend from one man. Genesis Rabbah 24:7 adds Rabbi Tanhuma: if you shame your neighbour, know whom you shame, since he was made in God's likeness. Tanya ch. 32 (in the draft) grounds love of the fellow Jew in the common soul root. Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5: whoever destroys one life destroys a world, paralleled in Quran 5:32.
Tier: [M]; Sifra and Genesis Rabbah locators medium.
Use: Akiva against Ben Azzai is an ancient version of the reciprocity versus unity split, inside one tradition. Tanhuma gives a one-line identity argument.

11. Confucius and the Confucians: shu, and one body.
Claim: Analects 15.24 (15.23 in Legge): Zigong asks for one word to act on for life; the Master says shu (reciprocity) and gives the negative rule. 12.2 repeats it. 6.30 gives the positive form and calls taking what is near as an analogy the method of ren. 4.15: the Master's way is one thread, which Zengzi calls loyalty (zhong) and shu. All reciprocity or analogy. The identity rung comes later. Cheng Hao (11th century): the person of ren forms one body with heaven, earth and the ten thousand things. Zhang Zai's Western Inscription: heaven is my father, earth my mother, all people my siblings. Wang Yangming, Inquiry on the Great Learning (1527): the great person sees all things as one body, and the proof is Mencius 2A6, the child about to fall into a well, felt before any calculation.
Tier: [M]; Analects numbers high confidence; Neo-Confucians by name only.
Use: shu for the first rung; Wang Yangming and the child at the well for the last, from a tradition outside the Indian and Abrahamic ones. Specialists note that Confucian one body is graded love, close people first, and not numerical identity (Mencius rejected Mozi's equal love).

12. Hindu texts: reciprocity, analogy and identity in one book.
Claim: Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva 13.113.8 (CE numbering; 13.114.8 in the Vulgate numbering the Declaration uses): do not do to others what would harm you; this is the whole of dharma, the rest is desire [M, number medium]. Gita 6.29: sees the Self in all beings and all in the Self. 6.30: sees me everywhere. 6.31: established in oneness worships me in all beings. 6.32: sees sameness everywhere by analogy to the self (atmaupamyena), in pleasure and in pain. 13.28: seeing the Lord equally present everywhere, he does not harm the self by the self and reaches the highest goal. Also 5.25 (intent on the welfare of all beings), 12.13 (hates no being). Shankara's commentary on 6.32 reads atmaupamya as empathy by analogy [M gist, U wording]. Chandogya 6.8.7 (first tat tvam asi, repeated to 6.16.3) is metaphysics with no ethic attached; the ethical reading is later.
Tier: [M] verses; [U] alternative Mahabharata numbers and Shankara's wording.

13. Buddhism: parity, no owner, exchange.
Claim: Dhammapada 129 and 130: all tremble at violence; put yourself in the other's place and do not kill. Sutta Nipata 705: as I am so are they; drawing the parallel to yourself, do not kill. Samyutta Nikaya 55.7 (S V 353): the disciple reflects that he wants to live, so how could he do to others what he would not want done. Samyutta 3.8 and Udana 5.1: nothing is dearer than oneself, others hold themselves as dear, so do not harm. Udanavarga 5.18 (Rockhill): do not hurt others in ways you would find hurtful. Shantideva, Bodhicaryavatara 8 goes further: the limbs are many yet one body, so beings are one in wanting happiness (8.91 in Padmakara; numbering differs by edition); suffering has no owner, so remove it because it is suffering; then the practice of exchanging self and other.
Tier: [M]; chapter 8 verse numbers [U].
Use: Shantideva's argument is not identity. Paul Williams (Altruism and Reality, 1998) and Charles Goodman (Consequences of Compassion, 2009) keep that clear. A comparer who calls it One is the comparer.

## 4. How they compare

Six methods.

Thematic anthology. Huxley, Perry, Wilson's World Scripture and the Declaration's endnote put short passages from different traditions under one heading and let the commentary say they match. Each excerpt was chosen for fit, so agreement looks strong. The golden rule is the easiest theme because the surface wording is so close.

Derivation from metaphysics. Schopenhauer, Deussen, Vivekananda and the Traditionalists argue from the One to the ethic, treating reciprocity as visible effect and identity as cause. Differences are explained by how much of the identity each tradition says aloud: exoteric forms keep the rule, esoteric ones keep the reason (Schuon's method in general).

Consensus on practice. Kueng starts from ethics, claims a minimum already shared and avoids metaphysics on purpose. He handles differences by setting them aside.

Historical and axial. Armstrong, after Jaspers, places the rule in a short historical window and explains likeness by shared conditions, difference by context.

Philosophical typology. Wattles, Ricoeur and Gensler sort the rule by depth or logic. Ricoeur's pair (equivalence, superabundance) ranks the rule, which is what Alex needs.

Internal exegesis. The rabbis argue inside one verse (Akiva, Ben Azzai, Tanhuma): the oldest comparison of the two grounds, and the cleanest.

Who among the comparers separates reciprocity from unity:
- Kant (Groundwork, 4:430 footnote) rejects the trivial golden rule as a basis [M].
- Schopenhauer: reciprocity rests on self-interest, compassion on identity [M].
- Vivekananda: only oneness explains why to be unselfish [M gist, U source].
- Schuon and Huxley treat the ethic as the third part of the metaphysic [M gist].
- Ricoeur: the rule sits between exchange and gift [M].
- Wattles: levels [U].
- The rabbis, Akiva against Ben Azzai [M].
- Shankara reads Gita 6.32 as analogy, an empathy reading [M gist].
- Cialdini 1997 tests oneness against empathy; Batson says no merging is needed [M].
- Neusner and Chilton keep reciprocity in the title and stress differences [M].
- Kueng and Armstrong do not draw the line [M].

On differences more generally: Schuon says forms differ, the essence does not. Kueng says differences exist but do not block a shared floor. Panikkar's homeomorphic equivalents (same function in different concepts) is a useful middle path [M, outside my slice].

## 5. What scholars say

Historians (Dihle, Betz) show the rule is old and widespread but that formulas differ in sign, scope (kin, co-believers, all) and motive (prudence, fairness, compassion). Inside a hierarchy it can keep the hierarchy. Kant called it trivial, since it gives no content: my wants may differ from yours. Shaw's joke (do not treat others as you would be treated, their tastes may differ) is the popular form of the same point [M, wording from memory].

On the unity ground: Advaita specialists have long asked whether a doctrine that calls the world appearance can ground ethics, and Shankara treats ethics as purification before knowledge, with the knower freed from injunctions. Rambachan and Neo-Vedanta argue the opposite. Levinas held that ethics begins from the otherness of the other and that unity absorbs the other into the same (Totality and Infinity, 1961). Buber's I and Thou needs two. Zaehner (1957) held that the monistic mystic and the theistic lover are different cases. Nietzsche attacked pity, which hits Schopenhauer. Batson contests Cialdini. Confucian specialists say one body is graded love; Buddhist specialists say Shantideva does not teach a One.

Kueng and Armstrong get respect and the usual criticisms: a thin floor, drafted largely by Western or liberal hands, and broad generalisation (especially for Armstrong, a popular historian, not a specialist in each tradition) [M, soft].
