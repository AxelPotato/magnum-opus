## 1. The picture in brief

The first generation of the Traditionalist school (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) wrote the grid. The second generation is the group who lived inside one tradition and used the grid to explain it, and to explain the others through it. Burckhardt and Lings wrote Sufism and sacred art. Nasr carried the school into the university. Pallis wrote Tibetan Buddhism. Schaya wrote Kabbalah. Sherrard wrote Eastern Orthodoxy. Smith wrote physics, Vedanta and Catholicism. Raine wrote Blake and the Platonic poets. Northbourne wrote on farming and the critique of progress. Needleman reaches toward the school from Gurdjieff's work (he is a bridge, not a Traditionalist), and Upton is a late polemicist inside it.

What they add to the comparative project is depth in each language. Anthologists of the comparing kind (Huxley, Perry, Stace, Wilson) line up passages by theme; these people take one doctrine (the Unity of Islam, the Ein Sof of the Kabbalah, the void of Buddhism), show its structure from inside, and then name its equivalents in a sentence or a footnote. The unit of comparison is the structure of a doctrine. [C1]

Their usefulness to Alex is uneven. On the veil, the illusion and the return they are strong, and the best texts (Burckhardt on Sufi doctrine, Schaya on Kabbalah, Lings on the hereafter [U, see finding 9]) can be cited as witnesses. On lifetimes and karma they are weak witnesses: the school followed Guenon in rejecting reincarnation as the West understands it (the same individual returning to earth), and reads rebirth as change of state or as a doctrine peculiar to one tradition. The essay should say so openly. Which of the second generation is the most rigorous is a matter of impression, on my memory only [U] (finding 3); do not say it in the essay.

## 2. Findings

1. What defines the second generation.
Claim: Most of them practise, or practised, inside one traditional form (the Shadhili-Alawi Sufi line for Burckhardt and Lings; Tibetan Buddhism for Pallis; Orthodoxy for Sherrard; Catholicism for Smith after Vedanta) and compare from there. Not all: Schaya's own affiliation is not known to me [U]; Needleman is a Gurdjieffian; Raine was a poet and scholar; Northbourne was an English landowner and writer. The school's rule, from Guenon and Schuon, is that a path needs a tradition with a valid lineage, and that understanding the others follows. [C2]
Traditions cited: all the world religions, with Islam, Vedanta and Christianity most often.
Tier: [M] for the rule and the main cases; [U] for Schaya.
Use for the essay: honest framing. Alex is the opposite case (no lineage, many witnesses), so say that he reads them as witnesses and claims none of their authority.

2. The grid they share.
Claim: They sort a doctrine into three layers (the Absolute or Beyond-Being, Being as the personal Lord, and manifestation) and a person into three (spirit, soul, body). They split each tradition into outer form (exoterism) and inner content (esoterism). In Islam they use the hadith of Gabriel (submission, faith, excellence or vision: islam, iman, ihsan) as the same split in Islamic terms [M, high].
Who: Guenon and Schuon first; Burckhardt and Lings in their Sufi works; Nasr in Knowledge and the Sacred (1981). Locators unknown.
Tier: [M].
Use for the essay: a table of equivalent terms, labelled as the school's grid, with the note that specialists keep several of these apart.

3. Who is most rigorous inside the movement, and why.
Claim: By my memory of how insiders write, among the founders Guenon is the measure of doctrinal strictness, Coomaraswamy of textual scholarship, Schuon of range. Among the second generation, Burckhardt is held up as the most exact and sober exponent (impersonal prose, no polemic, no autobiography, a trained eye for how doctrine fits form). Schaya is the school's reference for Kabbalah. Lings is valued for clarity and for living what he wrote. Nasr has the strongest credentials (Harvard PhD, Gifford Lectures) and is the public face. Smith is the scientist.
Why: in this school rigour means keeping a doctrine inside its own terms, using the original technical words, refusing to mix traditions, and refusing to psychologize.
Tier: [U]. This is my impression of the reception, not a fact. [C3]
Use for the essay: if one voice is cited for the Sufi side, Burckhardt is a reasonable choice; for Kabbalah, Schaya. Check each first. Do not state a ranking in the essay; say what each author does and let the reader judge.

4. Titus Burckhardt: life and works.

5. Burckhardt: Sufi doctrine as a map of the axiom.
Claim: In Introduction to Sufi Doctrine he gives the Sufi reading of the Shahada as a statement about reality (nothing is real but the Real) and the world as the self-disclosure of the One, with Universal Man (al-insan al-kamil) as the point where the world knows its source. The Essence is beyond all Names; the Names unfold the cosmos; the person returns by the passing away of the sense of a separate self (fana) and abiding in the One (baqa). The path has stages in Sufi terms (fear, love, knowledge) [U that he frames it so].
Who: Burckhardt, Introduction to Sufi Doctrine (whole work; chapters not remembered).
Traditions cited: Vedanta (Atman, maya) in comparison notes, as I recall [U].
Tier: [M] for the doctrine, which is Ibn Arabi's as the school presents it; [U] for Burckhardt's own cross-references.
Use for the essay: the closest second-generation statement of Alex's three clauses (one whole, it appears as many, the many return). Cite as "the Sufi account as Burckhardt gives it" after checking passages.

6. The veil, the hidden treasure and the renewal of creation.
Claim: Three Sufi ideas used by Burckhardt and the school bear on the illusion. First, the world is a veil (hijab) that also reveals: the One is hidden by the forms through which it shows itself. Second, a saying that God was a hidden treasure and loved to be known, so He created ("kuntu kanzan makhfiyyan fa-ahbabtu an u'rafa fa-khalaqtu al-khalq li-u'rafa"). It supplies a reason for separation. Hadith scholars (Ibn Taymiyya, and I recall al-Zarkashi and Ibn Hajar) judged it not authentic as a hadith, and Ibn Arabi and the Sufis accept it by unveiling (kashf) though they grant it has no sound chain [M]. Third, Ibn Arabi teaches that creation is renewed at each instant (tajdid al-khalq) and that the Divine self-disclosure never repeats in the same form (la takrar fi al-tajalli) [M]. A form cannot literally return, and nothing is lost. [C13]
Who: Ibn Arabi (Fusus al-Hikam, Meccan Openings), through Burckhardt and, in English, Chittick (The Sufi Path of Knowledge, 1989 [M]). Locators unknown.
Traditions cited as parallels: maya as veil and projection; tzimtzum.
Tier: [M] for the ideas and the hadith status; [U] for where Burckhardt cites them.
Use for the essay: the treasure saying answers "why would the whole go into separation". Say it is a saying, not a verified hadith. The renewal teaching is the Sufi reason for caution about reincarnation.

7. Burckhardt: Alchemy and sacred art as method.
Claim: Alchemy reads the Hermetic, Arabic, Chinese and Indian arts as one symbolic science whose real work is the transformation of the soul, and criticizes Jung's reading (alchemy as projection of the psyche). The three colours (black, white, red) stand for dissolving, purifying and fixing. Sacred Art argues that a form (icon, mandala, mosque) supports contemplation.
Traditions cited: Greek-Egyptian, Islamic, Chinese Taoist, Indian [U for the Indian part].
Tier: [M] thesis and Jung critique, [U] details.
Use for the essay: black, white, red as a picture for stages.

8. Martin Lings: life and the three certainties.
Claim: English, 1909 to 2005. Studied at Oxford, taught in Lithuania, went to Cairo in 1939 and met Guenon [U for the date and for the order of conversion, initiation and meeting], became a Sufi in the Alawi line (name Abu Bakr Siraj ad-Din), later keeper of Oriental manuscripts at the British Museum and Library [M, details U]. The file's gloss "certainty by report, by sight, by being" is a gloss, not Lings's wording. [C11]

9. Lings on the hereafter.
Claim: The school's picture (Guenon, Schuon) is that the physical world is the outermost layer of a nested cosmos and the other worlds are inward states, not distant places. Islamic (barzakh, the garden, the fire), Christian (purgatory, heaven, hell, Dante) and Hindu (the three worlds) after-death teaching are read as the same doctrine in several vocabularies. The answer to "where does a soul go" is into other states of being, and not back into a body. That Lings sets these three side by side in a named book of his own is [U]: I cannot place it (candidates: The Book of Certainty, Symbol and Archetype, What is Sufism?). [C4]
Who: the school; Lings as one exponent [U].
Traditions cited: Islam, Christianity, Hindu, Platonic.
Tier: [M] for the school's doctrine; [U] for the attribution to Lings and for which book makes which point.
Use for the essay: the continuing soul the school can offer is a being in states, and the returning person is outside its picture. Read Lings directly before using, and do not write "Lings says" until a passage is open.

10. Lings on cycles, superstitions and symbols.
Claim: The Eleventh Hour reads the present as the late stage of a long cycle (Hindu yugas, Hesiod's ages, the Quranic Hour). Ancient Beliefs and Modern Superstitions argues that modern creeds (progress, evolution, spiritualism, psychoanalysis) replace lost doctrine. Symbol and Archetype reads each created thing as a symbol of a higher archetype, so Plato's Ideas and the Quranic Names are one layer in two languages.
Who: Lings, 1987, 1965, 1991. Locators unknown.
Traditions cited: Hindu, Greek, Islam, Christianity, Platonism.
Tier: [M] for theses of The Eleventh Hour, [U] for the other two theses, for chapters, and for whether Ancient Beliefs addresses reincarnation by name.
Use for the essay: Alex's arrow (a person's progress) differs from the school's cyclical decline of history, so say the arrow is about a person. The symbol view gives a gentler word than illusion: the world is a real pointer.

11. Seyyed Hossein Nasr: the academic voice.
Claim: Born Tehran 1933, MIT and Harvard (PhD in history of science), professor in Tehran, head of the Imperial Iranian Academy of Philosophy, then Temple and George Washington University after 1979 [M]. 16. The Kabbalistic form of separation and return.
Claim: Lurianic Kabbalah (Safed, 16th century) holds the tradition's own version of the axiom. The Infinite withdraws to make room (tzimtzum), the vessels break and sparks fall into the world (shevirat ha-kelim), and human acts gather the sparks and repair the whole (tikkun). The first man (Adam ha-Rishon) is described as containing every human soul. This is a different figure from Adam Kadmon, who in Luria is the first configuration of light after the tzimtzum, not the first human [M]. [C6] Transmigration (gilgul) is accepted in the Bahir, the Zohar and Luria. Hebrew teshuvah means return, read by Kabbalists as a cosmic return. Chabad Hasidism reads Deuteronomy 4:35 (there is nothing besides Him) literally and teaches, in the Tanya (Shaar ha-Yichud ve-ha-Emunah), that from God's standpoint the world has no separate existence [M, as history of ideas]. Chabad's own teaching distinguishes a lower and a higher unity and does not deny the world; some scholars call the system acosmic (Rachel Elior [U]). [C7]
Who: Luria's circle (Hayyim Vital), Zohar, Bahir, Shneur Zalman of Liadi (Tanya). Whether Schaya treats gilgul: unknown [U].
Tier: [M] for the doctrines.
Use for the essay: the strongest Jewish parallel to the axiom and to the fingers of one hand. Check with Scholem and Idel. Do not write that Chabad says the world does not exist.

18. Philip Sherrard: Orthodoxy and sacred cosmology.

## 4. How they compare

The method, as I recall it from the books above.

1. They start inside one tradition and state its central doctrine in its own technical terms. Burckhardt gives the Arabic words and explains them from Ibn Arabi before he says anything about Hinduism.
2. They then name equivalents in a sentence or a footnote, using the shared grid (finding 2). Schaya, as I recall, says that a Kabbalistic term corresponds to a term elsewhere and moves on. The comparison maps roles in a structure and needs no long quotation.
3. They sort differences into three kinds. Differences of outer form (rites, laws, stories) are adaptation to a people and an age. Differences of emphasis (Buddhism stresses the void, Islam the Unity, Christianity love) are differences of perspective, each tradition choosing one face of one reality. Differences that fit neither are blamed on degeneration or a popular misreading, which is Alex's own correction.
4. They compare by practice as well as doctrine. Pallis and Lings point to invocation, stillness and the dying of the self as one operation in several traditions. Here the school stands closer to Alex than to the anthologists, because Part one's method (letting go) is itself the cross-tradition constant.
5. They reject psychological and historical reductions (Burckhardt on Jung, Lings on modern superstitions) and syncretism in practice. Alex quotes many traditions, so he stands outside the rule while using the results.
6. Where the comparer equates, say so. Ein Sof with Brahman or the Dhat is an equation drawn by the comparer, since Jewish and Hindu specialists keep them apart. The void as the Absolute is the Traditionalist reading of Buddhism, which Theravada scholars deny. Rebirth as the return of the same human individuality to earth, taught in sequence of lives, is the Theosophical and spiritist reading, which the school itself rejects; karma as a law of action and result is Indian and the school does not reject it, though Guenon reads it as "action" in a wider sense [M]. [C8]

## 5. What scholars say

Short, from memory [M for the schools of thought, U for the critics' exact positions].

- Mark Sedgwick's Against the Modern World: Traditionalism and the Secret Intellectual History of the Twentieth Century (Oxford University Press, 2004) is the standard history of the school. It treats Traditionalism as a real intellectual current with influence on Islamic and Western thought, and records its anti-modern politics.
- Steven Katz and the constructivists argue that mystical experience is shaped by tradition, so no common core can be assumed. The school replies that the unity lies in the Intellect and in doctrine, which sidesteps the objection and leaves open what evidence settles it.
- Specialists object to specific equations. Theravada scholars deny that nirvana is the Absolute, and many say dependent origination is no One. Scholem and Idel treat Kabbalah as a historical development and do not use the grid. Within Islam, Sirhindi's unity of witnessing against Ibn Arabi's unity of being, and the Salafi charge of pantheism, show that the Sufi reading is disputed from inside [M].
- Smith's critique of evolution and the Big Bang is rejected by mainstream scientists [M]. Nasr is respected as a historian of Islamic science and philosophy, and his role under the Shah (he headed the Empress's private office, as I recall) has drawn criticism [U].
- Zaehner (Mysticism Sacred and Profane, 1957) is the best-known critic of the Huxley-style claim that mystical experience is one kind; his types (nature, monistic, theistic) apply to the Traditionalists' claims as well [M].
- Allegations about Schuon's circle (see P04) touch the school's reputation, so present it as a source and not as a spotless authority.
