# 1. The picture in brief

On lives and karma the perennial camp is split, and the split is the useful fact. There are five camps.

The strict Traditionalists (Guenon, and after him Schuon, Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt) deny reincarnation in the Western sense, the return of the same individual to an earthly life. Guenon calls it a nineteenth century error born in Spiritism and Theosophy [M]; the others follow him on this because they hold the same Islamic or Traditionalist position [U]. They teach instead that a being has many states, that no state is repeated, and that the Hindu and Buddhist talk of rebirth is a lower or popular way of speaking about this. Mainstream Islam and Christianity, by contrast, teach one earthly life and then an afterlife, and Guenon and Schuon read that afterlife as passage to other states; the religions themselves do not say it in those terms. [M]

Coomaraswamy keeps transmigration but changes the question. The one who goes from life to life is not the individual but the one Self, "the one and only transmigrant" (the title of his paper). This is the nearest thing in the whole literature to Alex's axiom.

The Theosophists (Blavatsky, Olcott, Sinnett, Besant, Judge) put reincarnation and karma at the centre, with a soul that carries the harvest of each life into the next.

Huxley, Smith and Wilber do not make rebirth part of the common core. Jung translates it into psychology. (Huxley is one popular anthology among many; see section 9.)

They disagree for five reasons: a metaphysical argument (nothing is repeated), loyalty to an Abrahamic home religion, a polemic against modern ideas of progress, a quarrel over which layer of a tradition counts as the real teaching, and a quarrel over who or what is the subject that continues.

They agree on more than they admit: the ego is not the final self, the being has more room than one short life, action has consequences that outlast the act, and the aim is the same return. Alex's axiom does not need to take a side on reincarnation, because on his axiom the one who lives every life is the whole.

# 2. Findings

1. The map of positions. Claim: no single perennial position on rebirth exists. Who: synthesis of Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon, Blavatsky, Huxley, Jung (works below). Words: paraphrase. Parallels cited: all the traditions below. Tier [M]. Use: state it in one plain paragraph, with four positions. (a) The same individual returns (popular Theosophy, Hindu and Buddhist folk belief). (b) One earthly life, then other states or worlds (Islam, Christianity in their own terms; Guenon, Schuon and the Muslim Traditionalists read this as passage through other states). (c) No return to the same state but an indefinite passage through many states (Guenon's own doctrine). (d) The one subject lives all lives (Coomaraswamy, Advaita, the axiom). The first file folded (b) into (c); that was wrong, see corrections log entry 2.

2. Guenon rejects reincarnation. Who: Rene Guenon, Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921; English Theosophy: History of a Pseudo-Religion), L'Erreur spirite (1923; English The Spiritist Fallacy), L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta (1925; English Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta), Les Etats multiples de l'etre (1932; English The Multiple States of the Being), and Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921). Locator for the specific chapters: unknown. The words, paraphrased: the human bodily state is one state among an indefinite number open to a being; possibility is unlimited and a state once run through is not run through again, because repetition would contradict the infinity of possibility; what a being does is pass to other states. He traced Western reincarnationism to Allan Kardec and the Theosophists and tied it to the modern faith in progress. Parallels he points to: the Vedantic doctrine of states and of liberation beyond all conditioned states. A matching idea exists in Ibn Arabi (no repetition in the divine self-disclosure). Guenon was initiated into Sufism, I recall around 1912 [U on the date], and lived as a Muslim in Cairo from 1930 until 1951 [M]. Tier [M] for the position and the titles, [U] for the claim that he went as far as saying the Hindu and Buddhist traditions themselves do not teach reincarnation, which I recall but cannot place. Use: he is the strongest voice against "I come back". He does not deny continuation, only return to the same state, and the axiom needs neither. Say that he does not count a Spiritist or Theosophist as a tradition's voice.

3. Coomaraswamy and "the one and only transmigrant". Who: Ananda K. Coomaraswamy, "On the One and Only Transmigrant", printed with "Recollection, Indian and Platonic", Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3 (1944); reprinted in Selected Papers vol. 2, Metaphysics (ed. Roger Lipsey, Bollingen 1977). Tier [M] for the title and year. Words, paraphrased: the Upanishads say the only seer, hearer, thinker and knower is the one Self (Brihadaranyaka 3.7.23 and 3.8.11), so the only one who travels from life to life is that Self, wearing each individual as a vehicle; the personalities do not come back. The verses I recall are real and fit his argument: the Self as the thread on which all beings are strung (Brihadaranyaka 3.7.1 to 2, Gita 7.7, the sutratman), the Knower of the field in all fields (Gita 13.2), the two birds on one tree (Rigveda 1.164.20, Mundaka 3.1.1, Katha 1.3.1), Plato's recollection, Eckhart's ground of the soul, and the Gospel command to deny oneself (Matthew 16:24). DEMOTED to [U]: that Coomaraswamy cites each of these in THIS paper. Do not write "he cites X in this paper" until someone has opened it. Use: this is the axiom in older words, and the thread in Alex's "lives and the thread" picture is the sutratman. Check the exact argument in the paper before quoting.

4. The Buddhist test of Coomaraswamy's reading. Claim: he read the Buddha's anatta as a denial that the aggregates are the Self, not a denial of the Self. Texts at stake: the monk Sati who says one consciousness runs on through rebirths and is rebuked (Majjhima 38); the Buddha's silence when asked if there is a self (Samyutta 44.10); his refusal of the question "who feels?" (Samyutta 12.12). Tier [M] for the texts. Scholars: the mainstream reading (Rahula 1959, Collins 1982, Gombrich) is that early Buddhism denies any abiding self, so Coomaraswamy's reading is a minority one, though a few modern scholars and Thanissaro Bhikkhu argue the silence is strategic [U on details]. Use: the essay's line "no fixed soul, yet something continues" is the safe one. Say that the comparer reads the Buddha's silence as agreement with the Upanishads, and specialists keep the two apart.

5. Coomaraswamy on karma and second birth. Claim: karma is the Sanskrit word for action, and he insisted it is a law of consequence and not fate or reward and punishment; action done without grasping at results frees (Gita 3.9, 4.18). He also read "rebirth" as a second birth inside this life: the twice-born (dvija), initiation, Eckhart's birth of God in the soul and John 3:3. Tier [M] for karma as action, [U] for the second birth claim being a sustained thesis of his. Use: supports the essay's treatment of John 3:3 and the door as birth inside a life. Check Hinduism and Buddhism (1943).

6. Schuon: forms differ, the truth does not. Who: Frithjof Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French De l'unite transcendante des religions 1948, English 1953), Esoterism as Principle and as Way (English 1981, French 1978 as I recall), In the Tracks of Buddhism (English 1968), and many other books. Claim, paraphrased: religions disagree at the level of form and agree at the level of essence, and the formal contradictions are providential, each tradition stressing an aspect. The Eastern long cycle and the Western single decisive life can both be read this way: one stresses the gravity of this life, the other the length of the road. Tier [M] for the general principle, [U] for the rebirth application, locator unknown. He agreed with Guenon that modern reincarnationism is wrong [U]. On law and grace he was a close student of Pure Land Buddhism and set it beside Christian and Hindu devotion. A later paperback of The Transcendent Unity carries an introduction by Huston Smith [U]. Use: Schuon is the best source for how a comparer explains why traditions differ on rebirth. Open his Transcendent Unity before citing.

7. Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt, Pallis, Perry. Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, 1981), Lings (What is Sufism?, 1975; The Book of Certainty, 1952) and Burckhardt (Introduction to Sufi Doctrine, 1959) are Muslims in the Guenon and Schuon line, so they hold the Islamic position: one earthly life, barzakh, resurrection, and rejection of tanasukh. Marco Pallis (A Buddhist Spectrum, 1980) is a Traditionalist and a Buddhist, so he treats rebirth as part of his tradition. Whitall Perry's A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971) is a very large topical anthology from this circle. Tier [M] for the people and titles, [U] for what each says on rebirth and for any entry in Perry on it. Use: this shows that the Traditionalist stance on rebirth follows the author's own religion, which is itself a fair point to make.

8. Huxley (rewritten; he is one popular anthology among many, see section 9). Who: Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945; United States 1945, Britain 1946 as I recall), with a chapter titled "Immortality and Survival" (I think chapter 14, [U]) and a separate chapter "Time and Eternity". Do not confuse the latter with Coomaraswamy's 1947 book of the same name. Claim: rebirth is not in Huxley's own three-part definition of the perennial philosophy, given in his foreword (a divine Reality in the world, something in the soul like it, and the aim of knowing it). In the chapter on survival he sets posthumous states beside one another (the Tibetan bardo, Christian purgatory, Hindu and Buddhist rebirth) as depending on the state of the soul. Tier [M] for the foreword's three parts, [U] for his stance in the chapter. Caveat: the foreword says the phrase was coined by Leibniz; the phrase is older (section 9.1). Use: Huxley shows that one popular statement of the common core can omit rebirth, which supports saying the traditions are not unanimous. He is a door for a reader, not an authority; the load-bearing perennial doctrine on lives and karma sits in Guenon, Schuon, Coomaraswamy and Smith.

9. Smith and Wilber. Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976; I recall the subtitle changing between editions, one being "The Primordial Tradition" and a later one "The Common Vision of the World's Religions", [U] on which came first): four levels of reality and of selfhood, from body to Spirit, which gives a vertical map of what continues. Ken Wilber, The Atman Project (1980), Up from Eden (1981), Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995): development as a movement from union through separation back to union, involution then evolution. Tier [M] for the models, [U] for each man's own stand on reincarnation (I think both are cautious and I cannot place a passage). Use: Wilber's Atman project is the nearest modern developmental twin of "we are one, experiencing separation, returning"; his involution and evolution scheme owes much to Aurobindo and to Hegel.

10. The evolutionary reincarnationists. Sri Aurobindo, The Life Divine (serial in the journal Arya 1914 to 1919, book 1939 to 1940) and The Problem of Rebirth (1952): a psychic being, the evolving soul, takes on a new mind, life and body each time; the personality does not return; there is no fall back into animals; karma is a working of nature that the soul can outgrow. Rudolf Steiner, Theosophy (1904) and later karma lectures, teaches rebirth and a Christ-centred karma; he left the Theosophical Society and founded Anthroposophy. Tier [M] for the broad lines. Use: Aurobindo is the best Indian model for a staged ascent across lives, so he is a parallel for the arrow of consciousness.

11. Theosophy. Who: H. P. Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (1877), The Secret Doctrine (1888), The Key to Theosophy (1889), The Voice of the Silence (1889); A. P. Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (1883); Annie Besant, Reincarnation (1892) and Karma (1895), The Ancient Wisdom (1897); William Quan Judge, The Ocean of Theosophy (1893). The words, paraphrased: in Isis Unveiled she rejected Kardec's reincarnation and allowed return only in exceptional cases; from the later 1870s and 1880s she and her circle taught reincarnation as the rule. The reincarnating Ego (the individuality) returns, the personality does not; between lives come a purgatory-like kamaloka and a long heaven-state, devachan; karma is the law of cause and effect and of restored harmony, with no judge. The third of the three Proem propositions in The Secret Doctrine reads very close to Alex's axiom. Recalled wording, NOT to be printed until checked against the text: "The fundamental identity of all Souls with the Universal Over-Soul, the latter being itself an aspect of the Unknown Root; and the obligatory pilgrimage for every Soul, a spark of the former, through the Cycle of Incarnation (or Necessity) in accordance with Cyclic and Karmic law, during the whole term." Tier [M] for the substance, [U] for the exact wording. The thread-self (sutratman) is used here too. They also teach that the unity of humanity is the basis of ethics and of karma, and "separateness" is called the great heresy [U on the exact phrase and place]. Like Guenon, they hold that the popular belief in rebirth as animals is a distortion; unlike him, they hold the true teaching is reincarnation. Tier [M] except where marked. Use: quote the third proposition after checking the text; it is public domain.

12. Theosophy's critics. Max Mueller said in 1893 that there is no "esoteric Buddhism" (the Buddha says he kept no closed fist; Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha 16, the passage numbers differently in different translations, so take the number from the translation used). William Emmette Coleman (1895, an appendix to Solovyov's A Modern Priestess of Isis) traced much of The Secret Doctrine to nineteenth century books. The Hodgson Report (1885) judged her phenomena fraudulent, a verdict Vernon Harrison's 1986 paper "J'Accuse: An Examination of the Hodgson Report of 1885" reopened (the notes already list both). Guenon's Le Theosophisme calls it a pseudo-religion. Campbell (Ancient Wisdom Revived, 1980) and Godwin (The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994) are the standard histories. Tier [M]. Use: Alex can cite Theosophy for the comparative project and admit that specialists dispute its sources.

13. Jung. Who: "Concerning Rebirth" (Eranos 1939, in Collected Works 9 part 1); "Psychological Commentary" on Evans-Wentz's Tibetan Book of the Dead (written 1935, first in German, in CW 11); Memories, Dreams, Reflections (1962), the chapter on life after death. Claim: the word rebirth covers five different things: metempsychosis, reincarnation proper, resurrection, rebirth as renewal inside one life, and taking part in a rite of transformation. He treats them as psychic facts and does not rule on metaphysics. He read the bardo as an initiation process [M] and said, in the opening of the commentary, that the book had been a constant companion to him (recalled wording, not to be printed unchecked); "reversed initiation" is [U]. In his memoir he wrote, as I recall, that he sometimes felt he was answering questions left open by earlier lives, without claiming it. Tier [M] for the five senses and the commentary, [U] for the memoir passage. Use: Jung gives Alex a clean way to separate the senses of "rebirth" before claiming one.

14. The Greek record. Pythagoras and the soul of the beaten dog (Xenophanes, Diels-Kranz 21 B7); Empedocles' "I have been a boy and a girl, a bush, a bird" (DK 31 B117); Herodotus 2.123 on Egyptian teaching (Egyptologists do not accept it); Pindar, Olympian 2 (three lives in each world to reach the Isles of the Blessed); Orphic gold tablets with the cycle of births and the waters of Memory against Lethe; Plato in Meno 81, Phaedo, Phaedrus 246 to 249, Republic 10, Timaeus 41 to 42, Laws 10 (903 to 905). Plato's key point for karma: in the Myth of Er the blame lies with the chooser and God is blameless (Republic 617e). Caesar, Gallic War 6.14, says the Druids taught that souls pass to other bodies. Whether Greek rebirth came from India is disputed; many scholars say it arose independently. Tier [M]. Use: Myth of Er is the best Western statement that responsibility, not punishment, is the point.

15. Plotinus and the Neoplatonists. Enneads 4.9 asks whether all souls are one, and answers that they are one and many; 3.2 compares souls to actors who change roles; 3.4 covers the allotted spirit; 6.4 and 6.5 treat the one Being present everywhere whole. Plotinus accepted transmigration, including punitive falls, while Porphyry and Iamblichus rejected rebirth into animals (reported by later writers, so the report is secondhand), a dispute that looks like the later Theosophist and Guenonian dislike of animal rebirth. Tier [M] on the lines, [U] on exact passages. Use: the oneness of souls and many lives are already together in Plotinus, which is the axiom's pairing.

16. Christianity beyond the essay. Origen (De Principiis; Commentary on Matthew 13.1) taught pre-existence and final restoration and called transmigration foreign to the church; the notes say that the claim that the Council of 553 banned reincarnation is not supported, and Theosophical and anthology writers (Head and Cranston, Reincarnation: An East-West Anthology, 1961, and Reincarnation: The Phoenix Fire Mystery, 1977; Cranston was a Theosophist, so these are partisan anthologies) repeat it. John 9:2 is quoted by rebirth advocates (was the man blind through his own sin?) and read by scholars as a reflection of Jewish views on prenatal sin. Geddes MacGregor (Reincarnation in Christianity, 1978) argues a Christian case; John Hick (Death and Eternal Life, 1976; Evil and the God of Love, 1966) argues that if the world is a place of soul-making then the task is longer than one life and so continues in other lives or worlds. Tier [M]. Use: Hick makes Alex's "otherwise the whole is a bad designer" argument in academic form.

17. Judaism, gilgul. Appears in the Bahir (12th century), the Zohar, and above all Isaac Luria (1534 to 1572) and Hayyim Vital (Sha'ar ha-Gilgulim): the soul returns to repair a flaw (tikkun), souls have roots in the limbs of the first Adam, and there are special forms (ibbur, dibbuk). Saadia Gaon (Emunot ve-De'ot, 933) argued against transmigration; Menasseh ben Israel wrote on it (1651). Scholem, "Gilgul" in the Encyclopaedia Judaica and in On the Mystical Shape of the Godhead (1991). Tier [M]. Use: the picture of souls as organs of Adam and repair as the purpose of return is the fingers and the thread in Jewish form.

18. Islam. Mainstream theology rejects tanasukh (Avicenna's argument that each soul arises with a fitted body; Quran 23:99 to 100), while the Druze (taqammus: death is followed directly by rebirth as a human), Nusayris, Yarsan and some extremist Shia groups teach it. Stevenson drew many cases from Druze Lebanon. Barzakh (Quran 23:100) is the in-between, often set beside the Tibetan bardo, though one ends in resurrection and the other in rebirth. Ibn Arabi teaches that creation is renewed at every moment and that nothing repeats. Mulla Sadra teaches the soul's substantial movement and development after death without rebirth in this world. Rumi's lines on dying as mineral, plant, animal and man (Masnavi 3, around 3901 to 3906 in Nicholson's numbering) describe the soul's rise through forms; Chittick and others read them as the arc of ascent and not reincarnation. Tier [M]. Use: the Islamic position is a strong case of "growth continues, the return is dropped". Warning: use a named translator for any English Rumi line.

19. Hindu texts that answer "not enough time". Gita 6.45 says the yogi perfected through many births reaches the goal; 7.19 says the wise one comes to Me at the end of many births; 4.5 has Krishna remembering many births; 8.6 says the last thought at death directs the passage. Brihadaranyaka 4.4.3 to 5 (the caterpillar and the goldsmith: the Self fashions a newer form; as one acts so one becomes), Chandogya 5.10.7 (conduct decides the womb), Yoga Sutra 2.13 (the store of karma bears fruit as birth, lifespan and experience). Tier [M]. Use: Gita 6.45 and 7.19 are direct witnesses for "the run is longer than one life".

20. Hindu texts for the axiom. Gita 13.16 (13.17 in some editions) and 18.20: the Reality is undivided in beings and yet appears divided. Gita 15.7: the living soul is an eternal part of Me. Brahma Sutra 2.1.34 to 36 faces the objection that a creator who makes unequal lives is cruel and answers with karma and a beginningless cycle. Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 2.32: from the highest standpoint no one is bound, no one is liberated. Ramana Maharshi's habit, as I recall, was to turn the question "who is reborn?" back to "who are you now?" [U on place]. Tier [M] except Ramana. Take verse numbers from the translation printed in the essay.

21. Buddhist teaching on karma. Intention is karma (Anguttara 6.63). Beings are heirs of their actions (Anguttara 5.57, the five recollections). Short and long analyses of action (Majjhima 135, 136). The Buddha rejects "everything I feel comes from past karma" and lists other causes (Samyutta 36.21), and later tradition gives five orders of natural law, karma being one. At the awakening he gains three knowledges, recall of past lives, the passing of beings by their deeds, and the end of the taints (Majjhima 4, 36). Dhammapada 1 and 2 (the wheel follows the ox), 127 (no spot to escape the result), 165 (purity and impurity depend on oneself). Yogacara adds the store consciousness carrying seeds; Tibetan tradition has recognised lines of rebirth (tulku) from the 13th century. Tier [M]. Use: Samyutta 36.21 supports the essay's warning that blame is the cheap karma.

22. Jain and Sikh. Jain: karma is fine matter that binds the soul through passion; eight kinds; souls are many, eternal and uncreated; liberation is the shedding of karma (Tattvartha Sutra chapter 8, 8.1 on the causes of bondage; 5.21 on souls helping one another). Sikh: rebirth through a long cycle of births, the human birth a rare chance, liberation while living; Japji pauri 4, closing line (Guru Granth Sahib page 2) says the body comes by karma and liberation by grace. Tier [M], including the page 2 locator from reviewer recall. Use: Jain and Sikh are strong on rebirth and karma but not on one whole (Jain souls are many), so cite them for the lives, not the oneness.

23. Karma in the West and the fingers. Hosea 8:7 (sow the wind, reap the whirlwind), Psalm 7:15 to 16, Matthew 7:2 (measure for measure), Galatians 6:7, Aeschylus (the doer must suffer, Choephori 313), Anaximander (things pay penalty to one another, DK 12 B1), Heraclitus B94, Emerson's essay "Compensation" (1841). The closest match to the fingers is Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Representation vol. 1, section 63 ("eternal justice"): since one will lives in all, tormentor and tormented are the same, and the tormentor suffers what he inflicts. He drew on the Upanishads through Anquetil-Duperron's Latin Oupnek'hat and put the doctrine of rebirth as palingenesis (the will returns, not the memory) against metempsychosis (World as Will vol. 2, chapter 41). Tier [M] on works, [U] on exact section numbers beyond 63. Use: Schopenhauer is public domain in old translations and is the best precedent for the fingers.

24. Law and grace. The traditions that teach consequence also teach a way out that is not payment: Japji 4, Pure Land other-power, Paul's grace (Ephesians 2:8), the Islamic mercy that outruns wrath, the Jain verse of forgiveness. Tier [M]. Use: Alex says repair undoes karma; add that forgiveness is the partner of repair.

# 4. How they compare

The Traditionalists use the exoteric and esoteric split. Differences between religions sit in the outer forms and doctrines; unity sits in the inner or metaphysical level. Guenon tests a doctrine against his own picture of "Tradition", so reincarnation fails and the multiple states pass. Schuon refines it: formal contradictions are intended, each form a refracted whole. The risk is circular: the inner layer is whatever the comparer needs.

Coomaraswamy uses philology. He lines up Sanskrit, Pali, Greek, Latin and Eckhart's German in a single paragraph and treats matching terms as evidence: smrti and anamnesis (recollection), sutratman and the thread, the two birds and the two selves. His strength is real learning, his risk is that translated words differ in context.

The anthologists (Perry, Huxley) cut quotations into topics and set them side by side, which shows resemblance and hides context. Perry's Treasury is by far the larger and the more systematic of the two [M]; Huxley's is the shorter and more widely read.

The Theosophists use the wisdom-religion model: one ancient teaching, Senzar stanzas and hidden masters, spreading into all religions, with garbled outer forms. Blavatsky and Guenon share the move of "popular belief is a distortion" and disagree about what the original says.

Smith and Wilber use levels: one map of reality and selfhood with each tradition mapping a part.

Jung uses types of experience: he sorts the senses of rebirth and does not say which is metaphysically true.

Concrete examples of equation: bardo and barzakh (both "between"), atman with Intellect with Spirit with the ruh, karma with measure for measure with sowing and reaping, Lethe with the Myth of Er's river. How they explain disagreement on rebirth: Guenon, deformation plus modern error; Theosophy, garbling of one teaching; Schuon, providential perspectives; Coomaraswamy, a confusion about who the transmigrant is; Hick, a plurality of responses to one Real; Jung, different psychological meanings.

# 5. What scholars say

Mark Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) is the standard history of Traditionalism and notes its sources, its Muslim and anti-modern commitments, the Evola strand with fascist politics, and the later controversies around Schuon. Guenon read Sanskrit sources through informants and never went to India. Indologists reject any claim that Hinduism and Buddhism do not teach rebirth. Katz, Proudfoot and Sharf (already in the notes) reject strong perennialism as hiding the role of language and doctrine; Forman and Hick answer. Coomaraswamy's reading of anatta is a minority view. Donald Lopez (The Tibetan Book of the Dead: A Biography, 2011) shows how the title and framing came from Evans-Wentz. Paul Edwards (1996) is the main critic of reincarnation; Stevenson's cases did not convince the scientific community. Scholem treated gilgul as a late medieval Kabbalistic doctrine with disputed origins. Chittick reads Rumi and Ibn Arabi as ascent and renewal, not reincarnation. Historians of the Origen controversy reject the claim of a council ban on reincarnation.

# 9. Beyond Huxley: the wider perennial shelf (new section, added at Alex's note)

Alex's point: the perennial philosophy movement is much larger than Huxley's book, and in his view Huxley is not even the best of them. This section lists what else exists and what each gives Part two on lives, the soul and karma. It does not rank. All entries are from reviewer recall, not searched; tier marked.

9.1 Where the phrase comes from. Agostino Steuco (Italian, 1497 to 1548) published De perenni philosophia in 1540 [M]. Leibniz used "philosophia perennis" in his correspondence in the 1710s (a letter to Remond, I think 1714, [U on the date]) and is the author Huxley names as coiner; the standard article correcting the story is Charles B. Schmitt, "Perennial Philosophy: From Agostino Steuco to Leibniz", Journal of the History of Ideas 27 (1966) [M]. Marsilio Ficino and Pico della Mirandola are the earlier Renaissance background (the "ancient theology") [M]. Use: if the essay says where "perennial philosophy" comes from, do not repeat "Leibniz coined it".

9.2 Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951). The founder of the Traditionalist line; his word is "Tradition" (primordial and one) rather than "perennial". For lives and karma: the doctrine of multiple states and the rejection of reincarnationism (findings 1 and 2). The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times (French 1945) is another book where he touches survival and the after-death states, [U] on details. Tier [M] for the person, titles and thrust.

9.3 Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998). The most developed theorist of "the transcendent unity of religions" and of the exoteric and esoteric levels; his phrases "religio perennis" and "sophia perennis" are the ones the Traditionalist presses use in their names [M]. For lives: why traditions differ on the length of the road (finding 6). Tier [M] for the person and the main title.

9.4 Ananda Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947). Scholar of Indian and Western art and metaphysics; the source for "one and only transmigrant" (finding 3). Other books to open: Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), Time and Eternity (1947; do not confuse with Huxley's chapter), Selected Papers (Lipsey, Bollingen 1977). Tier [M].

9.5 Huston Smith (1919 to 2016). Forgotten Truth (1976): a plain vertical map of body, mind, soul, Spirit. He was a student and friend of Schuon's thought and wrote the introduction to a later edition of The Transcendent Unity [U]. For lives: gives Part two a readable "levels" vocabulary for what continues. Tier [M] for the book, [U] for the personal link.

9.6 Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971). A very large topical anthology from the Schuon circle, arranged by theme, the closest thing to "Huxley done at three times the scale and with a Traditionalist spine". 9.11 What this means for the essay. Alex's instinct is sound on method: a reader who meets only Huxley sees the perennial philosophy as a nice anthology, and a reader who meets Guenon, Schuon, Coomaraswamy and Smith sees a long argument among serious authors, including an argument about whether the self returns. That is a better witness for his claim that the traditions speak of the same thing in different words, because the authors themselves argue about which words are right. The safest framing: name two or three of them, say they split on rebirth, and then place the axiom above the split (the whole lives all lives). Do not call any of them "the best"; it is not a claim anyone here can support.
