## 1. The picture in brief

After Blavatsky died in 1891 her movement split into four kinds of writer, and they compare religions in four different ways.

The colleagues who kept her words (Judge, then Tingley at Point Loma, Crosbie's United Lodge, Purucker) wrote plain handbooks of her system. They compare by repeating her equivalences and by stressing karma and rebirth.

The Adyar school (Besant, Leadbeater, Jinarajadasa, Hodson) added claimed clairvoyant observation as a second witness, and ranked maps of the path (probation, five initiations). They lay one list of terms over each religion and read each religion as the public face of an inner teaching.

The scholars and translators (Olcott on Buddhism, Subba Row on Vedanta, Mead on Gnostic and Hermetic texts) compare most checkably, because they work from named texts.

The heirs and dissenters (Steiner, Bailey, Krishnamurti) kept the frame and changed the centre: Christ, the Hierarchy, or nothing at all.

All of them share one frame. There is one boundless source. Each soul is a pilgrim who descends into matter, lives many lives under a law of consequence, and returns. That is close to the three clauses of Alex's axiom (one whole, it experiences separation, the separation ends). It is the nearest precedent in English for his structure.

Three cautions. Much of the Adyar layer rests on observation nobody can repeat. Specialists keep apart what these writers equate. The perennialist school (Guenon, and in his wake Coomaraswamy and Schuon) attacked Theosophy, so presenting both as one family would be wrong. ## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

One. Start from a master list and find each item in each religion. Besant's Seven Principles of Man (1892) gives Sanskrit names (Atma, Buddhi, Manas, Kama, Prana and two bodies) and lines them up with Christian spirit, soul and body and the Kabbalah's soul names. Specialists note that Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold scheme and that Buddhi is the discriminating faculty, not a "spiritual soul".

Two. Match by position in a sequence. Four Buddhist stages become the first four initiations. The gospel life becomes five. The Advaita fourfold means become four qualifications. Order is taken to prove identity.

Three. Match by number: Trinity, Trimurti and Osiris, Isis and Horus; seven principles with seven rays, holy immortals, planes, heavens. This is the weakest step. [Checker: the Zoroastrian count is six, or seven with Ahura Mazda.]

Four. Treat the public religion as the veil of an inner one. Besant's Esoteric Christianity and Mead's Gnosis both say the creed is the outer face of the mysteries. This lets any difference count as agreement, since the outer is never the real. It cannot be refuted, which is its strength and its flaw. [Checker: this is also the move Schuon makes with the exoteric and the esoteric; see section 9. It is the version of the move that Alex's own rule for Part two (public face and inner face) most resembles, and it needs the same care.]

Five. Explain differences by four devices. Each Great Teacher adapts to a people and an age (Besant). Priests and councils corrupted the teaching (Judge, Besant, with the 553 and 869 stories). Different temperaments need different religions (Hodson, Bailey's rays). Different words for the same item (the Glossaries of Blavatsky, edited by Mead (1892), and of Purucker).

Six. Add observation. Leadbeater, Besant and Hodson say trained clairvoyants confirm what scriptures hint at (Occult Chemistry, 1908, is the famous case). This turns a textual comparison into a report that only the reporters can check.

Seven. Negotiate common ground. Olcott's Fourteen Propositions (1891) are the one case where authorities of two schools were asked to sign.

Eight. Translate and edit the sources. Mead on the Hermetic and Gnostic texts, Subba Row on Vedanta, Judge on the Gita and Olcott's Catechism work from named texts. They are the most checkable and the most useful to Alex.

What Alex's axiom does differently: it begins with a premise, asks what follows, shows each tradition's own wording, and does not claim identity. Seven and eight are worth copying. Four, used as a rule, is the one to avoid.

## 5. What scholars say

From memory, [M] unless marked.

- Campbell (Ancient Wisdom Revived, 1980) is the standard history. Washington (Madame Blavatsky's Baboon, 1993) is sceptical. Hanegraaff (1996, 2012) places Theosophy in Western esotericism and the New Age. Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, Brill 2001; paperback 2004 [U]) studies its claim to knowledge by direct observation.
- Olcott: Prothero (The White Buddhist, 1996). Gombrich and Obeyesekere (Buddhism Transformed, 1988) develop "Protestant Buddhism" (a term Obeyesekere coined in 1970) and place Olcott's catechisms and schools in it.
- Besant: Nethercot (1960, 1963) and Taylor (1992) are the biographies.
- Leadbeater: Tillett, The Elder Brother (1982). Historians treat his reports as unverifiable.
- Mead: respected as an editor, dated as a translator. The Hermetica now rests on Nock and Festugiere (1945 to 1954) and Copenhaver (1992), who read the texts as Greek philosophical and religious writing of the early centuries CE, and on Fowden (1986), who stresses the Egyptian setting and a lived "way of Hermes"; none finds a secret ancient doctrine in the Theosophical sense. Nag Hammadi (found 1945) transformed Gnostic studies, and the label "Gnosticism" is now contested (Williams 1996, King 2003). [Checker, change 19a.]
- Mystery religions: Burkert (Ancient Mystery Cults, 1987) shows they were rites and experience without systematic doctrine, which weakens the claim that they taught rebirth and oneness as a creed.
- Perennialists: Guenon's Le Theosophisme (1921) called Theosophy a pseudo-religion; Coomaraswamy and Schuon shared the hostility [M for Guenon, U for the other two on Theosophy specifically]. ## 9. [Checker, new] Perennial philosophy beyond Huxley: a pointer list, memory only

The name and the idea
- Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), the first use of the phrase; Leibniz used it in a letter to Nicolas Remond (26 August 1714) [M, per the P01 and P08 sibling notes, which cite an earlier session]. - Charles Schmitt, "Perennial Philosophy: From Agostino Steuco to Leibniz" (Journal of the History of Ideas, 1966) is the standard scholarly history of the term [U on the exact title and year].

The Traditionalist school (the core of the perennialist movement in the strict sense; not Theosophists, and hostile to them)
- Rene Guenon, Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921) and Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921). Good for: the claim of a single primordial tradition, and the criticism of Theosophy that Alex's essay should acknowledge.

- Frithjof Schuon, De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English as The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953). Good for: the exoteric and esoteric distinction, which is the strongest worked version of "the religions differ in public form and agree at the inner level". - Titus Burckhardt and Martin Lings: on Sufism and sacred art; Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981); Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a very large thematic anthology that is a rival in kind to Huxley's, from the Schuon circle.
- Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), the history of Traditionalism, for the critical view.

Other perennialists
- Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth: The Primordial Tradition (1976). Good for: a clear, readable version of the "levels of reality" idea for a general reader, taken largely from Schuon.
- Ken Wilber, The Atman Project (1980) and later works. Good for: stages of growth with a perennialist frame (this is closest to Alex's arrow). Contested.
- Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945): an anthology with his commentary, the movement's popularizer. Useful as an entry for readers. It is one voice, it is not the rigorous one, and the essay should not stand on it.
