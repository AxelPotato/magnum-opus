## 1. The picture in brief

"Theosophy" names three different things, and Alex's essay needs to keep them apart.

First, an old word. Theosophia is Greek for divine wisdom. It turns up in late antiquity. From the seventeenth century it named a school of speculative Christian thought around Jakob Boehme, carried on by William Law, Oetinger, Saint-Martin and Swedenborg's followers. Scholars still call Kabbalah and the Shi'a hikma tradition "theosophies" in that technical sense.

Second, a society. In 1875 in New York, Helena Blavatsky, Henry Olcott, William Judge and others founded the Theosophical Society. It drew on Spiritualism, Mesmerism, Freemasonry, Hermetic and Rosicrucian literature, Kabbalah, Emerson's circle and then Indian texts. Blavatsky said her Theosophy was the Wisdom-Religion, the one source of all religions, and said the name came from the third century school of Ammonius Saccas. Historians treat that genealogy as a nineteenth century reconstruction.

Third, a way of knowing: teaching said to come from living Masters.

The perennial philosophy shares the family idea (one core under many religions) and uses another method: anthology, metaphysical exposition and initiation inside an existing tradition. Theosophy was one of the main organised Western carriers of the common-core idea and one main route by which English readers met karma, rebirth and the unity of life (R1: the number "millions" and the superlative are removed). The Traditionalists (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) were cousins who disowned it: Guenon wrote a whole book against it in 1921, and Coomaraswamy had passed through it as a young man [C for 1907].

The two agree in outline on one Principle, a soul identical with it, many levels, the world as veil, religions as forms and liberation as return. They diverge on reincarnation, initiation, orthodoxy, Masters, and progress against decline. For Alex: Blavatsky's 1888 third proposition is a close forerunner of his three-clause axiom (finding 7), with the softer step noted in C17.

The perennial philosophy is not one book and not Huxley's book: its twentieth-century core is a school of several authors who began before him (Guenon 1921) and went on after him (Schuon 1948, Perry 1971, Smith 1976, Nasr 1981). See section 9.

## 2. Findings

1. The word, and Blavatsky's story of it
Claim: theosophia is a Greek compound (theos, sophia), divine wisdom or wisdom about the divine. Late antique use is real but I cannot give a locator; the Tubingen Theosophy (an anthology of pagan oracles and sayings compiled by a Christian, about 500, so "late fifth century") is a later example [M, about 80 percent]. Lutheran mystical writers adopted the word, and Gichtel's Amsterdam edition of Boehme (1682) was titled Theosophia revelata [M].
Who: Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy (1889), opening section (I recall its title as "Theosophy and the Theosophical Society", with the sub-heading on the meaning of the name). Gist: Theosophy is divine knowledge, not a new religion. The word goes back to the third century and the Alexandrian school of Ammonius Saccas, whose followers were called lovers of truth (Philaletheians), Analogeticists (some editions: Analogists) and Eclectic Theosophists [M for the gist, [U] for the section number and for the spelling "Analogeticists"].
Her further claim: Diogenes Laertius traces a theosophic system to an Egyptian priest called Pot-Amun [U]. What Diogenes Laertius, Lives, Prologue 21, does say is that Potamo of Alexandria introduced an eclectic school a short time ago [M]. Scholars read Potamo as a Greek name, so the Egyptian etymology is hers [M].
Parallels: Neoplatonic, Egyptian, Hindu.
Tier: [M] and [U]. Use: "an old Greek word for divine wisdom" is safe. Do not repeat Pot-Amun.

2. Ammonius Saccas
Claim: Ammonius (died about 242, Alexandria, nicknamed Saccas) taught Plotinus for about eleven years from 232 [M]. He wrote nothing. Porphyry reports that Plotinus, Herennius and an Origen (the pagan Platonist, not the Christian) agreed not to divulge his teachings, which later readers took as a sign of a secret doctrine [M].
Who: Porphyry, Life of Plotinus, section 3 [M]. Hierocles of Alexandria, through Photius, calls him theodidaktos (God-taught) and says he reconciled Plato and Aristotle [M].
Blavatsky's picture of an eclectic reconciler of all religions comes from eighteenth century church history (Mosheim) and from her friend Alexander Wilder, who edited Isis Unveiled [M]. Historians say little is known of his teaching and that "Neoplatonism" is a modern label.
Parallels: Greek, Egyptian, Christian, reconciled by one man.
Tier: [M]. Use: if Ammonius appears, say "Blavatsky's story of him". For a verified Neoplatonic witness use Plotinus himself (finding 20).

3. Boehme and Christian theosophy
Claim: Boehme (1575 to 1624, a shoemaker of Gorlitz) wrote Aurora (1612), Mysterium Magnum (1623), De signatura rerum (1622) and The Way to Christ (1624). His God is an abyss (the Ungrund, the "unground") that becomes manifest to itself through will, opposition and nature. The soul's task is to return [M for the gist; wording and locators [U]].
Followers: the Philadelphian Society (Jane Leade, London, 1694), William Law (1686 to 1761; the "Law edition" of Boehme in English, 1764 to 1781, was published after his death and edited by Ward and Langcake from older translations, C1), Oetinger, Baader, Saint-Martin; Hegel and Schelling read him [M].
Scholarly frame: Antoine Faivre calls this "Christian theosophy", speculative knowledge of God, nature and the human being in one scheme [M].
Parallels: Christian, Kabbalistic, Paracelsian.
Tier: [M]. Use: a Western precedent for a whole that reveals itself through opposition. Check a source before any sentence about Boehme goes in.

4. Swedenborg
Claim: Swedenborg (1688 to 1772) taught correspondences between the visible and spiritual worlds, which is a way of reading scripture and nature as symbol, close to Blavatsky's "Analogeticists". He described heaven as a Grand Man (Maximus Homo, Homo Maximus) in which each soul and community has a place like an organ [M for the idea; locator: Heaven and Hell (1758), about sections 59 to 102, [U]].
He rejected reincarnation: one earthly life, then development in the afterlife [M].
His followers formed a "Theosophical Society" in London in 1783 [U].
Emerson wrote on him (Representative Men, 1850) [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: the Grand Man is a Western picture very near Alex's fingers of one hand. His denial of rebirth shows the Christian theosophical stream did not supply the reincarnation in the Theosophical package.

5. Saint-Martin and Pasqually: fall and reintegration
Claim: Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin (1743 to 1803), "the Unknown Philosopher" (his own pen name), wrote Des erreurs et de la verite (1775), Le Tableau naturel (1782), L'Homme de desir (1790), and translated Boehme into French [M]. His teacher Martines de Pasqually (died 1774) left a Traite de la reintegration des etres (written about 1770, printed 1899), about spirits fallen into matter and their return [M]. The Elus Coens and later Martinism came from this.
Parallels: Christian, Kabbalistic, Hermetic.
Tier: [M]. Use: "reintegration" is an old Western word for the axiom's third clause (the separation ends).

16. Where the two movements agree
In outline [M]: (a) one ultimate Principle beyond the world (Parabrahman for Blavatsky; the Absolute, "Beyond-Being" for the Traditionalists); (b) the human core is not other than it (third proposition, with C17; the second clause of Huxley's Foreword definition, "similar to, or even identical with"; Schuon; Coomaraswamy's one Self) (C5); (c) reality has levels (planes; states of being); (d) the world is veil or maya; (e) religions are forms of one truth, with an inner and an outer side; (f) liberation is return, by knowledge and conduct; (g) modern materialism misses all of it; (h) both take the Hindu cyclic time scheme (yugas, a present dark age) seriously. As for a hidden spiritual centre, this is Blavatsky's (the Masters) and Guenon's (Agarttha), not a shared doctrine of the two movements, and Guenon denied the likeness (C4, R2).

17. Where they diverge
(a) Reincarnation. Theosophy teaches repeated rebirth of the higher individuality (Blavatsky's Isis Unveiled, 1877, rejected the Spiritist version; by the 1880s she taught it; scholars note the shift) [M]. Guenon rejected it as an error [M]; the Traditionalists are not one voice here: Coomaraswamy and Schuon treat Hindu and Buddhist rebirth as the teaching of those traditions and not as the return of an individual [U]. Swedenborg also rejected it (finding 4).
(b) Initiation. The Society had open membership with a later Esoteric Section (1888) and graded "initiations" under Besant and Leadbeater; the Traditionalists require regular transmission in an existing tradition [M].
(c) Orthodoxy. Theosophy: "no religion higher than truth", with no need to belong to any religion (the motto is the Benares family's, adopted around 1880 [C]; it is not a Blavatsky saying). Traditionalists: a person needs a real tradition and its forms [M].
(d) Masters against lineages. Theosophy claims living Mahatmas as source; Traditionalists claim authority from actual lineages (Guenon's Shadhili initiation of 1912, Schuon's Maryamiyya of 1965, a branch of the Alawiyya) and from intellectual intuition [M] (A2).
(e) Direction of history. Theosophy: evolution of souls and races; Traditionalists: cyclic decline, and Perry's The Widening Breach (1995) attacks evolutionism [M].
(f) Attitude to modernity. Theosophy sought a synthesis of science, religion and philosophy (the subtitle of The Secret Doctrine); Traditionalism is anti-modern [M].
Tier: [M]. Use: where Alex says "perennial philosophy and Theosophy," he is joining two groups whose members would object to each other. A one-line note will do.

## 4. How they compare

Blavatsky. She claims one archaic source and then looks for its traces. Method: (a) parallel cosmogonies (The Secret Doctrine sets "stanzas" beside commentary drawn from the Puranas, Kabbalah, Chaldean, Egyptian, Greek, Gnostic and Christian texts); (b) analogy and correspondence, reading myths as the soul's events (the "Analogeticists" idea); (c) etymology and symbol; (d) citation of Orientalist scholarship when it helps and attack when it does not [M]. She explains differences as corruption by priests and literalists and as deliberate "blinds" (veils) by the initiates [M]. Authority rests on the Masters and her own inner knowledge. Olav Hammer (Claiming Knowledge, 2001) names three strategies in this literature: appeal to tradition, appeal to experience, and scientism [M].

Besant's Four Great Religions (1897) and Esoteric Christianity (1901) read each creed as a veiled form of one plan [M].

The Traditionalists. Start from a metaphysical principle, then show that each authentic tradition states it in its own terms. Coomaraswamy's way is dense equations across Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and German terms with footnotes (for example, Eckhart's Godhead beside Brahman) [M]. Differences are explained as different forms revealed for different peoples, each valid and each aiming at the same summit; the unity is at the formless level, not in history [M]. No claim of a single historical source.

Huxley's way (one line, for contrast): anthology by theme, read side by side, the "Highest Common Factor in all preceding and subsequent theologies" (his Foreword, as I recall it, A3); differences put down to temperament (his chapter "Religion and Temperament") and the ineffability of the Reality [M, phrases from memory, not for quotation]. A practical weakness of the anthology method as a source for Alex: Huxley read the mystics in English translations of his day (for Eckhart, the usual source behind such anthologies is Pfeiffer's 1857 edition, which mixes authentic and doubtful texts), so a line that reaches the essay by way of Huxley has to be checked against a critical edition (P07, from memory) [M for the Pfeiffer problem, [U] for which translation Huxley used].

Campbell: comparative mythology, with Jungian archetypes and Bastian's elementary ideas as the cause of sameness [M]. Wilber: a map of levels (the Great Nest) and a theory of knowing that tries to make the claim testable [M]. Smith: a phenomenology of each religion, then a primordial scheme of levels of reality and of selfhood [M].

The contrast: Blavatsky's explanation is historical (one source, then corruption); Schuon's is metaphysical (one Reality, many forms); Campbell's is psychological. Each is a claim by the comparer, and not by the traditions. Alex's own is a fourth: common logic (the agreement follows from one axiom), as P01 already notes.

## 5. What scholars say

Hanegraaff, Godwin, Bevir and Campbell (Bruce, the historian, not Joseph) place Theosophy in a long line of Western esotericism and note that it tied that line to Asian texts for the first time at scale. They treat the Masters as the modern form of the old "Unknown Superiors" and the "Stanzas of Dzyan" as unverifiable [M].
Richard Hodgson's 1885 report to the Society for Psychical Research judged Blavatsky's phenomena fraudulent; Vernon Harrison's 1986 paper in the SPR Journal (JSPR 53, pp. 286 to 310) argued the report was flawed [C, repo notes; the SPR's own later statement is not used]. William Emmette Coleman (1895) catalogued sources she drew on (an appendix to Solovyoff's A Modern Priestess of Isis) [M]. K. Paul Johnson argues the Masters had historical models; this is contested [M].
Sedgwick (2004) shows Traditionalism as a modern movement with an anti-modern message, and shows how much it owed to the occult milieu it condemned [M]. On the common-core idea itself, Steven Katz (1978, aimed in part at Stace's Mysticism and Philosophy, 1960) and the constructivists say mystical experience is shaped by its tradition; Robert Forman (1990), John Hick and Huston Smith ("Is There a Perennial Philosophy?", 1987) defend modest versions (as the repo notes already record) [M] (C14).
On India: Theosophy is credited with stirring pride in Hindu and Buddhist heritage, and criticised for helping shape a "Protestant Buddhism" and a Hinduism fitted to Western taste [M].
Overall: influential, with a mixed reputation.

- Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971): a very large cross-tradition anthology and the school's counterpart to Huxley; The Widening Breach (1995) against evolutionism.
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981, the Edinburgh Gifford Lectures). Use: the school's case against the modern worldview.
- Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976; subtitle The Primordial Tradition) and The World's Religions (first published 1958 as The Religions of Man); "Is There a Perennial Philosophy?" (1987). Use: levels of reality and of selfhood; the most readable map for a general reader. "Primordial tradition" is Guenon's phrase, not Smith's invention.
- Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945; British edition 1946). Use: the easiest door; chapter 1 "That Art Thou" for the first clause of the axiom; the Foreword's three-clause definition as an outline. Do not use him as the movement's yardstick.
- Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926; English 1932), which sets Eckhart beside Shankara. Early comparers outside the schools.
- W. T. Stace, The Teachings of the Mystics and Mysticism and Philosophy (both 1960): the academic form of "mystics agree"; the target of Katz (1978).
- Henry Corbin and Mircea Eliade (Eliade's 1979 essay on "theosophia perennis", C10): the scholarly, non-Traditionalist cousins; Corbin on Shi'a hikma, a technical "theosophy".
- Other comparers whom a reader will meet: S. Radhakrishnan (for example Eastern Religions and Western Thought, 1939), Swami Vivekananda (the 1893 Parliament), Alan Watts (The Supreme Identity, 1950; The Book, 1966), Ken Wilber (The Spectrum of Consciousness, 1977; The Atman Project, 1980), Joseph Campbell (comparative mythology). Several came through Theosophical doors (finding 13). Wilber is a popular synthesiser, to be cited as a map and not as a witness.
- Guides to the school: Harry Oldmeadow, Traditionalism: Religion in the Light of the Perennial Philosophy (2000), sympathetic; Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), critical.

Where each helps the axiom (my mapping): one whole appearing as many (Schuon, Coomaraswamy, Smith; Blavatsky's third proposition for the Theosophical form); the veil and why it feels real (Smith, Schuon, Huxley); stages and an arrow of growth (Wilber, and the traditions' own ladders, for example Sadra's four journeys); lifetimes and karma (not the Traditionalists, who reject the Theosophical form: use the Gita, the Milindapanha, Plato, and Theosophy's own books, with the Guenon caveat); the honest objections (Katz, Forman, Sedgwick, Hanegraaff, Prothero).

One true thing to say in the essay, in a sentence: the perennial philosophy is a family of related claims with real disagreements inside it (the Traditionalists and the Theosophists disagree about rebirth, initiation, Masters and progress), not one doctrine and not one book.
