## 1. The picture in brief

Theosophy built one of the most elaborate numbered maps of stages in the modern West (corrected from "the largest"), and it scales outward in three layers. The first is cosmic time: a Great Breath, with universes that appear and vanish through manvantara and pralaya, so that even a universe is an episode. The second is a stack of scales of being: person, planet, sun, cosmos, each with its own conscious Life (a planetary Logos, a solar Logos, a cosmic level above), each body made of the lives of the level below. The third is the Path: a person climbs from probation and discipleship through initiations to the Master, who faces a choice between private bliss and staying to help.

The sources layer, and the layering matters. Blavatsky (1888 to 1891) supplies the Breath, the cycles, the chain of globes and rounds, the Dhyani hierarchy, three Logoi, the Voice of the Silence and the Great Renunciation, building on Sinnett and the Mahatma Letters (about 1880 to 1884 or 1885, published 1923). Besant and Leadbeater (about 1895 to 1925) supply the named seven planes, the planetary and solar Logos hierarchy, the five initiations with Pali, Hindu and Christian labels, and the clairvoyant root-race history. Bailey (1922 to 1949, with posthumous volumes to 1960) supplies nine initiations, the cosmic planes and the paths beyond the Master.

The logic fits Alex's axiom closely: the whole goes down into separation, turns, and returns with something gained. The numbers do not fit his arrow. Theosophy has no speed, no galaxy rung of its own and no minus four to plus four. The mapping to run, bike, car, fly, orbit and escape velocity is his, and the essay should say so. The system also carries root races, which scholars treat as its most problematic part. The essay should leave them out.

Use: one growth path from the lowest form to the highest being, no outside grace. It sits well beside "the whole experiencing itself through individual lives."

2. The Great Breath, manvantara and pralaya
Claim: She calls the periodic out-breath and in-breath of the unmanifest the Great Breath. A manvantara is a period of manifestation, a pralaya a period of rest, and she applies both at every scale: a round, a chain, a solar system, a universe. The numbers are Puranic. A day of Brahma (kalpa) is 1,000 mahayugas, 4.32 billion years. A manvantara is 71 mahayugas, about 306.7 million years; fourteen plus their fifteen junctions (each a krita-yuga length, 0.4 of a mahayuga, so 6 in all) fill a kalpa (14 x 71 + 6 = 1,000). The life of Brahma is 100 divine years, 311.04 trillion years. The Puranas list four dissolutions: occasional (naimittika), elemental (prakritika), final or absolute (atyantika, individual liberation) and continual (nitya).
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, Proem and Stanzas I to III (the Eternal Parent sleeping seven eternities, the lotus bud swelling). The Puranic scheme: the Vishnu Purana (Wilson's English version, 1840, which she read) and the Bhagavata Purana (not translated by Wilson; her route to it is [U], perhaps Burnouf's French). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu cosmology chiefly; Buddhist kalpas.
Tier: [M] for numbers (arithmetic checked by me) and the four dissolutions' Sanskrit names; Stanza wording [U]; the English words for the four [U]. My recall places "the Eternal Parent ... slumbered once again for seven eternities" in Stanza I and the lotus bud in Stanza III [M].
Use: a safe source for "even plus four may be a stage." A whole universe is one breath, followed by another. Alex's multiverse sentence has an older precedent, in time rather than space. The Puranas also have the plural-in-space version (many universes at once, each with its own Brahma; finding 16e), so both precedents exist.

A third volume (1897) was assembled by Besant from her papers; some Theosophical bodies do not accept it as her own arrangement (change 9).

Words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for structure, the seven and twelve, and vol. 3 in 1897 under Besant; [U] for Stanza titles, the 1893 revised edition and the 1897 reissue.

4. Descent and ascent: the chain of globes and the Rounds (the axiom in cosmic form)
Claim: The human Monad (Atma with Buddhi) travels a chain of seven globes (A to G); one circuit is a Round. Globes A, B, C are the arc of descent (spirit into matter), D, our Earth, is the lowest point, and E, F, G the arc of ascent. There are seven Rounds, and we are in the fourth, past its midpoint, the turning point. The return is not a repeat of the start: the being comes back a conscious god where it began an unconscious one. Atma is universal and not anyone's possession, so the separateness is in the vehicles.
Who: the scheme appears in the Mahatma Letters (private, 1881 to 1883) and in Sinnett's Theosophist articles (1881 onward, [U]), and in book form in Sinnett, Esoteric Buddhism (1883); the Mahatma Letters (about 1880 to 1884 or 1885) were published in 1923. Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, widens it. Atma as universal: The Key to Theosophy (1889), locator unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu going out and returning (pravritti, nivritti). Neoplatonic procession and return (Plotinus, Proclus) is a comparer's pairing, not hers.
Tier: [M] for scheme and book attribution; [U] for "first published" in the magazine.
Use: the nearest nineteenth-century cosmic version of Alex's three clauses: one whole, separation, return. Return with consciousness gained answers "why separate at all."

5. Planes and principles: hers and the later list
Claim: The septenary man and seven planes entered Theosophy in 1881 to 1883 through Sinnett and the Mahatma Letters. Isis Unveiled (1877) had a threefold man. In The Secret Doctrine she uses seven principles and planes with cautions that they are states, not places. The standard named list (physical, astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic or anupadaka, logoic or adi) is the Besant and Leadbeater systematisation, in its final form Leadbeater's (change 11). The buddhic plane is called the plane of unity, where the sense of separation drops away.
Who: Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897, older names for the top planes, [U]); Leadbeater, Man Visible and Invisible (1902) and A Textbook of Theosophy (1912). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels the comparers draw: Vedantic sheaths (five koshas), lokas, the four worlds of Kabbalah and of Sufism, Neoplatonic hypostases. Guenon argued Theosophy misread the Vedantic constitution of man and the sheaths (Le Theosophisme, 1921; L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta, 1925 [U for the sheath passage]); L'Erreur spirite (1923) is against Spiritism and reincarnationism.

6. Root races and sub-races
Claim: Volume 2 teaches seven root races per globe period, each with seven sub-races. The first two are ethereal, the Third (Lemurian) gets mind and solid bodies, the Fourth (Atlantean) is the peak of materiality, the Fifth (Aryan) is ours, two more follow. Later writers made it concrete: Scott-Elliot (The Story of Atlantis, 1896; The Lost Lemuria, 1904) mapped continents; Besant and Leadbeater (Man: Whence, How and Whither, 1913) gave a clairvoyant history and a coming sixth sub-race.
Problem: the scheme ranks peoples. Blavatsky treats some living peoples as degenerate remnants fated to die out and puts the Aryan highest among the living. Defenders say root races are stages every soul passes through, and note the Society's first object, brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour (the wording changed over the years [U]). Critics say the hierarchy stands in the text regardless.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 2; Scott-Elliot; Besant and Leadbeater. Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Hindu yugas, Hesiod's ages (loosely).
Tier: [M] for content and debate; every locator [U].
Use: leave it out. If Theosophy is introduced as a stage-mapper, one sentence naming this as the part scholars find hardest to defend would be honest.

7. Hierarchies and Logoi: Blavatsky, then Besant and Leadbeater
Claim: Between the Absolute and humanity Blavatsky places Dhyan-Chohans, planetary spirits and builders, who work by law and are not personal gods; some are said to be perfected humanity of earlier cycles. She uses "Logos" at three levels: unmanifest, the Spirit-Matter pair, and manifested universal Mind. The Vedantic scaffolding (Parabrahman, Mulaprakriti, Logos, Pranava) came from T. Subba Row's Bhagavad Gita lectures (delivered in 1886, printed 1887 [U]). Besant and Leadbeater then built tiers: a Solar Logos (the conscious life of a solar system); seven Planetary Logoi, read into the "seven spirits before the throne" of Revelation; for Earth, a Lord of the World (Sanat Kumara, the Ancient of Days) heading a Hierarchy of Manu, Bodhisattva or World Teacher, Mahachohan, the ray Chohans and the Masters; and a cosmic level left in shadow.
Who: Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1; Subba Row; Besant, The Ancient Wisdom (1897); Leadbeater, A Textbook of Theosophy (1912) and The Masters and the Path (1925). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Greek Logos (Heraclitus, Stoics, Philo, John 1), Hindu Ishvara and Om, Daniel 7, Revelation 1:4, Hindu Sanat Kumara (Chandogya Upanishad, chapter 7, where Sanatkumara teaches Narada [M]).
Tier: [M] for structure and sources; "perfected humanity of earlier cycles" as her own wording [U] (the idea is also in the Mahatma Letters and Esoteric Buddhism [U]); whether Sanat Kumara equals the Planetary Logos [U] (the authors differ).
Use: this is the planetary, solar, cosmic ladder in plain form, and it is mostly the later layer. Do not write "she says" for the tiers.

8. Bailey: the Heavenly Man and the cosmic planes
Claim: Alice Bailey (1880 to 1949), writing for "the Tibetan" (Djwhal Khul), held that a planet's Logos is a "Heavenly Man" whose cells are humans, and that our seven planes are the seven sub-planes of one cosmic physical plane, with six higher cosmic planes above (cosmic astral, mental, buddhic, atmic, monadic, logoic). The pattern repeats at each scale. The Solar Logos draws on stars such as Sirius, the Pleiades and the Great Bear.
Who: Bailey, A Treatise on Cosmic Fire (1925); Initiation, Human and Solar (1922). Locators unknown.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Kabbalah (Adam Kadmon, also in The Secret Doctrine), Hermetic "as above, so below."
Tier: [M] for structure; star details [U]. Critics have flagged passages about Jews in her texts [M that the criticism exists, U for the passages]: check before any citation.
Use: the strongest statement I know of a self-similar ladder, each scale's whole being one plane of a larger scale. It resembles Alex's small square inside a 1,000 by 1,000 map. The likeness is structural only.

Besant formalised a Probationary Path and a Path of Discipleship. At the Feet of the Master (1910) gave four qualifications: discrimination, desirelessness, good conduct (six sub-qualities) and love. The first three adapt the Vedantic four means (viveka, vairagya, shatsampatti, mumukshutva, from the Vivekachudamani), with love replacing the desire for release. My recall of the six: self-control as to the mind, self-control in action, tolerance, cheerfulness, one-pointedness, confidence [M, same recall as the colleague's count].
Who: as named; Besant, The Path of Discipleship (four lectures at the Adyar convention in December 1895, published 1896 [U]). At the Feet of the Master is credited to "Alcyone" (the young J. Krishnamurti) with Leadbeater's help; authorship is disputed.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Advaita (viveka, vairagya).
Tier: [M] for books and qualifications; Besant's date and the authorship [U].

10. The Voice of the Silence: Three Halls and Seven Portals
Claim: The Voice (1889), which she says she translated from The Book of the Golden Precepts, has three fragments. In the first the pupil crosses three Halls: Ignorance (avidya), Learning (a serpent under every flower), and Wisdom, beyond which lies the shoreless ocean of the imperishable; the text speaks of passing through three states into a fourth and thence into seven worlds. In the third he enters seven portals, each a virtue: dana (giving), shila (harmony in word and act), kshanti (patience), viraga (indifference to pleasure and pain), virya (dauntless energy), dhyana (meditation), prajna (wisdom, which makes a Bodhisattva). The Mahayana has six perfections; the Voice inserts viraga as the fourth.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (1889), Fragments I and III. T03 treats the text. The find-handles in changes 17 and 18 are my recall of the verses, not for printing.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels: Mahayana paramitas (six standard, ten in some lists [M]).
Tier: [M] for the portals and the Halls' order (my recall agrees, not verification); exact wording [U].
Use: a seven-step map whose fourth step is indifference to pleasure and pain is the nearest older text to letting go as the hinge of the path. Caution: the seven are not a standard Buddhist list, and viraga is a Buddhist term but not a paramita (T03).

11. The Two Paths: Great Renunciation and Nirmanakaya
Claim: Fragment II ("The Two Paths") and the close of Fragment III set a choice at the end (locator corrected, change 14, [U]). One way is private liberation into nirvana (Dharmakaya), tied to the Pratyeka-Buddha, whom she paints as self-regarding. The other is the "Great Renunciation": the adept who could enter nirvana declines, keeps a Nirmanakaya ("transformation body") and stays near humanity, adding to a protective "Guardian Wall" [U]. The voice of compassion (my recall: the end of Fragment III) asks whether there can be bliss while all that lives must suffer. The Theosophical Glossary (1892, ed. Mead) defines the Nirmanakaya this way.
Who: Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence, Fragments II and III; The Theosophical Glossary.
Words: paraphrase.
Parallels she cites: the Mahayana bodhisattva vow (Shantideva), and the Buddha's "Great Renunciation" (leaving the palace) reused with a new meaning.
Tier: [M] for the content and the choice; [U] for the fragment locator and "Guardian Wall".
Scholars: in Buddhist usage nirmanakaya is a Buddha's visible emanation body (Shakyamuni is one), not an adept who stays on, and the Pali pratyekabuddha is a solitary awakened one, not a villain. ## 4. How they compare: the method

Blavatsky uses five moves. All are [M].

First, genealogy. All religions descend from one archaic teaching kept by initiates. Agreement is inheritance, difference is loss: symbols read literally, keys lost, priestcraft. Her Preface says she brings only the string that ties a nosegay of flowers gathered by others (a line she takes from Montaigne, Essais III.12; The Secret Doctrine, vol. 1, Preface, about p. vii [M for the lines, U for the page]; note that Florio's English Montaigne says "thread", T02). Alex can borrow the stance: he gathers and does not claim to discover.

Second, the vocabulary list. She sets terms side by side across languages: Atma, Buddhi, Manas with Greek and Hebrew counterparts; Logos with Ishvara; Nirvana with Moksha. The Glossary is that table alphabetised.

Third, the template. She and her heirs impose a sevenfold shape on every source: five Hindu sheaths become seven principles, six Mahayana perfections become seven portals, Puranic cycles become seven Rounds. Here comparers and scholars object most: the fit comes from the template, and the sources are bent to it.

Fourth, one label per tradition for each rung. Besant and Leadbeater write one initiation as Sotapanna, Parivrajaka and Birth at once. The reader feels three religions agree, though the three lists differ in length and meaning and the Theosophists cut and joined them.

Fifth, exoteric against esoteric: the popular form hides the inner form, so apparent contradictions are veils. Later writers add claimed observation, clairvoyant investigation (Besant and Leadbeater, Occult Chemistry), which turns a claim into a report that cannot be checked from outside.

For Alex the contrast is plain. He claims no original, no template and no clairvoyant source. He starts from one axiom and shows which parts of each tradition follow. That is a different method from the later Theosophists, who report from observation, and it is also different from every school in the perennial philosophy movement: the Traditionalists (Guénon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) argue from metaphysical doctrine and a claimed primordial tradition, and the experience line (James, Underhill, Stace) argues from the reports of mystics (corrected from "closer to the Perennialists who argue from metaphysics", which treated the movement as one school; see section 9). [M]

## 5. What scholars say

- Sources of The Secret Doctrine: William Emmette Coleman (1895, in Solovyoff, A Modern Priestess of Isis, in Walter Leaf's translation) traced large parts to printed works of her day (Wilson's Vishnu Purana, Winchell's World-Life, Donnelly's Atlantis, Jacolliot). No Book of Dzyan has been found. Godwin (1994) and Hanegraaff (1996) read her system as a nineteenth-century creation from Western esotericism, Romantic nature philosophy, evolutionism and Orientalist readings of India, not a transmission. [M]
- Historical weight: Campbell (1980) and Hammer (2001, with a later paperback printing [U]) take Theosophy as a main source of modern ideas of karma, reincarnation, ascended masters and the New Age, whatever one judges true. Chajes (2019) shows Blavatsky's own view of reincarnation shifting between 1877 and the 1880s. [M]
- Buddhism: Prothero (The White Buddhist, 1996) shows the Society's Buddhism, through Olcott, as a Victorian, Protestant-flavoured composite; Lopez (Prisoners of Shangri-La, 1998) shows Tibet and the Mahatmas as partly a Western fantasy (reattributed, change 15). [M]
- Root races: Goodrick-Clarke (1985) shows the scheme taken into racist Ariosophy (List, Lanz von Liebenfels). Historians debate how far this implicates Theosophy itself; none says Theosophy is Nazi. Ramaswamy (2004) follows Lemuria from a zoologist's land bridge of 1864 (Sclater) through Haeckel to Theosophy; plate tectonics removed the geological basis. [M]
- Traditionalists: Sedgwick (2004) reports that Guenon attacked Theosophy (Le Theosophisme, 1921; L'Erreur spirite, 1923) as a pseudo-religion mixing the psychic with the spiritual. The Traditionalist school he founded, with Coomaraswamy and Schuon, was among Theosophy's sharpest critics (corrected from "the perennialists most respected in the field", which is a judgement and covers the whole movement; other authors were not hostile in the same way). [M for Guénon; U for the other two on Theosophy specifically]
- Splits: Theosophists loyal to Blavatsky and Judge (the United Lodge of Theosophists, founded in Los Angeles in 1909, and the Pasadena and Point Loma line, which are two bodies) call the Besant and Leadbeater layer "Neo-Theosophy." De Zirkoff's Collected Writings (1950 to 1991) is the standard text for separating her words from later ones. Tillett (1982) is the standard Leadbeater biography, including the 1906 scandal; Hanegraaff treats Bailey as the main bridge to the New Age. [M]

## 9. Perennial philosophy is wider than Huxley, for this slice (added on the user's instruction, 2026-10-07)

Status: outside the colleague's slice, written from memory by the fact-checker, no search, no fetch. All [M] unless marked [U]. The point from the user: there are plenty of other books in the perennial philosophy movement besides what Huxley covers, and Huxley is not even the best of them. "Best" is a judgement; I have not ranked anyone. What can be said as fact:

Huxley's place. The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is an anthology of passages with his commentary, the best known book under that name. He did not found the movement or coin the phrase: Steuco used "philosophia perennis" in De perenni philosophia (1540) and Leibniz in a letter of 1714 (both already in Part two). The Traditionalist authors (Guénon from 1921, Coomaraswamy, Schuon from 1948) are earlier, and Huxley drew on some of them [U on the extent]. Huxley is the popularizer of a body of work that existed before him.

The authors who matter for the questions in this slice (levels of reality, cycles, stages that scale outward), with what each is for:
- René Guénon (1886 to 1951). The Multiple States of the Being (Les états multiples de l'être, 1932): a being has many states and the human state is one. The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times (1945) and the essays later gathered as Formes traditionnelles et cycles cosmiques (posthumous, 1970 [U]) set out the cycles in the Hindu manner (manvantara, the four ages). He rejected Theosophy outright (Le Théosophisme, 1921) and reincarnationism (L'Erreur spirite, 1923). So the one perennialist who most uses cosmic cycles is a critic of Theosophy's version.
- Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947). Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), Time and Eternity (1947): the doctrine of time, eternity and the one transmigrant rather than staged ascent. [M]
- Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998). The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953): the thesis that religions differ in outer form and agree at the inner, metaphysical level. This is close to Alex's "popular face against inner face" correction, and the essay can credit Schuon and the Traditionalists for that and not only Huxley. His doctrine of degrees of Reality (Beyond-Being, Being, Existence) is the Traditionalist counterpart of a ladder of scales, hierarchical and not evolutionary [U for the book that holds it]. [M]
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr. An Introduction to Islamic Cosmological Doctrines (1964): a source for how one tradition draws its cosmic ladder; Knowledge and the Sacred (Gifford Lectures, 1981). [M for titles, U for the 1964 publisher]
- Huston Smith. Forgotten Truth (1976; later reissued with the subtitle The Common Vision of the World's Religions): four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite) matched to body, mind, soul and spirit, the best-known stage-and-scale picture in the movement after the Theosophical one. [M]
- Whitall Perry. A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971): a thematic anthology of the same kind as Huxley's and much larger. [M]
- Ken Wilber. The Spectrum of Consciousness (1977) and later books: the "great nest of being", each level transcending and including the one below. A modern stage-and-scale map that explicitly claims the perennial philosophy. [M]
- Evelyn Underhill. Mysticism (1911): a staged mystic way (awakening, purification, illumination, the dark night, union; the exact count is [U]), compiled from Christian and other sources. The experience line, not the Traditionalist line. [M]
- Others in the Traditionalist circle: Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis (T03 lists them with years [U]). P02 to P09 and P18 cover these authors; I did not re-read them for this section.

Why it matters for Part two.
(1) The two comparers in Alex's brief are a feud: the Traditionalist perennialists called Theosophy a counterfeit, so "the perennial philosophy and Theosophy" are not one family, and the essay should not say so.
(2) For stages, the schools disagree in kind. Theosophy and Wilber describe growth through time (a Path, initiations, a spectrum). The Traditionalists describe degrees of Reality and cycles of the world, and treat the evolutionary picture and reincarnationism as modern errors. Alex's arrow is a growth picture, so its natural comparers are Theosophy, Wilber and Underhill, not Guénon or Schuon. Where he cites the Traditionalists, he should cite them for "different outer forms, same inner truth", not for stages.
(3) His method differs from all of them: he derives the common core from an axiom and does not argue that a primordial tradition exists or that mystics' reports converge.
(4) The critics of the whole perennialist project (Katz 1978, Proudfoot 1985, Zaehner 1957, Forman 1990, Hick 1989) are in part-2-notes.md and T03.verified section 9 [M]; keep them in view when the essay says "the same things in different words".
