## 1. The picture in brief

In 1880 Alfred Percy Sinnett, a newspaper editor in British India, began a correspondence with two men he called the Masters, Koot Hoomi (K.H.) and Morya (M.), through Madame Blavatsky and Colonel Olcott. His questions and their answers became the raw material of two books. The Occult World (1881) argued that such men exist and can do remarkable things. Esoteric Buddhism (1883) set out a teaching: a person made of seven principles, a chain of seven globes toured seven times by the life-wave, a rest between lives called devachan, karma as the law that sorts one life into the next, and nirvana at the far end. The letters themselves were published only in 1923.

The book carried the word Buddhism, and Buddhist scholars (Max Mueller, Rhys Davids) said it was not Buddhism. The Madras Brahmin lawyer T. Subba Row, a trained Advaitin, was the circle's insider on Hindu thought. He treated the Buddhist and Vedantic schemes as two ways of cutting one body of fact, wrote an essay whose title joins them (Aryan and Arhat), and corrected Sinnett's Sanskrit names. He also dissented from Blavatsky and Sinnett on how many principles a person has, and on whether the spirit (Atma) is a principle of a person at all.

The strongest form of their "same teaching, different words" is Subba Row's: the counts differ between systems because each cuts the same thing at different joints. The weakest is the habit of equating terms that specialists keep apart: anatta with a "lower self", devachan with Sukhavati, Parabrahm with Law.

## 2. Findings

1. Sinnett and the two books

Claim: Sinnett (1840 to 1921) edited The Pioneer at Allahabad, met Blavatsky and Olcott around 1879 to 1880 and received letters from K.H. and M. from October 1880. He published The Occult World (Trubner, 1881), an argument that adepts exist built on his own experience of phenomena (the Simla episodes of autumn 1880) and extracts of K.H.'s letters, with no real doctrine, and Esoteric Buddhism (Trubner, 1883), which carries the doctrine. A fifth, annotated edition (1885) added notes after criticism from inside the Society.
Before the book, a series called "Fragments of Occult Truth" in The Theosophist (from October 1881, signed "a lay chela", attributed to Hume or to Sinnett, check) gave the first printed outline of the seven principles [U on authorship and dates].
Tier: [M] for books, years and publisher; [U] for the annotated edition and the Fragments.
Use for the essay: if Part two names Sinnett, say "an English newspaper editor in India, 1883". The Occult World is no source for the traditions.

2. What Esoteric Buddhism teaches

Claim: (a) A person has seven principles. Sinnett's English names were body, vitality, astral body, animal soul, human soul, spiritual soul and spirit, with Sanskrit equivalents rupa (in the book, as I recall; sthula sarira is the later usage) [U], prana or jiva, linga sarira, kama rupa, manas, buddhi and atma. (b) Death is a sorting. The lowest principles fall away. The animal soul lingers in a shadowy state (kama-loca) and fades as a "shell". The human and spiritual souls pass into a long subjective rest (devachan). (c) After the rest the person returns, and the sum of a life's causes (karma) shapes the next. (d) All of it happens on a chain of seven globes that the life-wave tours seven times (the rounds), and humanity moves through seven root races. (e) Nirvana lies at the far end and is not annihilation.
Who: Sinnett (1883). I do not trust myself to list chapter titles.
Traditions cited: the title claims the Buddha's own secret teaching; the text uses Sanskrit and some Tibetan terms and, as I recall, no Pali texts.
Tier: [M] for the list; [U] for chapter titles.
Use: the sevenfold person is a Theosophical scheme. No Hindu or Buddhist text has this seven. In Part two it can appear only as "the Theosophists' own count".

3. The rounds: a V-shaped journey with a turning point

Claim: in the Letters and in Esoteric Buddhism the life-wave goes down into denser matter during the first half of its journey and comes back toward spirit in the second. The turning point is the middle of the fourth round, on the fourth globe, our Earth. After it, evolution is an ascent. The soul gains self-consciousness on the way down and carries it back up.
Who: Sinnett (1883); the Letters' Cosmological Notes; Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine vol. 1 (1888), which widens the scheme. The cycles are likened to Puranic yugas and manvantaras [U].
Tier: [M] for the shape (descent, nadir in the fourth round, ascent); [U] for locators.
Use: the nearest early-Theosophical form of Alex's axiom (one whole, separation, return). The nadir can sit beside his arrow only as a pointer, since his scale is not theirs. Sibling T05 covers it.

4. Kama-loca, devachan and the "shells"

Claim: devachan is the long, happy, subjective rest between lives, made of the person's own highest thoughts and affections from the life just ended, lasting in proportion to the weight of that spiritual energy. The word comes from Tibetan bde-ba-can, the name of the Western paradise that Sanskrit calls Sukhavati. Kama-loca is where the lower principles dissolve; their remnants (the shells) are what mediums meet at seances. This was the Masters' reason for saying that spirit communications are not the dead.
Who: Sinnett (1883); the Letters; Blavatsky's glossary (1892) and The Key to Theosophy (1889).
Traditions: Theosophists equate devachan with Sukhavati (Pure Land), Hindu svarga and "heaven"; the Tibetan bardo is nearby.
Tier: [M] for the content, etymology and shells; [U] for durations (I do not trust the figures that circulate).
Scholars: not a Hindu or Buddhist doctrine in this form. A Pure Land is a place to practise on the way to awakening, not a reward-rest. The Theravada tradition rejected an intermediate state between death and rebirth (Kathavatthu, as I remember it VIII.2 [U]; my recollection agrees, unchecked); the Sarvastivada accepted one (antarabhava).
Use: "the soul continues between lives" in Theosophical form. Call the equation a Theosophist's equation, and cite Sukhavati or bardo in their own terms.

5. The title problem: "Esoteric Buddhism" and "Budhism"

Claim: the Masters and Sinnett said the word Buddhism in the title did not mean the religion as practised. The Letters play on Budh (wisdom, from the Sanskrit root budh, to know) against Buddha, so that the real thing is a wisdom-teaching of which Gautama was one great exponent. Blavatsky later said in The Key to Theosophy that Theosophy is not Buddhism.
Who: K.H. in the Letters and Sinnett's later prefaces [U]; Blavatsky (1889).
Tier: [U] for who said what and when; [M] that the naming dispute existed.
Scholars: seen as an after-the-fact fix, since the book was sold as the Buddha's secret doctrine.
Use: this is where Alex should stop. If he calls anything Buddhism he should let Buddhists speak.

6. The Mahatma Letters: what they are and how to hold them

Claim: about 140 to 150 letters (the count depends on the edition) [U], mostly from 1880 to about 1884, from K.H. and M. to Sinnett and Hume. Edited by A. T. Barker: The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett from the Mahatmas M. and K. H. (T. Fisher Unwin, London, 1923). Vicente Hao Chin Jr., The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett in Chronological Sequence (Theosophical Publishing House, Manila, 1993), re-orders them by date, restores some passages and gives a concordance with Barker's numbers. The manuscripts are in the British Library [U on shelf-marks].
Provenance: the Hodgson report to the Society for Psychical Research (1885) judged Blavatsky the author of the K.H. letters. Vernon Harrison attacked its handwriting method (1986 article, 1997 book), and the SPR did not formally withdraw it [U]. The "Kiddle incident" (1883) turned on a K.H. passage that closely paralleled a Spiritualist lecture by Henry Kiddle; Theosophists blamed a hasty copying disciple, critics saw human authorship.
Tier: [M] for editors, titles, years and the three episodes; [U] for counts and shelf-marks.
Use: treat the Letters as Theosophical scripture. Alex can say "the Letters claim" and "Theosophists hold", not that Tibetan Masters taught this. Siblings T01 and T06 go deeper on provenance.

7. The letter on God

Claim: in one of the best known Letters (Barker Letter 10, as I remember it [U]; my recollection agrees, unchecked), K.H. says that the Masters' doctrine denies God, "as philosophers and as Buddhists" (corrected: the earlier draft of this file said "as philosophical pantheists and as theists", which I do not recall). They know of planetary and other spiritual lives, and say there is no God in their system, "either personal or impersonal". Parabrahm is not a god but absolute immutable Law, and Ishvara is the effect of avidya and maya, born of ignorance and the great illusion. A teaching offered as the root of all religions begins by denying the God of most of them.
The words: paraphrase, from memory, not checked against the text. Do not quote until the letter has been read in Barker and Hao Chin.
Traditions: Buddhism (non-theism), Advaita (Parabrahm as nirguna Brahman; Ishvara as Brahman seen through maya), Christianity (the personal God, rejected).
Tier: [M] for the gist (as corrected); [U] for the letter number.
Scholars: an Advaita specialist would object on two points. Brahman is not "Law" but being and consciousness (sat-chit), and Ishvara is not a mere product of ignorance but Brahman as seen with maya as its power. So the Letter's Parabrahm-as-Law is a Theosophical reading, a place where the comparer equates what specialists keep apart.
Use: an example of "the One is not a person". Attribute it to the Theosophical Masters, not to Advaita. Note the self-description "as Buddhists": the Masters claim the denial on Buddhist grounds, which a Theravada reader would accept for "no creator god" but not for "Parabrahm is Law".

8. Spirit and matter, the One Life, the Absolute

Claim: the Letters (the Cosmological Notes especially) teach that spirit and matter are two states of one element. The Absolute is beyond definition. The differentiated universe appears from within the One, and every being is a ray of the One Life, not a separate being. They distinguish the Absolute from the manifested first Logos and from the Dhyan Chohans, the planetary intelligences.
Who: K.H. and M. in the Letters (Barker numbers I do not trust); Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine (1888), Proem.
Tier: [M] for the gist; [U] for locators.
Traditions: Advaita (Brahman as the One), Samkhya (purusha and prakriti, which Samkhya keeps as two), Mahayana (emptiness), Neoplatonism.
Use: an 1880s English form of Alex's axiom, not a proof. For the Advaitic form cite Subba Row (finding 12).

9. Nirvana, Paranirvana and what the Buddha taught

Claim: the Letters insist that nirvana is not annihilation. They separate nirvana (a state of highest spiritual existence, entered by the adept) from paranirvana (the state beyond, at the end of a manvantara, when the whole chain re-enters the Absolute). The Masters present themselves as followers of the Buddha's inner teaching, which they call the same as the older "Aryan" wisdom.
Who: K.H. and M. in the Letters; Sinnett (1883).
Tier: [M] that this is the Theosophical teaching; [U] for locators and K.H.'s exact form.
Scholars: in Theravada, parinibbana is the death of an arahant with no further rebirth. It is not cosmic. The Theosophical paranirvana reads a Hindu cosmic dissolution (pralaya) back into a Buddhist word.
Use: nirvana and moksha can carry "the separation ends", but those traditions describe the end in the first person (craving ends, ignorance ends), not as a cosmic event.

10. No-self versus the Self

Claim: the hinge of the whole Buddhist and Vedantic equation. The standard Theosophical answer: the Buddha's anatta denies that the personal, changing self (the lower principles) is the real self. It does not deny the One Self, which Vedanta calls Atman and Theosophy Atma. So the Buddha and the Upanishads say the same: not this personal I, but that which is the Whole. The Letters add that the personality does not survive while the individuality (the higher triad) does.
Who: the Letters; Sinnett (1883); Blavatsky, The Key to Theosophy and The Secret Doctrine; Subba Row.
Tier: [M] for the position; [U] for locators.
Scholars: Pali specialists (Rhys Davids, later Rahula, Harvey) read anatta as denying a self in any of the five aggregates, not as a hint of a universal Self. Two things complicate that. In one Pali discourse (SN 44.10 as I remember it [U]; the Ananda Sutta, where Vacchagotta asks whether there is a self and the Buddha stays silent) the Buddha is silent when asked whether there is a self. And Caroline Rhys Davids, T. W. Rhys Davids' wife, argued in later books (late 1920s and 1930s [U]; corrected from "1930s") that early Buddhism taught a Self. Most specialists have not followed her.
Use: the honest sentence is "the Theosophists reconcile the two by splitting the self in two", which settles nothing. If Alex uses Buddhism he should use the Buddha's own text on the Unconditioned (Udana 8.3, the unborn, unmade, unconditioned [M]) and say that Theravada and Mahayana read such lines differently.

11. The Masters on Christianity, Hinduism and the Buddhist churches

Claim: as I recall the pattern in the Letters, Jesus is honoured as an adept while church doctrine (a personal God, atonement, hell) and the priesthood are rejected. Brahmins are honoured as heirs of an old wisdom and criticised for caste and ritual. Southern (Ceylon) Buddhists are said to hold the Buddha's letter but to have lost the key to his inner meaning, while the reformed Tibetan Buddhism of Tsongkhapa is said to keep it. The Society must be neutral among religions and aim at brotherhood.
Tier: [U]. I recall the attitudes, not the wording or the place.
Scholars: the attitudes track 1880s freethought criticism of church Christianity more than any Tibetan source.
Use: Alex's own position (the inner teaching of each tradition carries the idea, the official face often does not) is close to this pattern. He should say it as his own and not cite the Letters for claims about Tibet.

12. T. Subba Row and the Bhagavad Gita lectures

Claim: Tallapragada Subba Row (1856 to 1890) was a Telugu Brahmin, a lawyer in the Madras High Court and a learned Advaitin. He joined the Society in the early 1880s [U], wrote in The Theosophist, acted as its authority on Hindu thought, was said to have a teacher among the Masters [U], and died aged 33 (corrected from "34": born July 1856, died June 1890, so a few weeks short of 34 [U on the exact age]). At the Adyar convention in December 1885 he gave four lectures on the Gita (printed in 1886 or soon after; the printing year and titles vary [U]). The gist, from memory: Krishna is the Logos (Ishvara) speaking to Arjuna, the individual soul (jivatma). Behind the Logos is Parabrahman, the Absolute that cannot be described. Mulaprakriti, root-matter, is the veil of Parabrahman, and the Logos arises through it as a reflection arises on a surface. The Gita's point is that the individual must find its Self one with the Logos and with the Absolute behind it.
Traditions: Advaita, Samkhya (prakriti), the Christian Logos.
Tier: [M] for the man, the occasion, the Logos reading and "Mulaprakriti as the veil of Parabrahman"; [U] for printing dates, titles, the exact age and the equation of the "Light of the Logos" with Blavatsky's Fohat.
Scholars: the Logos language is Christian and Neoplatonic, not Advaitic. Advaita would call the root-matter maya or avidya, not a principle in its own right. Academics treat the lectures as a real meeting of Advaita and Western occultism.
Use: the best early example of a veil made of the One itself, like the computer's walls made of the machine. Verify the wording before quoting. Subba Row is a better witness for Vedanta than Blavatsky.

13. "Aryan-Arhat Esoteric Tenets on the Sevenfold Principle in Man"

Claim: the title says that the Hindu (Aryan) and the Buddhist (Arhat) inner teachings are one. As I recall the argument: the Theosophical seven is one way of dividing a person, from the Arhat side. The Brahmanical way groups fewer divisions, as bodies and states (gross, subtle, causal, with the Self as witness). The counts do not conflict, because the number of principles depends on how finely you cut, and both describe the same facts. He also complained that Sinnett's Sanskrit names did not match their Sanskrit sense (buddhi is the discriminating intellect, not a "spiritual soul"). It appeared in The Theosophist, I believe 1886 to 1887 [U].
Tier: [M] for the title and the claim of equivalence; [U] for the grouping and the complaints.
Scholars: Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold person. Advaita uses the five sheaths (Taittiriya Upanishad 2) and, in later Advaita manuals, the three bodies (gross, subtle, causal); the Mandukya Upanishad gives four states of consciousness (waking, dream, deep sleep, turiya) (corrected: the earlier draft credited "three bodies with four states" to the Mandukya Upanishad, but the three-body scheme is later Advaita). Mapping Buddhist aggregates onto Vedantic sheaths is a comparer's move.
Use: Subba Row's logic that different counts are different cuts of one thing. Read the original first.

14. Subba Row's dissent from Blavatsky and Sinnett

Claim: on at least these points, as I remember. (a) The number and kind of principles. (b) Whether Atma is a principle of a person: for an Advaitin the Atman is the one reality, not a part of anything, and the individual is its conditioned appearance. (c) The Sanskrit sense of buddhi and manas. (d) Aspects of the Logos doctrine in The Secret Doctrine. He is reported to have declined to contribute to that book (about 1888 [U]). Blavatsky answered him and defended the seven as the older Arhat form.
Tier: [U] for every specific point. [M] only that there was an open disagreement and that he was the Society's authority on Sanskrit and Vedanta.
Use: the "same teaching" claim was argued among the comparers themselves. Their own Hindu scholar did not agree with their founder on how to describe a person.

15. The Buddhist side in practice: Olcott

Claim: Olcott wrote A Buddhist Catechism (Colombo, 1881), approved by the Ceylonese monk Hikkaduwe Sumangala, and in 1891 drafted "Fourteen Propositions" that Buddhist leaders of Southern and Northern schools endorsed as a common platform.
Tier: [M]; [U] for the exact endorsers.
Use: a real case where Buddhists of different schools accepted a short common list.

16. The strongest "same teaching, different words" lines, and where each stands

(a) Blavatsky's stock claim that the esoteric philosophy reconciles all religions and shows their roots to be one. Locator (Key or Secret Doctrine) and wording unverified. [U]
(b) The Society's motto: no religion is higher than truth (Sanskrit satyat nasti paro dharmah). [M]
(c) Subba Row's title, "Aryan-Arhat", as an act of equation. [M]
(d) The three Fundamental Propositions of The Secret Doctrine (1888, Proem): one omnipresent, eternal, boundless principle; universes appearing and disappearing in cycles; the identity of all souls with the Over-Soul and each soul's pilgrimage through births. [M for gist; sibling T02 covers the wording.] The best compact nineteenth-century statement of the axiom.
(e) The Letters' claim that the Masters' wisdom is neither Hindu nor Buddhist nor Christian but the base under all three. [U]
Use: (c) and (d) are the usable two. The rest are slogans.

17. How Max Mueller and Rhys Davids answered

Claim: Max Mueller, the Oxford Sanskritist and editor of the Sacred Books of the East, published "Esoteric Buddhism" in The Nineteenth Century (1893, I believe May [U]) and treated the title as a mistake: Buddhism has no secret doctrine. The standard text cited is the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16, chapter 2; I recall verse 2.25 in Walshe's numbering and II.32 in Rhys Davids' older numbering, PTS D II 100 [U]), where the Buddha tells Ananda he has held nothing back, with no closed fist of a teacher (acariyamutthi). Mueller's Gifford Lectures (1892, printed 1893), Theosophy or Psychological Religion, separate the older meaning of theosophy (mystic knowledge of God) from Blavatsky's. T. W. Rhys Davids, founder of the Pali Text Society, also rejected the Theosophical use of Buddhism, as I recall; I cannot name a title [U].
Tier: [M] that Mueller wrote against Esoteric Buddhism and that the closed fist line is the standard reply; [U] for date, pages and any Rhys Davids locator.
Scholars: the core scholarly judgement, well supported. Tantric and Mahayana traditions do have restricted teachings, so "no esoteric Buddhism" holds for the Pali canon's claim, not for every school.
Use: do not write "the secret teaching of the Buddha". Write that the Buddha says he kept nothing back. The Theosophical reply (a private teaching to initiates) cannot be tested.

## 4. How they compare: the method, with examples

1. Tables of terms. Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan and English terms are laid in columns and equated: devachan, Sukhavati, svarga, heaven; five skandhas, five koshas, seven principles. It works where the items really are alike (karma and kamma) and fails where they only look alike (anatta and atman).

2. One source. Every religion descends from an older Wisdom-Religion kept by initiates. Differences are priestly corruption or adaptation to a people and an age. The Masters claim to speak for that source. It cannot be tested, which is why scholars set the claim aside.

3. Outer and inner face. Each tradition has a public face of dogma and ritual and an inner face that is the same everywhere. Subba Row joins the Hindu and Buddhist inner faces. Alex's own method (public face against inner face) follows the pattern, but he cites each tradition's own texts and claims no hidden lineage.

4. Adepts over texts. When a text and a Master disagree, the Master wins. This is the weak point, since a text can be checked and a Master cannot.

5. Different counts, same facts. Subba Row's argument: the number of divisions depends on how you cut, so a person described as threefold, fivefold or sevenfold is one person. This is the honest method, because it concedes that vocabularies differ and offers a way to read them as compatible.

6. Splitting the self in two. The Buddhist denial and the Vedantic affirmation are said to aim at different objects, the personal and the universal. Specialists keep them apart; the comparer equates them.

7. The counterexample as a check. The Buddha's closed-fist line (DN 16) is the line any claim of a hidden Buddhism must answer.

## 5. What scholars say, short

Scholars of religion treat the Mahatma Letters as founding writing of a new religious movement, not as a witness to an Asian tradition. The Masters have no corroboration in Tibetan sources (Lopez, Prisoners of Shangri-La, 1998; Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994; Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996 [M gist]). Gombrich and Obeyesekere (Buddhism Transformed, 1988) and Prothero (The White Buddhist, 1996) place Olcott's Buddhism as a modernist, Protestant-shaped movement. Bevir (JAAR 62, 1994) reads Theosophy as a Western occult tradition turned eastward. For the Hindu side, Halbfass (India and Europe, 1988) and King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) show how Vedanta was filtered through Orientalist ideas and fed the modern reform currents that said all religions are one. Buddhist specialists find no Buddhist source for the seven principles, rounds or devachan. Advaita specialists find the Theosophical Vedanta a Western occult scheme with Sanskrit labels, with Subba Row the partial exception. Historians credit it with opening English-language readers to Asian ideas and giving Asian reformers (Dharmapala in Ceylon, parts of the Hindu revival) a platform. The history of the movement is told by Campbell (Ancient Wisdom Revived, 1980), and Johnson (The Masters Revealed, 1994) argues that the Masters were real Indian figures behind a constructed Theosophical myth; neither is a defender of the Letters' own claims (corrected: the earlier draft listed them as defenders of authenticity). Vernon Harrison (handwriting, 1986 and 1997) criticises the Hodgson report's method, which is not the same as proving who wrote the letters. Defenders of authenticity are mostly Theosophical authors. Most academics put authenticity aside. The fair summary: early and sincere comparers, whose method mixes sound observation (karma is karma) with strained equations (anatta as a lower self).
