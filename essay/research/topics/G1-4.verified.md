## 1. The picture in brief

These people are not a school. Phrase historians (Steuco 1540, Leibniz 1714, Lessing 1779 and 1780) show the idea changing: a deposit of revelation, then a sifting task, then a claim that cannot be proved and must be lived. Western readers of the East (Emerson, Schopenhauer) supply the English and German words for one whole, the veil and the fingers, and Schopenhauer got his Upanishads by a documented chain from Dara Shikoh. Experience writers (James, Underhill, Bucke, Stace) compare reports, not doctrines, and give Alex a modest stance, a filter image, a stage ladder and a two-type map of unity experience. Indian insiders (Ramakrishna, Vivekananda, Dara, Radhakrishnan, Bhagavan Das) compare from inside one tradition, and each puts his own on top. The anthologists (Huxley, Perry, Wilson, Needleman) sort passages under headings, with different frames. [CHECKER: Huxley is one of several here, and on the evidence of the sibling slices he is neither the first anthologist of the twentieth century movement (the Traditionalist library begins in 1921, and Underhill, James and Bucke compare mystics before him), nor the strictest, nor the largest (Perry 1971, Wilson 1991). Whether he is "the best" is a judgement; section 9 gives "best for what" and ranks no one.]

## 4. How they compare: the method

Six moves recur.

1. History of the phrase (Steuco, Leibniz). Agreement is first one source, then a sifting task. Only Steuco claims a first revelation.

2. Heading and rows (Huxley, Perry, Wilson, Bhagavan Das). The editor picks the heading and sets passages in a row. The equation is made by heading and translation before the reader sees it. Wilson alone leaves room for disagreement.

3. Report and type (James, Bucke, Stace, Underhill). Treat mystics as witnesses and ask whether reports agree. James gives four marks, Bucke marks and cases, Stace seven characteristics, Underhill an order. Differences go to over-beliefs (James), interpretation (Stace), temperament (Underhill).

4. Term pairing (Dara, Schopenhauer, Radhakrishnan's notes). Put a term from A beside a term from B. Dara paired angels with gods, and his Vedanta came out Sufi. The pairing is the comparer's, not the tradition's.

5. Image (Ramakrishna's lake, Rumi's grapes, Vivekananda's streams, James's screen, Lessing's rings). The picture carries the proof and stays vague on purpose.

6. Insider speech and conditions (Vivekananda, Radhakrishnan, Huxley). Each speaks from one tradition and puts it on top: Advaita (Vivekananda, Radhakrishnan), Sufism (Dara), Christianity (Steuco, Underhill). Huxley adds that knowing needs a changed knower, the strongest and least testable move.

A worked row for Alex's karma step: Emerson's thief who steals from himself, Schopenhauer's doer and sufferer who are one, Lessing's coming back to learn, under "what I do to another I do to myself". The row hides that Emerson gives no metaphysics, that Schopenhauer's ends in denying the will, and that Lessing speaks of the individual's progress.

- James's "neither birthday nor native land" is a saying he passes on ("as has been said"), not his own coinage [M]. Do not credit it to him as author.
- Rumi: any line found on a quotation site, and any popular English "version", is a free rendering until it is found in Nicholson (Masnavi) or another scholarly translation. The four-men-and-a-coin story is genuine (Masnavi, Book II) [M]. Treat unsourced oneness lines credited to Rumi (for example the ocean-in-a-drop line) as unsourced until a page is found [U].
- Eckhart: any Eckhart line reaching the essay by way of Huxley or a quotation site must be checked in Quint, Walshe or Colledge and McGinn (finding 3). The line "if the only prayer you ever say is thank you, it will be enough", widely credited to him, has no source known to me [U].
- "We are not human beings having a spiritual experience; we are spiritual beings having a human experience", credited to Teilhard de Chardin: I know no source in his writings, and its origin is unsettled [U]. Do not use it under his name.
- Einstein's letter of 1950: genuine as far as I know, but quoted in several wordings. Take the base text from a scholarly edition of his quotations, not from a quotation site [M-].
- Titles confused between books: Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is not Smith's Forgotten Truth (two subtitles: 1976 "The Primordial Tradition", 1992 "The Common Vision of the World's Religions"), nor Schuon's Transcendent Unity of Religions, nor Perry's Treasury. Huxley's Doors of Perception (1954) and Heaven and Hell (1956) are two books, often cited as one. Underhill's Mysticism (1911), The Mystic Way (1913) and Practical Mysticism (1914) are three [M].
- Two meanings of "perennial philosophy": the Catholic neo-scholastic one (Thomism) and the common-core one; see lead 14 [M].
- A tradition said to teach what it does not: Huxley equates the Mahayana Dharmakaya or "Suchness" with the Ground, and Buddhist scholars would not accept that (P08); Hindu inclusivism is older than Ramakrishna, but its modern universalist form is more recent (section 5); Buddhist interdependence is not a "One" (the specialist objection the essay already notes) [M].
- A Traditionalist credited with another's view: Huxley was not an initiate, not a Traditionalist and not a Theosophist, so Guenon's and Schuon's demand for initiation does not apply to him, and nothing the Traditionalists wrote against Theosophy applies to him either [M].
