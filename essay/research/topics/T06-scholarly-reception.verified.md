## 1. The picture in brief

Scholars of religion no longer ask whether Blavatsky was right about an ancient wisdom. They ask what the Theosophical Society was and what it did. The answers fall into four groups.

Sources. Coleman (printed 1895; the brief dates it 1893 [U]) argued, and later Godwin, Hanegraaff and Hammer in their own ways support, that her comparative material came mostly from nineteenth-century books: Orientalist translations, Masonic and Rosicrucian writers, Eliphas Levi, popular science. Her lineage is European esotericism, with an Eastern vocabulary laid over it from about 1879.

Authority. The hidden Masters cannot be checked. The Hodgson report (1885) judged her phenomena fraudulent. A minority reopened the case: Harrison on the handwriting evidence (1986); Johnson asks a different question (who the Masters were) and does not retry the 1885 evidence [M]. Most academics set the question aside and study effects.

Influence. The Society was a real channel for Asian ideas into the West, and for reworked versions back into Asia. Olcott's Buddhist Catechism, Dharmapala, Besant, Hume, Gandhi's first reading of the Gita, Kandinsky and Mondrian, Yeats, Anthroposophy, Bailey, Krishnamurti and the New Age all pass through it. Specialists in Buddhism and Hinduism judge what it taught about their traditions as often wrong, and its social effect as often large.

Harm. The root-race scheme gave later racial occultists a cosmic vocabulary. Goodrick-Clarke follows it to Ariosophy in Austria and Germany, and does not say Theosophy caused Nazism. Defenders (Santucci) say her races are spiritual stages.

The "ancient wisdom" claim is treated as a legitimising move to be studied (Hanegraaff, Hammer), not adopted. Perennialists of the Traditionalist school attacked her from the other side.

### C. Influence in Asia

13. Olcott, the Buddhist Catechism and the Sinhalese revival
Claim: Olcott and Blavatsky took the Five Precepts publicly at Galle in May 1880. Olcott wrote the Buddhist Catechism (Colombo, 1881), endorsed by the monk Hikkaduwe Sumangala, and it ran through many editions and languages. Who: Stephen Prothero, The White Buddhist (Indiana, 1996); Thomas Tweed, The American Encounter with Buddhism, 1844 to 1912 (Indiana, 1992); Gombrich and Obeyesekere (1988).
Tier: [M] for the story; [U] for the flag year and the number of editions.

14. Protestant Buddhism and its critics

### D. Influence in the West

19. Arts, offshoots, society
Claim: Kandinsky read Blavatsky, Besant and Leadbeater (Thought-Forms, 1901) and Steiner, and in Concerning the Spiritual in Art treated the Theosophical movement as a spiritual force (Ringbom, 1970). Mondrian joined the Dutch Theosophical Society in 1909. Hilma af Klint, Scriabin, Yeats and AE (George Russell) moved in the circle. Yeats met Blavatsky in 1887, joined the Esoteric Section in 1888 and left in 1890 [U]. Carlson shows its large role in Russian culture. Dixon shows it as a route for women's work in England.
Steiner led the German section (1902 to 1912), split after the Krishnamurti affair and founded the Anthroposophical Society (1912 to 1913), more Christian in focus. Alice Bailey left in the early 1920s and founded the Arcane School, writing "Tibetan" teachings ascribed to Djwhal Khul. Krishnamurti, found by Leadbeater in 1909, was made head of the Order of the Star in the East (1911), dissolved it in 1929 and left the Society, saying truth is a pathless land. Hanegraaff and Campbell show the New Age and the "Ascended Masters" groups continuing these themes.
Who: Sixten Ringbom, The Sounding Cosmos (1970); Maria Carlson, No Religion Higher Than Truth (Princeton, 1993); Joy Dixon, Divine Feminine (2001); Alex Owen, The Place of Enchantment (2004); Campbell; Hanegraaff.
Tier: [M] for Kandinsky, Mondrian, Steiner, Bailey, Krishnamurti; [U] for Yeats's dates and Kandinsky's formal membership.

### E. Race, Ariosophy and misuse

20. What the Secret Doctrine says
Claim: the Secret Doctrine (vol. 2) teaches seven root-races, each with seven sub-races. The Lemurian (third) and the Atlantean (fourth) came before the present Aryan (fifth). Some living peoples are described as dying remnants of earlier stock, and apes as degenerate descendants of mindless humans. Her "Aryan" is not the German race of the later racists: she counts Indians and Persians as Aryans. She treats Semites as a later and in some respects lower offshoot [U]. Besant and Leadbeater extended the scheme (a sixth root-race beginning in California). The word "Aryan" came from philology, and Max Mueller said late in life that it was a linguistic term and that "Aryan blood" was nonsense [M gist]. Trautmann, Aryans and British India (1997), traces the British Indian side.
Who: Blavatsky (1888), vol. 2; Besant and Leadbeater, Man: Whence, How and Whither (1913) [U]. Locators unknown.
Tier: [M] for the scheme; [U] for the passages on Semites and apes.
Use: the content is unacceptable now. A writer who touches it should say so briefly.

21. The defence and the critics
Claim: James Santucci argues that Theosophical "race" meant stages of spiritual and cosmic evolution, not a biological hierarchy of living peoples, and that Blavatsky attacked colonial racism. Critics reply that the vocabulary of higher and lower races was there and was borrowed from her century. Peter Staudenmaier argues that Steiner's anthroposophy carried racial doctrine into its politics in the fascist era (anthroposophists contest his reading [M]), and a Dutch commission (2000) found some statements discriminatory but the movement not racist [U].
Who: Santucci, "The Notion of Race in Theosophy," Nova Religio 11 (2008), pages 37 to 63 [U]; Staudenmaier, Between Occultism and Nazism (Brill, 2014).
Tier: [M] for authors and positions; [U] for pages and the Dutch report.
Use: say both things. The First Object (brotherhood without distinction of race) and the root-race scheme sat together.

22. Ariosophy
Claim: Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke showed that Austrian and German groups around Guido von List and Jorg Lanz von Liebenfels fused the Theosophical root-race Aryan, Atlantis and esoteric hierarchy with pan-German racial nationalism. "Ariosophy" is Lanz's term (1915). The Thule Society and Sebottendorf touched the early Nazi party, and Himmler used Wiligut. Goodrick-Clarke is careful: these were fringe groups, the main effect was an ideological climate, and the regime later suppressed them. Later work (Hermann Gilbhard, Die Thule-Gesellschaft, 1994 [U on first name and year]) cuts the Thule claims back further [U]. Sensational books (Pauwels and Bergier, 1960) made the popular legend. Savitri Devi is a Hindu-Aryan variant (Goodrick-Clarke, 1998).
Who: Goodrick-Clarke, The Occult Roots of Nazism (1985); Hitler's Priestess (1998); Black Sun (2002).
Tier: [M] for the thesis; [U] for Gilbhard and for the ban on Theosophical lodges in Nazi Germany (1937).
Use: if anyone asks, the safe reply is that Theosophy's cosmic race scheme was one raw material among several for a small racial-occult fringe, and that the Nazi regime banned Theosophy and Anthroposophy [U on the dates].

### F. The "ancient wisdom" claim and the Traditionalist attack

23. Guenon against the Theosophists
Claim: Rene Guenon wrote Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923). He held that Theosophy was a modern invention built from Western occultism, that it misread Eastern doctrine, that its Masters were counterfeits, and that its reincarnation was an error. Sedgwick shows Traditionalism took shape partly against Theosophy while sharing its shape: a primordial tradition, an esoteric core, cycles.
Who: Guenon (1921, 1923); Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (Oxford, 2004).
Tier: [M].
Use: if he cites both movements, say they disagree. On rebirth the Traditionalists reject the Theosophical doctrine (see P02, P12).

24. How scholars treat the "ancient wisdom" claim

As reception history it shows how traditions are translated and reshaped (Chajes and Huss, Theosophical Appropriations, 2016). Richard King reads the "mystic East" as an Orientalist construction. Defenders such as Huston Smith (a primordial tradition, Forgotten Truth, 1976) and Wilber (a perennial philosophy of levels) keep a form of it. [Checker: the colleague wrote "a modest form"; that does not fit Wilber, whose scheme is large. Dropped "modest".]

Tier: [M].

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

1. Equate the technical terms. Parabrahman, Ain Soph and the One go in one line. The Theosophical Glossary (1892) and the glossaries in The Key to Theosophy do this at length. The weakness is that each system's own problem drops out: Brahman is a ground of being, Buddhist emptiness is not a One, Ain Soph is a hidden divine infinity.

2. Read myths and symbols as veiled doctrine. Isis and the Secret Doctrine treat the flood, the fall, the serpent and the lotus as one symbolic language. Nearly any story can be read this way, so the method cannot be tested.

3. Pile up authorities. Isis cites authorities from Plato, Philo and Origen to Max Mueller in great number. [Checker: the colleague wrote "on one page"; I know of no such page and cannot support the detail, so it is dropped.] The nosegay image (flowers from others, only the string one's own) is Montaigne's, not hers: Essais III, 12, "De la physionomie". She quotes it, I think in the Preface of The Secret Doctrine, and that locator is [U]; so the essay may say "Blavatsky quotes Montaigne's nosegay" and must not give the line to her. [M on Montaigne; U on her locator and wording] Coleman argues that many quotations were second hand.

4. A history that explains agreement and difference. One archaic Wisdom-Religion kept by initiates explains why the religions agree. Priestcraft, literal reading, lost keys and the split between outer and inner teaching explain why they differ. Cycles and root races explain the layering.

5. Hidden teachers as the proof. The Masters are said to have read the original "Book of Dzyan" and sent letters. This is the unverifiable part.

6. Science as an ally (Hammer's second strategy).

7. Difference as a lower level. Besant's Four Great Religions (1897) and Esoteric Christianity (1901) read each religion by its "esoteric" core, with Theosophy as the key [U on dates].

A contrast with Alex's method (my own observation, not a scholar's): hers presupposes a single origin and secret custodians, and his uses a single logic, an axiom and its consequences, plus examples. His claim is the more modest of the two, and it needs no hidden teacher.

## 5. What scholars say, short

Credited by historians:
- It gave Asian religions a hearing in the West and gave some Asians self-respect under colonial rule (Gandhi's own report, Dharmapala, Besant's college).
- It spread karma, reincarnation, states after death and the habit of comparative reading. It was a main channel and not the only one: Kardec's Spiritism came first.

Judged wrong by specialists, by tradition:
- Hinduism: real Sanskrit terms in a re-arranged Western scheme. The sevenfold constitution is not a classical Indian one [M]. Rebirth as a ladder of evolution is a nineteenth-century Western idea laid over Indian sources [M]. [Checker: cumulative progress across lives is in the Indian sources too (Gita 6.41 to 45), so the claim is about the evolutionary frame, not about growth over lives as such.]
- Buddhism: "esoteric Buddhism" is a misnomer, the Buddha taught no secret doctrine, and an enduring reincarnating Ego strains against anatta.
- Tibet: the Mahatmas and the Book of Dzyan have no Tibetan source.
- Method: its history (Lemuria, Atlantis, root races, the Stanzas) is fantasy, and its authority rested on unverifiable Masters and proven borrowing.

Open: whether Blavatsky was a fraud, a sincere creator of fictions, or both. Scholarship does not need the answer.

## 9. Perennial philosophy beyond Huxley (added in the check pass; every item [M], none searched)

Why this section exists: Alex's note says there are plenty of other books in the perennial philosophy movement beyond what Huxley speaks about, and that he is not even the best. The T06 slice never leaned on Huxley, but the essay's plan treats "the perennial philosophy" and Theosophy as the two comparing movements, and the first must not shrink to one 1945 anthology. Titles and dates are from memory and are not for printing until opened. Which book is "best" is a judgement, so I give a fit for Alex's method and no ranking.

The idea and its name [M]:
- Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), gave the phrase currency. Leibniz used "philosophia perennis" in a letter of 1714 (to Nicolas Remond [U]). The Renaissance "prisca theologia" of Ficino and Pico is the older form of the claim (Walker, The Ancient Theology, 1972, in finding 24). Huxley's Foreword credits Leibniz [M].

The Traditionalist (Perennialist) school, in the strict twentieth-century sense [M]:
- Rene Guenon: Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921); Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921), his attack on the other movement.
- Ananda K. Coomaraswamy: for example Hinduism and Buddhism (1943) [date U].
- Frithjof Schuon: De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948); in English The Transcendent Unity of Religions (1953; a revised edition in 1975 with an introduction by Huston Smith [dates U]). Fit for Alex: the outer forms differ and the inner, esoteric core agrees, which is the move his brief asks for.
- Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis, Seyyed Hossein Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, 1981 [date U]).
- Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971): a very large thematic anthology across traditions; a place to find passages by theme (the illusion, the soul, the return) [M on the book; U on how carefully it is checked].
- Huston Smith: The Religions of Man (1958; later The World's Religions); Forgotten Truth: The Common Vision of the World's Religions (1976). Fit for Alex: the most readable statement of the one-worldview-with-levels idea.

Other comparers, not Traditionalists [M]:
- Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911). Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926, English 1932), which sets Eckhart beside Shankara. W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960). Alan Watts, The Supreme Identity (1950). Ken Wilber, for the "levels" version [dates U].

How scholars treat the movement, relevant to this file [M]:
- Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004), is the history of the Traditionalist school and the best way to know who said what and who attacked whom (finding 23). Hammer's "appeal to tradition" (finding 4) applies to the Theosophical and the Traditionalist claim alike; so does Hanegraaff's reading of "ancient wisdom" as a category to be studied (finding 3).
- Critics Alex should know exist, since his rule is to say honestly where a specialist objects: R. C. Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957), who denies that all mystical experience is one; Steven T. Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978), the constructivist argument that mystical experience is shaped by the tradition and so is no common core. Note that the Traditionalists do not rest their claim on experience but on metaphysical doctrine, so Katz's quarrel mostly misses them [M; see P12].

Traps [M]:
- Huxley's Perennial Philosophy (1945) is an anthology with commentary built mainly from Christian, Sufi, Indian and Chinese sources; it is neither a Traditionalist text nor a Theosophical one. Do not use it as the voice of either.
- Quotations on oneness attributed on the internet to "Blavatsky", "Huxley", "Einstein", "Rumi" or "Eckhart" are often misattributed. Quote only from a printed text that has been opened.
