## 1. The picture in brief

The draft's motive step offers Eastern, Neoplatonic, Kabbalistic and Sufi witnesses and then says a whole that wants to know itself needs a gap. That sentence is close to the working position of a German line (Boehme, Schiller, Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) and of a Russian line that learned from it (Solovyov, Florensky, Bulgakov, Berdyaev, Frank). They state the three clauses as philosophy: one whole, it comes to know itself by passing through otherness, and the otherness is overcome in a richer unity.

Four points matter for the essay.

First, the Western answer to "why" is mostly need or longing or the logic of self-consciousness. The Eastern answer is mostly play (lila) or overflow. A minority of the Russians answer with free sacrificial love. The essay should say which it is borrowing. [Checker: the Eastern side is not one answer either; see item 37. Say "at least two" kinds of answer, not "two".]

Second, these authors keep a difference. Hegel mocks a One that swallows all distinctions. Teilhard says union differentiates. Whitehead says the One is increased. Lossky allows union with God in his energies and never in his essence. This is the Christian participation against identity, and it answers the fear that return means loss.

Third, they are not perennialists. Hegel and Solovyov rank religions with Christianity on top. Schelling compares by process. Guenon and Schuon would reject a Principle that needs anything. The lineage witnesses for the logic, not for "the same thing in different words".

Fourth, two corrections: the Blavatsky critic named Solovyov is the philosopher's brother, and the Eckhart eye line has no known place on the Cologne list.

C. Schelling

8. Schelling, the ground in God (Philosophische Untersuchungen ueber das Wesen der menschlichen Freiheit, 1809; English: Gutmann 1936; Hayden-Roy 1987; Love and Schmidt 2006). Claim: God has in himself a ground of his existence that is not God considered absolutely, nature in God. It precedes him logically, not in time. The longing the eternal One feels to give birth to itself is, as I recall the sentence, not the One itself but coeternal with it (gleichewig), and a will in which there is as yet no understanding; the colleague's gloss "its first stirring" may merge this with a nearby clause on the first stirring of divine existence [U, checker]. All birth is from darkness into light. Words: I recall the longing sentence closely [M, 85 percent], but treat as paraphrase. Traditions: Boehme (named), Spinoza, Kant. East: Scholem saw a likeness to Lurianic contraction, with no proved path (Boehme, Oetinger, Molitor [U]). Keeps: a personal God, a real difference between God and creature. Tier [M]. Use: clause two almost literally.

9. Schelling, the separation as will. Claim: evil is the particular will exalting itself over the universal will, when the creature wants to be apart what it is only in unity. Selfhood is the dark ground in a person; raised to spirit and joined to love it is the highest thing. Hence a veil of melancholy over all nature. The end is God all in all (echo of 1 Corinthians 15:28 [U]). Tier [M]. Use: a Western definition of the "I" that holds itself apart. It fits the fist and the wall, and it says selfhood is transformed, not deleted.

## 4. How they compare

They rarely say "same words, different traditions". Four methods occur.

Boehme and Fichte compare nothing across religions. They read one religion at two depths, outer and inner (Fichte: John against Paul), the perennial structure inside one tradition.

Schelling compares by process. Myths are stages of one consciousness, found in each people at its place. Resemblance is explained by one process, not borrowing, and the order has a top.

Hegel compares by form. One content, Spirit, appears in pictures and in concepts. The ranking is by freedom and adequacy. His Indian verdicts rested on 1820s sources, and his own idea of his task was to show Christianity as the concept's religion. Solovyov copies the move with Godmanhood as the measure.

Later comparers drop the ranking. Hartshorne sorts by logical type, Teilhard and Whitehead by function, the Kyoto School by dialogue (Eckhart with sunyata). The Traditionalists reject the process view and keep the doctrine.

For the essay: Alex's own method, "different words, same principles", is closest to Fichte (inner against outer) and to Whitehead's tone. Saying "Hegel agrees" would be wrong. He agrees on the logic and disagrees on the equality of religions.

## 5. What scholars say

Hegel scholarship is split. Metaphysical readers (Taylor, 1975; Magee, Hegel and the Hermetic Tradition, 2001, who stresses Boehme and Hermeticism) take the Absolute's self-knowledge seriously. Cyril O'Regan, The Heterodox Hegel (1994), reads him as a modern Gnostic. Non-metaphysical readers (Pippin, Pinkard) take Spirit as a social-normative achievement and drop the divine subject [M]. Voegelin called Hegel and Schelling modern Gnostics, Jonas drew a gnostic-modern analogy, and Blumenberg disputed the thesis [M]. Benz is the standard on Schelling and Boehme [M]. Halbfass shows how limited Hegel's Indian sources were. For the Russians: the sophiology was censured in 1935, and Florovsky and Lossky answered it. Tsiolkovsky's cosmism has a respected side and a grim one.

## 9. Perennial and Traditionalist authors beyond Huxley (added in answer to Alex's remark; all [M] or [U], none searched)

Alex's point is right on the facts that matter here: Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is one anthology with his commentary, and the movement is much wider than that book. This slice keeps him out. Where the essay wants a perennial voice on the motive question or on Boehme and Eckhart, these are the places to look, and the sibling files (P02 to P09, G2-2 section 9) hold the detail. I do not rank them.

- Before the modern movement: Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), is where the phrase becomes a book title; Leibniz used the phrase philosophia perennis in a letter of 1715 to Remond (P01). Neither is Huxley, and Leibniz did not coin it [M].
- The Traditionalist school: Rene Guenon (Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues, 1921; Le Regne de la quantite, 1945); Ananda Coomaraswamy (Hinduism and Buddhism, 1943; many essays, and he cites Eckhart and Boehme); Frithjof Schuon (De l'unite transcendante des religions, 1948, English 1953; Logic and Transcendence; Esoterism as Principle and as Way); Titus Burckhardt on Sufism; Martin Lings; Seyyed Hossein Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, 1981); Huston Smith (Forgotten Truth, 1976); Whitall Perry (A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom, 1971) [M, titles and years; the order is not a ranking].
- The motive question inside that school: Schuon answers with Infinity and possibility (the Absolute is infinite, so it includes the possibility of the relative; P05); Guenon says the Principle neither changes nor needs anything, so manifestation adds nothing to it (P02); Coomaraswamy wrote on lila (the essay "Lila" in the Journal of the American Oriental Society, 1941 [U]). None of them uses the German "need".
- Comparers who are not Traditionalists: Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926; Eckhart set beside Shankara), the closest to this slice; W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960) [M].
- Caution carried from G2-2 L18: Guenon wrote against Theosophy, so the two movements Alex leans on are rivals as well as cousins, and the essay should not call them one tradition.
