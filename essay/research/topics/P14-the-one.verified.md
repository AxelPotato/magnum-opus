## 1. The picture in brief

The perennial authors share a scheme more than a doctrine. It has three rungs and a return. At the top is a Source with no qualities that no name reaches: Brahman without qualities, Eckhart's Godhead, the Essence (Dhat), Ein Sof, the One of Plotinus, the nameless Tao, and, in the comparers' reading, the Void or Dharmakaya. Below it is the same Reality turned toward the world as a Lord with names: Ishvara, God, Allah as Rabb, the Sefirot. Below that is manifestation: Maya, Existence, the many. The human self (Atman, the Intellect, the spark, "something uncreated") is where the Source is present in the creature, which is what "that art thou" points at. Then comes the return: moksha, fana, theosis, nirvana, reditus.

They answer "why does the One appear as many" in four ways. Necessity: the Infinite cannot exclude anything, or Goodness overflows (Guénon, Schuon, Plotinus, Plato's Timaeus, Dionysius). Desire, play or delight (Brihadaranyaka 1.4, Taittiriya, lila, Aurobindo, Watts, Wilber). Relation: the Names need someone to be named to, or the Infinite withdraws to make room (Ibn Arabi, tzimtzum). Refusal: at the top the question cannot be asked (Gaudapada, the Buddha, the Rigveda's creation hymn). A second question, why I do not remember, gets its own answers: audacity (Plotinus), sleep and merging (Chandogya), hiding and recognition (Kashmir Shaivism).

The honest limits: the equations are the comparers', not the traditions'. Buddhists, Jewish scholars, Orthodox and Catholic theologians and many Islamic scholars each keep a line between Source and many that the comparers erase. "Advaita" means "not two", which is not "one". And Guénon fought Theosophy and Western reincarnation by name (Le Théosophisme, 1921; L'Erreur spirite, 1923), and Schuon's school followed [M]. The two movements are not allies, and the essay should not present them as one [C1].

## 2. Findings

### A. The authors' own accounts

1. Guénon: the Principle, and manifestation as a consequence of Infinity.
Claim: the supreme Principle lies beyond Being. Being is its first determination. The Principle is the Infinite and holds all possibility, manifested and not. Since nothing can lie outside the Infinite, manifestation (which looks like a limit) must be included in it, so manifestation follows from Infinity with no motive and no choice. A human life is one state among indefinitely many, and the true Self is touched by none of them.
Who: René Guénon, Les États multiples de l'être (1932), early chapters on Being and Non-Being and on the Infinite and Possibility; L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vêdânta (1925). Locators unknown.
The words: paraphrase.
Parallels cited: Brahman beyond Ishvara, Eckhart's Godhead, Taoist non-being, Plato's Good beyond being [M that he draws these; U on exact pairings].
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Sedgwick (2004) treats Guénon as founder of an esoteric current. Indologists note that his Vedanta is Shankara's Advaita, one school among several, some of which read the world as real.
Use: the best precedent for Alex's "logical consequences" style, and a firmer answer to "why separation" than "the whole wants to know itself". Caution: Guénon denied reincarnation as Spiritists and Theosophists teach it and wrote against Theosophy (P02 reports, [V in P02]).

2. Schuon: Absolute, Infinite, Maya inside Atma.
Claim: Reality is Absolute and Infinite. The Infinite holds every possibility, including a relative that looks like not-the-Absolute. That is Maya, real in its order, unreal if taken as separate. Reality has three degrees: Beyond-Being (his word, replacing Guénon's Non-Being), Being (the personal Lord), Existence. The ego is a ray that mistakes itself for a source. Return is remembrance and deliverance, which is identity and not arrival.
Who: Frithjof Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953); Logic and Transcendence (1970); Esoterism as Principle and as Way (French 1978, English 1981 [M]) [C2]; The Play of Masks (1992). P05 covers this at length. Locators unknown.
The words: paraphrase.
Parallels cited: Nirguna and Saguna Brahman, Dhat and Allah, Godhead and God, Ein Sof and the Sefirot, Plotinus's One, the Void [M; Jewish and Buddhist pairs U as his wording].
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: this is a comparer's grid, and Buddhist, Jewish and Christian specialists reject some pairs. The movement around him has a contested history (Sedgwick 2004).
Use: "fix the level before you equate".

3. Coomaraswamy: one Self, one transmigrant, lila.
Claim: the Upanishads, Plato, Eckhart and the Pali texts teach one philosophia perennis. The Self is the sole subject. The only transmigrant is the one Self in all beings, and the individual is a mask or persona. Maya comes from a root meaning to measure, the One's creative power. Lila is play with no motive outside itself.
Who: A. K. Coomaraswamy, "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3, 1944, printed together with "Recollection, Indian and Platonic" [M]); Hinduism and Buddhism (1943); "The Dance of Siva" (1918); a short paper "Lila" in JAOS (my recall: vol. 61, 1941, pp. 98 to 101 [U]). The first-pass researchers of P02 and P03 reported a library catalogue record for the transmigrant paper (https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/000146331). The verification passes of P02 and P03 could not re-open it, so the title is not confirmed by a search result; my memory agrees with the title, the year and the pairing [C3].
The words: paraphrase. The paper opens on a Shankara saying that there is no other transmigrant but the Lord (first-pass reports in P02 and P05, not re-seen; Brahma Sutra Bhashya 1.1.5; my memory agrees with the locator [M]).
Parallels cited: Plato, Eckhart, Pali texts, Islam [M].
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Pali scholars (Collins 1982, Rahula) reject his reading that the Buddha denied only the empirical self.
Use: the best Traditionalist answer to Alex's "if there is one mind, who carries over". The answer: the one Self.

4. Smith and Perry: the scheme made readable.
Claim: Smith presents four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite) and four of selfhood (body, mind, soul, spirit), with the Infinite as Godhead and Spirit as Atman. He argues from infinity as Schuon does (P09 reports the same). Perry's Treasury arranges quotations from all traditions under the Traditionalist outline.
Who: Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976; revised 1992); Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971).
Parallels cited: the four states of the Mandukya Upanishad, the Chain of Being, Maya as the Infinite's possibility [M].
Tier: [M]; Perry's table of contents [U].
Specialists add: Prothero (2010) argues the religions pose different problems. Religious studies mostly treats the "primordial tradition" as a philosophy and not a finding.
Use: Smith is the easiest honest summary of the Traditionalist metaphysic for a general reader.

5. Nasr, Lings, Burckhardt, Schaya, Shah-Kazemi: the same grid on Islam and Kabbalah.
Claim: Burckhardt and Lings give the Sufi doctrine of the Essence (Dhat) beyond the Names, the cosmos as self-disclosure (tajalli), the Perfect or Universal Man. Schaya reads Kabbalah in the same grid: Ein Sof beyond the Sefirot, tzimtzum as withdrawal, the four worlds. Nasr gives the school its academic voice (Knowledge and the Sacred, Gifford Lectures, 1981). Shah-Kazemi sets Shankara, Ibn Arabi and Eckhart side by side.
Who: Titus Burckhardt, Introduction to Sufi Doctrine (English 1959) [U on year]; Martin Lings, The Book of Certainty (1952) and What Is Sufism? (1975); Leo Schaya, The Universal Meaning of the Kabbalah (English 1971; the French original, as I recall, is L'Homme et l'Absolu selon la Kabbale, 1958, not 1960 [U; a correction from memory of an item that was already [U]]) [C4]; Reza Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence (2006).
Tier: [M] for works and gist, [U] for details.
Specialists add: Chittick's Ibn Arabi is the careful scholarly one (The Sufi Path of Knowledge, 1989). Scholem and Idel do not read Ein Sof as Nirguna Brahman, because Ein Sof stands in a dynamic and even theurgical relation to the Sefirot.
Use: the most direct three-way comparison of Brahman and Maya, Dhat and Names, Godhead and God is Shah-Kazemi's.

6. Huxley, Underhill, James: older anthologists and comparers.
Claim: Huxley's chapters 1 and 2, "That Art Thou" and "The Nature of the Ground", state the metaphysic. He uses Eckhart's pair Godhead and God to reconcile impersonal and personal traditions. His Foreword says, as I recall, that the Reality is "substantial" to the world, stronger than Alex's "underlying" [U]. The same recall is in P08.verified; it is one memory, not two sources. The Foreword also credits Leibniz with coining the phrase "philosophia perennis". That is wrong: Agostino Steuco used it as a title in 1540 (De perenni philosophia), and Leibniz reused it in a letter to Nicolas Remond in 1714 (P01 and P08 report search records for both, not re-seen by me) [M] [C5]. Do not repeat Huxley's attribution. Underhill (1911) puts the Absolute, the ground and spark of the soul, and a five-stage Mystic Way at the centre. James (1902) lists the shared notes of mystic reports and stops short of metaphysics.
Who: Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945); Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902, the mysticism lectures).
Tier: [M]; Huxley's wording [U].
Specialists add: Zaehner (1957) argued that nature, monistic and theistic mysticism differ and criticised Huxley's equations. Katz (1978) argued that mystic experience is shaped by tradition.
Use: Huxley is the popular door. For argument use Guénon, Schuon, Coomaraswamy. Underhill's stages are a precedent for the arrow.

7. Wilber, Watts, Aurobindo: the modern "why".
Claim: Wilber says Spirit is ground and goal. Spirit "involves" into matter by stages of self-forgetting, then evolves back. The ego's "Atman project" tries to rebuild wholeness by the wrong means, and the self-other boundary is drawn by the mind. Watts pictures God playing hide and seek with himself. Aurobindo says Brahman involved itself in matter for delight and evolves back through life, mind and supermind, with the world a real manifestation, not an illusion (P13 reports the same).
Who: Ken Wilber, The Spectrum of Consciousness (1977), No Boundary (1979), The Atman Project (1980), Up from Eden (1981), Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995); Alan Watts, The Supreme Identity (1950), The Book (1966); Sri Aurobindo, The Life Divine (serial 1914 to 1919, book 1939 to 1940).
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Ferrer (2002) rejects Wilber's fixed hierarchy. Aurobindo is not an Advaitin on Maya.
Use: Wilber's boundary language is the closest modern English match to Alex's wall and border. Aurobindo's supermind is a precedent for a stage beyond the top of the scale.

### B. The texts and traditions they lean on

8. The Upanishads on why the One becomes many.
Claim: Chandogya 6.2.1 to 3: Being alone, one without a second, thought "may I be many". Taittiriya 2.6: he desired to be many and be born. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.1 to 4: the Self alone looked round, was afraid, then was not delighted, wished for a second, and divided himself in two, from which couples and then all pairs followed. Mundaka 2.1.1: as sparks leap from a fire, so beings come forth from the Imperishable and return into it. Gita 15.7: the individual soul is a fragment of the Lord's own self.
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M], verse numbers [M] except Taittiriya [U].
Specialists add: translators such as Olivelle stress that the Upanishads speak in many voices, and that Advaita is one organised reading. Advaitins treat the Brihadaranyaka story as told from the level of appearance.
Use: Alex's essay quotes 1.4.10 only. Verses 1.4.1 to 4 are the most direct text on the why: loneliness, a wish for a second, self-division. It is a story with a motive, while Guénon and Schuon give a logic with none. State which one he means.

9. The Upanishads on forgetting, and the two birds.
Claim: Chandogya 6.9 and 6.10: as the honey of many trees does not know which tree it came from, and rivers do not know they were water from east or west, so creatures who merge into Being in deep sleep or death do not know it, and return as what they were, tiger, worm, gnat. Katha 2.1.10 and 11: one who sees plurality goes from death to death. Brihadaranyaka 2.4.14: where there is duality, as it were, one sees another. Mundaka 3.1.1 (and Svetasvatara 4.6, Rigveda 1.164.20): two birds on one tree, one eats, one looks on. The freeing line, that the eater is released from sorrow on seeing the other, is the next verse, Mundaka 3.1.2 (Svetasvatara 4.7) [M] [C6].
Tier: [M].
Use: Chandogya 6.10 gives the traditional reason for "why you don't remember" and the link to lifetimes in one passage. The two birds are the program and the processor.

10. Maya, lila, "never happened", and recognition.
Claim: in the Rigveda (6.47.18, quoted at Brihadaranyaka 2.5.19) Indra goes about in many forms through his maya, so maya first meant power or craft. Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 (lokavat tu lilakaivalyam) answers "why create" with lila, play. 2.1.32 is the objection (a creation without purpose) and 2.1.33 is the reply, so do not cite 2.1.32 for lila (P03.verified makes the same point [M]) [C7]. Shankara's gloss, as I recall, compares it to the unmotivated activity of a king with every wish fulfilled, or to breathing. Gaudapada's Karikas teach that nothing has ever arisen, so the question belongs to the level of appearance. Kashmir Shaivism (Utpaladeva, Abhinavagupta) says the Absolute is free consciousness (svatantrya) that manifests, conceals and grants grace. The world is a real appearance of it, and liberation is pratyabhijna, recognition of what one already is.
Tier: [M]; Gaudapada's locator [U].
Specialists add: Hacker and others hold that "maya" weighs less in Shankara himself than avidya and name-and-form, and that "illusion" is a later reading [U]. Shaiva scholars keep the world real, unlike Advaita.
Use: Kashmir Shaivism has Alex's story (a whole hides from itself, then recognises itself) in technical form, and the perennial authors rarely use it. "The question cannot be asked" is the Advaita answer and should be named.

11. Plotinus, Proclus, Dionysius, Eriugena: procession and return.
Claim: Plotinus's One is beyond being and thought, and is all things and none of them (Enneads V.2.1). Being perfect it overflows, without will or loss (V.2.1; III.8.10 for the spring image). Souls forgot the Father through audacity (tolma) and the wish to belong to themselves (V.1.1). Ennead IV.9 asks whether all souls are one. The return is the flight of the alone to the Alone (VI.9.11). Proclus (Elements of Theology, props 30 to 35) gives the triad remaining, procession, reversion. Dionysius (Divine Names 4) speaks of God's ecstatic love taking him outside himself in creating. Eriugena's Periphyseon (ninth century; the dating varies between authors, somewhere in the 860s to about 870, so write only "ninth century" [U]) [C8] makes the whole a circle, ending in nature that neither creates nor is created, to which all returns.
Tier: [M]; the locators are [M] except Dionysius [U].
Specialists add: for Plotinus the overflow is necessary, the world is good (against the Gnostics, II.9), and there are three real hypostases, so he is not a monist in Shankara's sense. Pope Honorius III condemned the Periphyseon in 1225 (the repo's notes report the date).
Use: the essay's three clauses (one, separation, return) are the Neoplatonic triad of remaining, procession, reversion. Aquinas's Summa is often read in the same pattern. Ennead IV.9 and V.1.1 are the nearest Greek lines to the axiom.

12. Eckhart: Gottheit and Gott, the ground.
Claim: God (Got) is what the Godhead (Gotheit) is in relation to creatures. In the Godhead there is no activity and no distinction. Eckhart says he prays God to rid him of God, that God and Godhead differ as heaven and earth, and that God "becomes" when creatures say "God". God's ground and my ground are one ground. There is something in the soul that is uncreated. The eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me. The way is detachment (abegescheidenheit) and breakthrough.
Who: German Sermons, in Quint's numbering roughly 52 ("rid me of God"), 56 (God and Godhead, God "becomes"), 5b ("God's ground and my ground"), 13 (the uncreated something) and 12 (the eye) [U on every number; my recall puts the "rid me of God" line in Q52 and the eye line in Q12, but Walshe and McGinn number differently, so cite the sermon's opening words and the translator's number, not Quint alone]. The bull In agro dominico (27 March 1329, 28 propositions, 17 condemned as heretical and 11 as ill-sounding and suspect, issued after his death) lists the "uncreated" saying [M; the 17 and 11 split U]. P04 and P05 report the same date and the same saying. Whether the "eye" saying was raised in Cologne in 1326, as the essay says, I cannot confirm [U].
Tier: [M] for the ideas.
Specialists add: McGinn (2001) and Turner (1995) read the Godhead as the Trinity's ground and the union as by grace, with the Creator and creature line kept. Otto (1926) found deep parallels with Shankara and deep differences.
Use: "God becomes when creatures say God" is the plainest Western statement that the Source appears as Lord through relation.

13. Ibn Arabi and the Sufi tradition.
Claim: God is incomparable (tanzih) and similar (tashbih) at once, and the knower holds both. The world is "He and not He". The Names need objects to be exercised, and the Breath of the Merciful brings the possibilities into being. The cosmos is God's self-disclosure. Later followers distinguish the Essence's absolute unity (ahadiyya) from unity in the Names (wahidiyya). Hallaj's "ana al-Haqq" (executed 922) is read as God speaking through an annihilated self. The hadith of voluntary devotion (Bukhari 6502) says God becomes the servant's hearing, sight and hand. "I was a hidden treasure" is not accepted as a hadith.
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: the phrase wahdat al-wujud is, as I recall, from later followers and not Ibn Arabi [U]. Sirhindi (d. 1624) argued for unity of witness only. Ibn Taymiyya attacked the school. Chittick (1989, 1998) is the careful reading.
Use: "the Names need someone to be named to" is the best classical Islamic "why", and Part two already has the hidden-treasure caveat.

14. Kabbalah and Hasidism.
Claim: Ein Sof, the Infinite, is beyond all description. In Luria's account (Safed, 1500s), Ein Sof withdraws (tzimtzum) to leave room, a ray enters, the vessels break, sparks fall, and human work repairs them (tikkun). Adam's one soul contained all souls. Cordovero held that God is all reality though not all reality is God [U on the exact formula; it is a standard summary of his panentheism, not a quotation]. Habad (Shneur Zalman) reads tzimtzum as apparent from our side only: from God's side nothing exists besides Him (acosmism). Whether Habad is strictly acosmist is itself debated among scholars of Hasidism, so say "close to acosmism in the Tanya's reading" and not "teaches acosmism" [U] [C9].
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Scholem (1941) read tzimtzum as a divine exile into self, and Idel (1988) disputes his reading of devekut and Gnostic roots. Hasidism's Lithuanian opponents feared pantheism. In Judaism separation is a real rupture to repair, not an illusion.
Use: tzimtzum and the broken vessels say why a gap is needed, and that the gap is a wound to repair. This fits the fingers better than Maya does.

15. Buddhism.
Claim: Perennialists equate Dharmakaya, Buddha-nature or the Void with the Ground (Schuon, Suzuki, Huxley's Clear Light). The closest Buddhist texts are the Awakening of Faith (One Mind, two gates, ignorance arising as a sudden thought), the Tathagatagarbha sutras, Huayan, Dzogchen (the ground, gzhi, and the clear light at death, recognised or missed), and Yogacara (the storehouse consciousness carrying seeds between lives). The Buddha declined to say what began samsara, and Nagarjuna denies any intrinsic nature, including a One.
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Critical Buddhism (Hakamaya, Matsumoto; Hubbard and Swanson 1997) calls Buddha-nature a non-Buddhist "locus" theory. Theravada has no ground. Lopez (2011) shows Evans-Wentz's Tibetan Book of the Dead was shaped by Vedantic and Theosophical language.
Use: the Awakening of Faith has Alex's structure; say Buddhists would not call it a One.

16. Taoism.
Claim: Tao Te Ching 1: the Tao that can be spoken is not the constant Tao. Ch. 25: something formless and complete before heaven and earth. Ch. 42: Tao gave birth to One, One to Two, Two to Three, Three to the ten thousand things. Zhuangzi 2: heaven and earth were born with me and the ten thousand things are one with me. Zhuangzi 7: Hundun (Chaos), the Emperor of the Center, died on the seventh day when two other rulers, Shu and Hu (Watson: Brief and Sudden), the emperors of the South and North Seas, drilled one of the seven openings of a human face into him each day, to repay his hospitality [M] [C10].
Tier: [M].
Specialists add: Graham, Roth and Ames caution against reading Tao as a metaphysical Ground. Izutsu (1966) compares structures without claiming identity.
Use: Hundun is the cost of differentiation in one short story. Chapter 42 is a "One becomes many" line the essay lacks.

17. Christian scripture, and the lines the churches keep.
Claim: Romans 11:36 (from him, through him, to him are all things), Acts 17:28 (in him we live and move), Ephesians 4:6, Colossians 1:17, John 17:21 (that they all be one), 1 Corinthians 15:28 (God all in all). Athanasius: God became man so that we might become gods (De Incarnatione 54, section 3 in the usual division [M]). Luke 17:21 is "within you" or "among you", and translators split; Thomas logion 3 supports "inside" [U].

Specialists add: Orthodoxy keeps participation and not identity, with Palamas's essence and energies. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215, canon 2, against Joachim of Fiore) says any likeness between Creator and creature contains a greater unlikeness [M]. Orthodox and Catholic theologians do not accept the perennialist mapping of Godhead and Trinity onto Beyond-Being and Being.
Use: say "participation" for Christianity. Strong on return and the one body, weaker on a whole that experiences separation as itself.

18. Kenosis and creation as self-limitation.
Claim: Philippians 2:7 says Christ emptied himself, which concerns the Son and not creation. Later thinkers extend it: Moltmann (1985) borrows tzimtzum; Weil says God could create only by hiding; Masao Abe compares kenosis with Buddhist emptiness (1990).

19. Theosophy: the nearest nineteenth century statement of the axiom.
Claim: Blavatsky's Secret Doctrine (1888), Proem, gives three fundamental propositions: an omnipresent, eternal, boundless and immutable Principle beyond thought; the universe appearing and disappearing in cycles, like tides; and the fundamental identity of all souls with the Universal Over-Soul, with an obligatory pilgrimage for each soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Terms: Parabrahman for the Absolute, the Monad as the spark, Over-Soul (Emerson's coinage). The Society's universal brotherhood rests on the same unity.
Tier: [M] for the gist, close to the original from memory; page numbers unknown (pagination differs between editions, so cite "Proem, third fundamental proposition" and the page of the edition used). My recall of the third proposition's wording is close to: "the fundamental identity of all Souls with the Universal Over-Soul, the latter being itself an aspect of the Unknown Root; and the obligatory pilgrimage for every Soul, a spark of the former, through the Cycle of Incarnation (or Necessity) in accordance with Cyclic and Karmic law, during the whole term" [U; recalled, not for quotation; sibling T02 gives the same wording from its own recall, so this is one memory and not two sources]. Check the Proem before quoting; it is public domain.
Specialists add: Campbell (1980), Godwin (1994) and Hanegraaff (1996) place Theosophy in modern esotericism. Guénon attacked it (1921), and Schuon's school followed.

### C. The movement is wider than Huxley (added in the verification pass, after Alex's remark)

21. Other perennialist books, and why Huxley is one door among many.
Claim: Alex's remark, relayed with this task, is that the perennial philosophy movement has many more books than Huxley's, and that Huxley is not even the best of them. The first part is a plain fact. Whether he is "the best" is a judgement, so this file does not rank anybody. What can be said without ranking: Huxley's book is an anthology with commentary, arranged by theme, and for the metaphysic of the One it offers quotations with linking commentary more than a sustained argument. The authors who argue the metaphysic are Guénon, Schuon and Coomaraswamy, with Huston Smith as the best-known expositor for general readers. The books most useful to this slice, grouped by what they do:
(a) Metaphysics of the One and of manifestation, in argument form: Guénon, Les États multiples de l'être (1932) and Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta (1925); Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions (French 1948, English 1953), Logic and Transcendence (1970) and, as I recall, Survey of Metaphysics and Esoterism (English 1986) [M; the 1986 date U]; Coomaraswamy, Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), "On the One and Only Transmigrant" and "Recollection, Indian and Platonic" (1944), Time and Eternity (1947) [M].
(b) Expositions and anthologies for a general reader, built on that metaphysic: Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976); Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), which is a far larger anthology than Huxley's, arranged by the Traditionalist outline [M]; Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981) [M].
(c) Comparative studies of the One across particular traditions: Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence (2006: Shankara, Ibn Arabi, Eckhart); Burckhardt, Introduction to Sufi Doctrine; Lings, The Book of Certainty (1952); Schaya, The Universal Meaning of the Kabbalah; Izutsu, Sufism and Taoism (1966, a structural comparison that does not claim identity); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (English 1932, German West-östliche Mystik 1926, on Eckhart and Shankara) [M].
(d) Older and modern comparers who start from experience: Underhill, Mysticism (1911); James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902); Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960) and The Teachings of the Mystics (1960) [M; the second title U on year].
(e) Earlier and nearby voices: Steuco (1540); Emerson, "The Over-Soul" (1841); Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine (1888); Radhakrishnan, Eastern Religions and Western Thought (1939); Bede Griffiths, Return to the Centre (1976); Raimon Panikkar, The Unknown Christ of Hinduism (1964) [M; the last three U on year].

Specialists add: the same critics who dispute the movement (Katz, Zaehner, Sedgwick, Prothero, King) apply to all of these, not only to Huxley. The Traditionalist authors in (a) and (b) are also the ones who were opposed to Theosophy, so the essay cannot use them as allies of Blavatsky (see section 6, lead 8).

## 4. How they compare: the method, concretely

1. Fix a ladder, then equate rungs. Schuon and Guénon set Absolute, Being and Existence first, then slot terms by level: Dhat, Nirguna Brahman and the Godhead sit together, Allah the Lord, Ishvara and God one rung lower. Shah-Kazemi's Shankara, Ibn Arabi and Eckhart trio does it in a book, Smith's four levels for general readers.
2. Sort inner from outer. Each tradition's metaphysicians (Shankara, Ibn Arabi, Eckhart, Dionysius, Lao Tzu) are taken as the inner doctrine and the outer teaching as adaptation. Differences are explained as perspective: each religion stresses one aspect.
3. Anthology and montage. Huxley and Perry set quotations side by side by theme. The risk is the filter: old translations used Christian and Platonic vocabulary, context is cut, and the selection decides the result.
4. Etymology and symbol. Coomaraswamy and Guénon match roots and symbols (maya and measure, persona, the axis) and argue that symbolism is a universal language.
5. Experience first. James, Underhill and Stace (1960) start from what mystics report and look for a core (unity, ineffability, timelessness). Constructivists such as Katz reject the core.
6. Structure without identity. Izutsu (Sufism and Taoism), Sells (Mystical Languages of Unsaying, 1994) and Copleston (Religion and the One, 1982) set structures side by side and say where they diverge.
7. Explaining differences. Schuon's perspectives; Huxley's temperaments; Ramakrishna's lake; Baha'i progressive revelation; Hick's one "Real" met through personal and impersonal faces; Guénon's decline from a primordial tradition; Hindu inclusivism, where other religions are lower stages of Advaita (Hacker). Each is the comparer's claim, not the traditions'.

## 5. What scholars say

Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) is the standard history of Traditionalism and treats it as an esoteric current opposed to modernity. Hanegraaff places perennialism within Western esotericism. Katz (1978) and Proudfoot (1985) argue that experience is shaped by doctrine, so a shared core is partly an artefact. Forman (1990) replies with pure-consciousness reports. Zaehner (1957) separates monistic from theistic mysticism. Hick (1989) keeps the plurality and puts the Real beyond every category, including "one". Ferrer (2002) rejects the single hierarchy. King (1999) and Hacker show that "all is one" often means "Advaita is the top", which is Hindu inclusivism. Prothero (2010) says the religions ask different questions. Defenders include Smith, Nasr, Oldmeadow and Shah-Kazemi. Specialists in Buddhism, Judaism and Orthodoxy each keep a line the comparers erase. All [M]; specific page claims [U].
