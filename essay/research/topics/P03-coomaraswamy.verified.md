## 1. The picture in brief

Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947) is often called the most learned of the three men usually named as founders of the Traditionalist or perennialist school, with Guenon and Schuon (the "most learned" is an admirer's judgement, not a fact). He came to the subject from the other end: geologist, then historian of Indian and Sinhalese art, then from the 1930s a writer on metaphysics who read Sanskrit, Pali, Greek and Latin sources side by side. His claim is that the doctrine of the Veda, the Upanishads, the Buddha's discourses, Plato, Plotinus, Eckhart and the Sufis is one doctrine in different languages, and that the agreement comes from a common source in a metaphysical truth that does not change, and not from borrowing.

His central pairing is the Self and the self. The Self (Atman, Spirit, Plato's nous, Eckhart's spark) is the one real knower, the same in all beings. The self (the mortal, empirical "I") is what the traditions tell you to deny, forget or sacrifice. From that follow his readings of transmigration (the Lord is the only transmigrant), of Buddhism (anatta denies the self, not the Self), and of the way (self-naughting, recollection, the Sun door).

For Alex the value is high and so is the risk. High, because Coomaraswamy states the logic of his axiom better than almost anyone, with checkable texts. Risk, because he was not a collector of witnesses. He held that a tradition is understood from inside and he disliked syncretism. He read Buddhism through Vedanta, a reading many Theravada scholars reject. His school treated Theosophy as a counterfeit and, as far as I know, rejected individual reincarnation. Alex's two comparing movements cannot be quoted as if they agree.

13. Maya, lila and the "why" of the whole

Claim, as I know his argument [M]: maya is not simply illusion. It is the divine power of measuring out the one into forms, from the root ma, to measure or build. ## 4. How he compares: the method, concretely

1. The unit of comparison is a sentence or a symbol, not a religion. He picks one formula (the two birds, the door of the sun, "no transmigrant but the Lord") and follows it through texts in the original languages. Alex already works this way, one consequence at a time.

2. He sets the texts in parallel and lets them speak: an Indian line, a Greek line, a Latin or German line, then a short comment. His claim is that any reader can compare the words and see the sameness. Titles like Figures of Speech or Figures of Thought (1946) [V] state the principle: a symbol is a precise language, not decoration, so a sun door in a temple, a Gospel saying and an Upanishadic verse can be one idea in three media [M].

3. He explains the sameness by a common source, not by borrowing or common psychology. The agreement is of doctrine, which is a metaphysical truth that does not change. Alex differs: he proposes a common logic (the axiom). Coomaraswamy would call the logic the doctrine itself, which is close, but he would deny that a doctrine can be judged by someone standing outside every tradition [M].

4. He explains differences by audience and level. A teaching for householders differs from one for monks. The Buddha's "no self" addressed people who took the empirical self for the Self. His "Paths" essay is the short version: paths differ at the foot and meet at the top, and you cannot see that from the foot [V gist].

5. He distrusts "evolutionism", "psychologism" and "historicism", which flatten old doctrine into early groping [M]. Alex's arrow looks like progress, so a Traditionalist reader may bristle, though they accept ascent on an individual path.

6. He refuses syncretism: blending traditions loses the form that carries the truth, and a person should walk one orthodox path [M]. A reader from his school will find Alex's witness approach thin, and Alex can say so himself.

## 5. What scholars say, short

Respected for the range and precision of his reading, his pioneering work on Indian art, and for bringing Indian metaphysics to Western humanists [M]. Standing criticisms: different periods and schools flattened into one voice; a strong Advaita tilt, most visible on anatta [V, Rahula]; little interest in historical development [M]; the anti-modern politics of his school [M]. The Traditionalists regard him as foundational and the model of rigour [V]. He changed his own mind at least once (findings 8 and 10), though see the caution in finding 8 about whose formula the 1916 line is.

## 9. Other perennialist works besides Huxley (added at the person's request)

The person's correction: the perennial philosophy movement has many more books than the one Huxley wrote, and Huxley is not even the best of them. This section lists the other main works. All are [M] (memory, not searched in either pass); check titles and dates before printing. It is a map, not a ranking; "best" is a judgement the file does not make.

The name and its earlier history.
- Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), the book that gave the phrase "philosophia perennis" its currency [M].
- Leibniz used the phrase "philosophia perennis" in a letter of 1714 to Nicolas-Francois Remond [M].
- Huxley's anthology The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is the popular entry point; it quotes Coomaraswamy often [M]. It is one book among many.

The Traditionalist school, the scholarly spine of the movement.
- Rene Guenon: Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921); L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta (1925, English "Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta"); La Crise du monde moderne (1927); Le Regne de la quantite et les signes des temps (1945) [M].
- Frithjof Schuon: De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English "The Transcendent Unity of Religions", 1953), the most direct statement of the "same truth in different forms" thesis [M].
- Titus Burckhardt: Introduction to Sufi Doctrine (1959) and Alchemy (1960) [M].
- Martin Lings and Marco Pallis (for example Lings, The Book of Certainty, 1952; Pallis, The Way and the Mountain, 1960) [M].
- Whitall N. Perry: A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a very large cross-tradition anthology, closer to Huxley's method than the others [M].
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr: Knowledge and the Sacred (1981, the Gifford Lectures) [M].
- Huston Smith: Forgotten Truth: The Primordial Tradition (1976) [M].
- Ananda Coomaraswamy himself (this file).

The academic common-core line and its critic.
- W. T. Stace: The Teachings of the Mystics and Mysticism and Philosophy (both 1960), an argument that mystical experience has a common core across traditions [M].
- The standard objection: Steven T. Katz (editor), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978), arguing that mystical experience is shaped by the tradition and has no common core [M]. Alex can say this himself, in the same honest way that Coomaraswamy's critics are named in finding 19.

The other comparing movement Alex names, for balance: H. P. Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (1877) and The Secret Doctrine (1888), and The Key to Theosophy (1889) [M]. The Traditionalists regarded Theosophy as a counterfeit (finding 18); the two movements are not one tradition and should not be quoted as agreeing.
