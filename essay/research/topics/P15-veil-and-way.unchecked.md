## 1. The picture in brief

The perennial writers tell one story in three steps. The diagnosis: a false centre has formed, a "me" that takes itself for the whole and lives braced against the rest. The cure: the false centre is seen through, set down or starved, and what was always there shows. The evidence offered: tradition after tradition prescribes the same operations (discern, detach, die to self-will, surrender) under different names.

The words differ more than the comparers admit. Hindu texts speak of avidya (not-knowing), maya (the measuring and veiling power) and ahamkara (the I-maker). The Buddha speaks of craving, clinging and the view that there is a self. Paul speaks of the old man. The German and English mystics speak of self-will and of "I, me, mine". The Sufis speak of the nafs. Each names something a little different, and the comparers equate them. That is the main honesty point for Alex.

Two kinds of explanation of why the veil feels real run through the sources. One is a fault of knowing, a superimposition, as when a rope is taken for a snake. The other is a fault of will, a clutch: craving, self-will. Most traditions carry both. Alex's machine picture suits the first. His BE axis, tension as the wall held up, suits the second, and the traditions' own images (knot, tangle, thread, rust) are about holding.

The comparers also pass on a warning: dying to self can turn cold (Zen's fox, the Free Spirit heresy, quietism). The best material for Alex is mostly in the traditions' own texts and not in the anthologies: the Upanishads tie fear to the arrival of a second, the Yoga Sutras pair practice with letting go, Plotinus compares the way to a sculptor taking stone away, Marcus Aurelius uses the severed limb, and early Chinese thought states the paradox of trying to be effortless.

## 2. Findings

1. The anthologists (Huxley, Perry, Smith). Claim: the obstacle to knowing the Ground is the personal ego, called self-will, and the cure is subtraction. Who: Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945), chapters on mortification and non-attachment, self-knowing, grace and free will (titles per P07, P08); Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a larger themed anthology (contents unknown to me); Huston Smith, The Religions of Man (1958, later The World's Religions), the four yogas (knowledge, devotion, work, meditation) as four temperaments. Words (paraphrase): Huxley reads mortification as dying to self-will, not harming the body, and non-attachment as the root of freedom; he leans on Law, Eckhart, John of the Cross and the Gita. Tier: [M]. Use: they show the theme exists in every tradition; they do not show why. Smith's four yogas are the cleanest "ways" list.

2. The Traditionalists. Guenon: the separate "ego" is one limited state of a larger being, the error is identifying with the part, and the cure is metaphysical realization, which needs initiation, a transmitted spiritual influence (Man and His Becoming according to the Vedanta, 1925; Perspectives on Initiation, 1946). He rejects popular reincarnation of the personality, so he is a counter-voice on lifetimes (P02). Schuon: the way has three parts, discernment of Real from unreal, concentration on the Real through a Name (japa, dhikr, the Jesus Prayer, the nembutsu), and virtue, humility first (Esoterism as Principle and as Way, 1981; P04, P05). He treats maya as relativity with a veiling and a projecting aspect, echoing the Advaita avarana and vikshepa [doctrine M, Schuon's use U]. Coomaraswamy: every tradition says to deny, forget or sacrifice the small self so the Self may be known; "Self-Naughting" and "Atmayajna" set Mark 8:34, John 12:25, the Vedic sacrifice and Eckhart together (titles reported by P03). Tier: [M]. Use: Schuon's three parts are the best compact method list in the movement. Call them his scheme.

3. Wilber, Watts, James: why it feels real, and what turns it. Wilber (The Spectrum of Consciousness, 1977; No Boundary, 1979; The Atman Project, 1980): suffering comes from drawing a boundary between self and not-self, and the "Atman project" is the drive to regain wholeness through substitutes (status, possessions) because the real goal is unseen. Watts (The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are, 1966): the "skin-encapsulated ego" is a social agreement taught to each child. James (Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902, lectures 8 to 10 on the divided self and conversion): the crisis often resolves when effort gives out and the person surrenders. Tier: [M]. Use: the Atman project maps closely onto Alex's Default, Driven and Drifting rooms, and James is a respectable Western witness that release follows the failure of grip, which is the shrinking swing.

4. The Theosophists. Blavatsky, The Voice of the Silence (1889: The Voice of the Silence, The Two Paths, The Seven Portals) says the mind slays the real and the disciple must slay the slayer [recalled near-verbatim, not for quotation], and contrasts private liberation with a path of renunciation taken for the world's sake. Shankara: rope and snake. Tier: [M]. Use: BU 1.4.2 and Taittiriya 2.7 give the draft's fear-and-separation link in the Upanishads' own voice; YS 1.12 and 4.3 are the oldest compact statements of work and stillness together.

6. Buddhist texts, including Zen. ## 4. How they compare: the method, with examples

Five moves recur.

Theme first. The anthologist picks a heading (detachment, charity, the Self) and strings passages from different traditions under it with a short link. Huxley's chapter on non-attachment puts the Gita, Buddhist teaching, Eckhart and Law one after another. Perry and Stace work the same way. It gives a strong impression of agreement and lifts each passage out of its argument.

Term equation. The Traditionalists line up words in a column and say they name one thing: Atman, nous, the spark of the soul; moksha, fana, theosis, nirvana as one event (P05); Eckhart's Godhead and God beside nirguna and saguna Brahman (Otto's formula, P03). The columns belong to the comparer.

Level matching. Guenon and Schuon insist on esoteric beside esoteric: Eckhart with Shankara and Ibn Arabi (Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence, 2006), never the catechism beside the Gita (P04).

Stage maps. Underhill's five stages, Smith's levels, Wilber's spectrum, Teresa's mansions, the ox pictures and the Jain gunasthanas are laid out as one ladder, with little said about how far the rungs were matched by choice.

Explaining differences away, six devices: form against essence (Schuon); skilful means (Buddhist upaya, borrowed by Smith and Wilber); exoteric against esoteric (Guenon); surface against deep structure (Wilber); two faces of one Reality, personal and impersonal (Schuon; Eckhart's God and Godhead); and the blind men and the elephant, told in the Udana, by the Jains and by Rumi, beside Ramakrishna's lake with its landing steps. Finding 17 gives their reasons for why the practice repeats.

What they do less often: ask which term does not translate, or whether one word (maya, nafs, ego) names the same thing in each tradition.

## 5. What scholars say, briefly

Steven Katz argues that no experience is free of the tradition that shapes it, so Buddhist and Christian ends differ (P11). Zaehner argued that Sufi fana and Vedantic identity differ [M]. Eckhart specialists (McGinn, Turner, Flasch) read him inside Dominican and Neoplatonic theology, and Turner treats apophatic language as a Christian discipline, not a generic mystical experience [M]. Chittick holds that "unity of being" is mostly not Ibn Arabi's own phrase [M]. On not-self, Rahula reads the Buddha as denying any self, the Rhys Davids and Coomaraswamy as denying only the small one, and Thanissaro calls it a strategy for letting go and not a metaphysical claim [M]. Critical Buddhism (Hakamaya, Matsumoto, 1980s) calls Buddha-nature thought a substance-monism that is not Buddhist, which is the specialist objection that interdependence is not a One [M]. Hindu schools read the Upanishads three ways (Advaita, qualified non-dual, dual), so even tat tvam asi is disputed [M]. King, Hanegraaff and Ferrer call the perennial frame Western and monistic, and Ferrer proposes a plural, participatory view; Sharf calls meditative "experience" talk modern rhetoric [M]. Weber's typology (inner-worldly ascetic against world-fleeing mystic) places Alex's work-with-stillness path as the inner-worldly kind [M]. The word "ego" is modern and psychological, and Paul's old man, the Pali self-view and the Sufi nafs (appetite, and also a self at peace) are not the same thing.
