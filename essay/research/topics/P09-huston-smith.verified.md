## 1. The picture in brief

Huston Smith (1919 to 2016) did two jobs that the perennial philosophy movement usually splits between different people. In The World's Religions (first The Religions of Man, 1958) he is the fair, warm guide who presents each tradition from the inside, mostly in its public and historical face, and leaves the comparing to the reader. In Forgotten Truth (1976) he is the advocate. He lays out one shared map, which he calls the primordial tradition: four levels of reality (terrestrial, intermediate, celestial, infinite), four levels of selfhood (body, mind, soul, spirit), and a theory of knowing in which reason is joined by a higher faculty, the Intellect. The map is Frithjof Schuon's, simplified and made readable, and Smith says so. In Beyond the Post-Modern Mind (1982) and Why Religion Matters (2001) he argues that the modern picture keeps only the lowest level and mistakes it for the whole.

Three reasons he is useful to Alex. First, his central idea sits close to Alex's first clause: at its depth the Self of each person is the Infinite, and the Infinite, being unlimited, has to include the finite, so the world of many is the Infinite's own limiting of itself (maya). That is a philosopher's answer to the question the essay says it cannot prove, why a whole would separate. Second, Smith practised inside Hindu, Zen, Sufi and Christian forms and still said the traditions differ. Third, his two books show the split Alex has been told to respect: the public face of a tradition in one book, the inner face in the other.

Cautions. His levels are levels of worlds, not stages of growth, so they do not map onto the arrow from minus four to plus four. He leans on Schuon, and Schuon's circle carries baggage. Reincarnation is the weak point: I could not recall what Smith himself held. The scope note says Forgotten Truth was "later retitled The World's Religions in part"; I believe that merges two different books (finding 1). [Verification pass: I agree. They are two different books with different jobs; only Forgotten Truth got a new subtitle on reissue.]

3. The four levels of reality, nested.
Claim: The core of Forgotten Truth is a ladder of worlds, each wider than the one below. Terrestrial: the physical world, what the senses and physics describe. Intermediate: the psychic or subtle realm, dreams and imagination, and in many traditions the place of spirits and of the soul after death. Celestial: the gods and God as a Person, Lord and Creator, with the heavens. Infinite: the Godhead beyond all form and distinction. Each level includes the one beneath it, so Smith draws the cosmos as concentric circles with the terrestrial in the middle and the Infinite as the circle that contains all. Higher means more real, in the sense of more inclusive and less limited, which is different from "better". In his account the modern mind kept the smallest circle and decided the others do not exist.
Who: Smith 1976, and Beyond the Post-Modern Mind for the diagnosis of modernity. The phrase "Great Chain of Being" reached modern readers through Arthur Lovejoy's 1936 book; Ken Wilber later called the same idea the Great Nest of Being because the higher levels enclose the lower.
Verification pass: Lovejoy popularised the phrase and the history of the idea; he did not coin it (Pope's Essay on Man, 1733, already has "vast chain of being"). [M].
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: the Infinite is Brahman without attributes (nirguna), Eckhart's Godhead (Gottheit), the Tao that cannot be named, Ein Sof, and in Mahayana the dharmakaya or emptiness. The celestial is Ishvara, Allah as Lord, YHWH, the Father. These equivalents are standard in the movement; whether Smith lists exactly these is [U].
Tier: [M] for the four names, order and nesting; [U] for his intermediate level and the diagram's form.
Use for the essay: a second map beside the arrow, labelled as worlds and not stages. The nesting has the same geometry as Part one's small square inside the big map, an honest parallel Alex can draw.

4. The four levels of selfhood and the Self.
Claim: A person has the same four levels as the cosmos, so a human being is a microcosm: body, mind, soul, spirit, matching terrestrial, intermediate, celestial and infinite. Spirit is the Self, in Vedanta the Atman, and the central claim of the Hindu inner teaching is that the Atman is Brahman, so that at its depth the Self of each person is not private. The ego is the identification with the lower levels, the part mistaken for the whole. P07 reports that Smith maps the Vedanta states of consciousness (waking, dreaming, deep sleep and the fourth, turiya) onto this scheme; I recall the same, but not which state goes with which level.
Who: Smith 1976; the states are from the Mandukya Upanishad; "that you are" is Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7, which Alex already cites.
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: Vedanta; Eckhart (the ground of the soul); Sufism (the saying "he who knows himself knows his Lord", not accepted by hadith scholars as the Prophet's); Mahayana Buddha-nature, which specialists keep apart from the Atman; Luke 17:21 (the Greek can mean "within you" or "among you", so this verse cannot carry the "Self within" reading alone [M]).
Tier: [M] for the four-level scheme and the Atman claim; [U] for state-to-level mapping and his list of parallels.
Use for the essay: the first clause of the axiom in the movement's words. It also suggests an answer to "who carries over, if there is one mind?": continuity belongs to the soul level, and the spirit level is never born and never dies. That is my inference, not Smith's words.

5. Knowing: reason and the Intellect.
Claim: Smith follows Schuon and Guenon in saying the human being has a faculty above reason. Reason works with concepts at a distance from its object. The Intellect (nous, buddhi, the "eye of the heart") knows by identity, so to know the Infinite is in some measure to be it. On this account the traditions agree because they report from the same faculty about the same object, not because they share feelings.
Who: Smith 1976, the chapter on knowing; locator unknown.
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: Plato and Plotinus (nous); Vedanta (jnana, buddhi); Sufism (the heart, ma'rifa); Orthodoxy (nous in the heart). Standard in the movement, not a list from Smith.
Tier: [M] for the claim; [U] for his wording and parallels.
Use for the essay: it explains why the movement thinks the comparison is more than matching emotions; the experience is, on this view, a way of knowing. Scholars dispute that (section 5).

6. Why the Infinite includes the finite: maya and the veil.
Claim: Smith, following Schuon and Guenon's Universal Possibility, argues that a truly Infinite reality has nothing outside it to limit it, so it must include the possibility of limitation, and so of the finite world. Maya is that possibility seen from the side of the many, and the world is less real than the Infinite, not unreal. This is an argument from the logic of infinity and not from a text. P07 reports the same argument in Smith and doubts the wording.
Who: Smith 1976, the chapters on the Infinite; Schuon; Guenon.
The words: paraphrase only; wording [U].
Traditions: Hindu lila and maya (Brahma Sutra 2.1.33); the Kabbalists' tzimtzum; the Sufi "hidden treasure" saying; Plotinus on the overflow of the One. All three of the first are already in the essay.
Verification pass, added [M]: tzimtzum is the 16th century Lurianic doctrine (Safed), not an ancient one, and whether it means a literal withdrawal or a figure of speech is disputed inside Kabbalah. The "hidden treasure" saying is a hadith qudsi that hadith scholars do not accept as authentic; Sufis, Ibn Arabi above all, hold it true by spiritual insight. Say "the Sufi tradition says" and not "the Prophet said".
Tier: [M] that Smith presents the argument; [U] for how and where.
Use for the essay: the essay offers "knowing needs a gap" as Alex's own reading. Smith's argument is another road to the same place: an infinite that could not be limited would be limited by that very fact. One sentence, credited to the movement, labelled an argument and not a proof.

7. How Smith explains why the religions differ.
Claim: As I recall, he gives several reasons (the colleague counted four). First, the levels: a quarrel between a theistic and a non-theistic tradition is often a quarrel between two levels, since the Person-God of Islam or Christianity is celestial and the impersonal Brahman or emptiness is infinite. Second, Schuon's split of outer form from inner content: the traditions differ most in rites, stories and dogma and agree most at the centre. Third, temperament and task: each tradition is a way suited to a people, a time and a type of person, as the four yogas of Hinduism are four paths for four temperaments. Fourth, each tradition has its own genius, and he insists the differences are real and valuable and should not be flattened.
Who: Smith 1976 and 1991; locators unknown.
The words: paraphrase.
Tier (re-tiered in verification): [M] for the first two (the levels reading and the outer/inner split are Schuon's standard apparatus and Smith uses both); [U] for the third and fourth as Smith's own reasons; the number "four" is the colleague's synthesis and must not be quoted as Smith's list.
Use for the essay: some apparent disagreements are about which level is meant, which answers "Buddhism has no God while Islam has one".

8. The World's Religions: his method as a guide, and his two books as two faces.
Claim: Each chapter presents a tradition on its own terms and in its best light, as a believer would recognise it, organised around what that tradition stresses. It is humane and narrative, not an academic survey: Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, Islam, Judaism and Christianity, with a framing chapter and, in 1991, primal religions. He compares mostly by implication and the reader does the matching. The inner, metaphysical face comes in Forgotten Truth, where mystics and metaphysicians from several traditions stand side by side. A reader who has only the first book may think "they say the same thing" is false, and a reader who has only the second may think the traditions are interchangeable. Smith meant them to be read together.
Who: Smith 1958, 1991, 1976.
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for the structure and the division of labour; [U] for chapter order, the framing chapter's title, whether he adds a comparing conclusion, and the figures in Forgotten Truth.
Use for the essay: one sentence telling readers why "they say the same thing" and "the traditions look very different" do not contradict each other.

9. The Hindu chapter: goals, paths, stages, Atman and Brahman, karma.
Claim: Smith organises Hinduism through fours. Four goals: pleasure, worldly success, duty, liberation. Four paths for four temperaments: knowledge, devotion, selfless action (karma yoga) and meditation (raja yoga). Four stages of life: student, householder, forest dweller, renouncer. Behind them stands the Atman as Brahman, Brahman as being, awareness and bliss (sat, chit, ananda), the world as maya and lila, and karma as the moral law of cause and effect that binds a soul to repeated births until liberation (moksha) ends it.
Who: Smith 1958, 1991, the Hinduism chapter.
The words: paraphrase.
Traditions: Hindu; Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (acting without clinging to the fruit); Gita 2.13 (the embodied self passes through childhood, youth and old age and then takes another body) and Gita 2.22 (the self changes bodies as a person changes worn-out clothes). Tier: [M] for the fours and the doctrine summary; [U] for his exact treatment of karma and rebirth. (I recall that the section on the goals is headed "What Do Men Want?" and that the paths are presented as ways of love, knowledge, work and psychophysical exercises, which would make the fours his and not a later gloss. Use for the essay: karma yoga, work done without clinging to the result, bridges to Part one's DO and BE. The link is mine, not Smith's.

10. The Buddhist chapter: rebirth without a soul.
Claim: Smith presents the Buddha as the man who woke up, the Four Noble Truths, and no self (anatta), and faces the question of what is reborn if there is no soul. The tradition's answer is a continuing stream in which character and momentum carry on without a fixed core, and karma is action driven by intention. I recall, without certainty, the parable of the man shot by an arrow who refuses treatment until he knows who shot him (Majjhima Nikaya 63, the Culamalunkya Sutta), used for the Buddha's reason for setting speculation aside.
Who: Smith 1958, 1991. The lamp image is in the Milindapanha, which Alex already cites.
The words: paraphrase.
Tier: [M] for the truths and no self as chapter content; [U] for the lamp and the arrow parable being in Smith's text.
Use for the essay: the cleanest case of the same thing in other words: the self is dropped and something still continues. Do not say Smith equates emptiness with Brahman; as I recall he keeps them apart in the chapters and joins them only in the levels.

11. Rebirth and resurrection side by side.

My guess is the second, because the movement around him (Guenon, Schuon, Perry) rejected the Theosophical form of reincarnation.

14. Smith's own practice: living inside several traditions.
Claim: Brought up Methodist in China, Smith studied Vedanta in St. Louis (introduced by Gerald Heard, taught by the Vedanta Society's swami) and practised for years. He also joined the Harvard psilocybin work of 1961 and wrote "Do Drugs Have Religious Import?" He returned to it in Cleansing the Doors of Perception (2000).
Who: Tales of Wonder (2009), interviews, the 1964 paper. Locators unknown.

## 4. How Smith compares: the method, concretely

Build a frame first. In Forgotten Truth the frame is the four levels of reality, the four of selfhood and the Intellect. The traditions are then placed in it: Brahman without attributes, the Godhead, the Tao, Ein Sof and the dharmakaya at the Infinite; Ishvara, Allah, YHWH and the Father at the celestial. The equation is made at the placing, before the reader sees a quotation, and the reader should know that.

Read a disagreement as two levels. The standard case is theism against non-theism: a personal Lord and an impersonal ground are, in Smith's reading, two levels of one reality. This turns many hard cases into matches, and it means a text that disagrees can always be assigned to another level, which is why critics call the method hard to test.

Split outer from inner. He takes the exoteric and esoteric distinction from Schuon. Rites, stories and dogmas diverge and the inner teaching converges. When two traditions look alike only in their mystics, the movement counts that as support.

Describe from inside. In The World's Religions each chapter is written as a believer would recognise it, with no ranking and no core imposed in the text. The comparison happens when the reader sees the same concerns recur: the self and its limits, a way to put them down, a reality that exceeds words.

Argue against modernity as much as between traditions. Much of Forgotten Truth contrasts the traditional worldview, which premodern civilizations shared in outline, with the modern one, which dropped the upper levels. The traditions are grouped as people who agree about what modernity forgot. That is nearer to what Smith does than the summary "all religions are the same".

Argue for the top-level claim. That the Infinite must include the finite is argued and not quoted, which is unusual: most anthologies cite and leave the claim to the reader.

Test by living it. He practised Vedanta, Zen and Sufi prayer, stayed a Christian, and held that you cannot know a tradition without trying it.

## 5. What scholars say

Admirers say Smith gave the clearest English account of the Traditionalist map and of each single tradition for general readers. The World's Religions was, by many reports, among the best-selling books of its kind [U for any figure; do not cite a sales number].

Critics make five points.

Circularity. The grid decides what counts as inner, so a text that disagrees is called outer, and the thesis cannot fail. P04 and P07 report the same objection.

Bias in the grid. The levels and the Intellect are Vedanta and Sufi theory laid over the rest, and traditions that do not share the frame (Theravada, Confucianism, everyday Jewish and Christian practice) are read through it. Specialists in Buddhism keep emptiness and Brahman apart, and Smith's levels put both at the Infinite. Say that the comparer equates them.

Experience. Steven Katz (1978) argued that mystical experience is shaped by the tradition that has it, so agreement across traditions is smaller than it looks. Robert Forman (1990) replied that some experiences have no content to be shaped. The movement answers that its unity lies in the Intellect and its object and not in the experience, which critics find hard to test (P05 and P07 report this debate). [Verification pass: Katz's target was the "common core" thesis of the academic perennialists, W. T. Stace above all (Mysticism and Philosophy, 1960); see 6A. [M]]

History. Mark Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, 2004) treats Traditionalism as a twentieth century esoteric current opposed to modernity and not as a neutral survey. Richard King and Wouter Hanegraaff argue, differently, that the Western picture of one mystical East was partly built by anthologists and comparers.

Science. Critics of Beyond the Post-Modern Mind say its scientism is a partial portrait of what scientists do [U].

Stephen Prothero's God Is Not One (2010) is the best known popular argument that the religions ask different questions and offer different cures; I cannot confirm he names Smith. Jonathan Z. Smith argued that comparison is made by the comparer, who chooses what to match. J. Z. Smith and Wilfred Cantwell Smith are different people from Huston Smith and easily confused with him.

Honest summary for Alex: Smith is a careful, attractive voice for a minority reading and not a neutral referee. His textbook is more even-handed than his advocacy.

## 6A. Added in verification: the movement is wider than Huxley (and than Smith)

Why this is here: Alex's note in this run says that there are plenty of other books in the perennial philosophy movement besides what Huxley speaks about, and that Huxley is not even the best. Whether any author is "the best" is Alex's judgement and I do not rate it. What I can offer is a map of the other main works and what each is good for. Everything below is [M] from memory, unsearched; titles and years are the part to check first at re-run. Nothing here is a quotation.

The houses of the movement (so the essay does not call them one thing):
- The older line: Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540); Ficino and Pico; Leibniz, who gave the phrase its later currency. Huxley says in The Perennial Philosophy that Leibniz coined "philosophia perennis"; scholars point to Steuco as earlier. [M] Do not repeat "coined by Leibniz" as fact.
- Theosophy: Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (1877), The Secret Doctrine (1888). Covered by sibling slices.
- The Traditionalist or Perennialist school: Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon, and after them Burckhardt, Lings, Pallis, Nasr, Perry, Smith. Hostile to Theosophy (finding 11).
- The popular and anthology line: Huxley (1945).
- The academic "common core" line: Stace, and the critics Katz and Forman (section 5).
- The developmental or transpersonal line: Wilber.

Works, with what each offers Alex (all titles and years [M], to be checked):
- Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945). An anthology of passages from many traditions with Huxley's commentary, arranged by theme and not by tradition. The easiest door for a general reader, and probably why many people think the movement is one book. Its chapter "That Art Thou" is the quickest path to the first clause of the axiom. [M for the chapter name; the rest of the contents list is not mine to recite]
- Frithjof Schuon, De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948); English The Transcendent Unity of Religions (1953). The systematic statement: the outer forms differ and the inner centre agrees. This is the source of Smith's map and the best single book for "the same thing in different words" as an argument and not an anthology.
- Rene Guenon, Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921); L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta (1925; English Man and His Becoming According to the Vedanta); La Crise du monde moderne (1927; The Crisis of the Modern World); Le Regne de la quantite et les signes des temps (1945; The Reign of Quantity). The founder of the school. Also, against Theosophy: Le Theosophisme (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923).
- Ananda K. Coomaraswamy, Hinduism and Buddhism (1943); Time and Eternity (1947); Selected Papers, ed. Roger Lipsey (1977). A scholar who compares Indian and medieval Christian texts line by line; the best source for checked parallels of the "one Self, many appearances" kind (Eckhart with the Upanishads).
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Knowledge and the Sacred (1981, from his Edinburgh Gifford Lectures). The Traditionalist case against the modern worldview, close to Smith's Beyond the Post-Modern Mind.
- Titus Burckhardt, An Introduction to Sufi Doctrine; Martin Lings, What Is Sufism? (1975) and A Sufi Saint of the Twentieth Century (1961); Marco Pallis, A Buddhist Spectrum (1980). The Sufi and Buddhist voices of the same school, useful where Alex wants the inner layer of Islam and of Buddhism said by someone who stood inside it.
- Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971). A very large Traditionalist anthology, the school's own counterpart to Huxley.
- Walter T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960). The academic form of the claim that mystics agree (a "common core" of experience); the target of Katz's 1978 attack. Use if Alex wants the claim in a scholar's dress, and mention that it is contested.
- Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (English 1932; German 1926), which sets Eckhart beside Shankara. Early comparers who were not part of any of the schools above.
- Ken Wilber, The Atman Project (1980); Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995). Takes the same hierarchy and adds stages of development. The nearest relative to Alex's arrow from minus four to plus four; Smith's levels are worlds, Wilber's are stages. Wilber is a popular synthesiser and is criticised by specialists, so cite him as a map and not as a witness.
- Harry Oldmeadow, Traditionalism: Religion in the Light of the Perennial Philosophy (2000). A sympathetic guide to the Traditionalist school; Mark Sedgwick's Against the Modern World (2004) is the critical one.

Where each helps the axiom: one whole appearing as many: Schuon, Coomaraswamy, Smith. The veil and why it feels real: Smith, Schuon, Huxley. Stages and an arrow of growth: Wilber, and the traditions' own ladders. Lifetimes and karma: not the Traditionalists (they reject the Theosophical form); use the Gita, the Milindapanha, Plato and Theosophy's own books. The honest objections: Katz, Forman, Sedgwick, King, Hanegraaff, Prothero (section 5).

Caution to carry: the Schuon circle has had serious allegations against it, reported in P04; and "the perennial philosophy" is a family of related claims with real disagreements inside it, not one doctrine.
