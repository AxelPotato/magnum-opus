## 1. The picture in brief

Theosophy's primary texts give Alex the nearest ready-made statement of his axiom, and also some useful differences. The Secret Doctrine opens with three propositions: one boundless, immutable Principle; the periodic appearing and disappearing of universes; and the fundamental identity of all souls with the Universal Over-Soul, together with an obligatory pilgrimage of every soul through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law. Blavatsky's gloss says the spark wins individuality only by its own effort, with no privileges. That is "one whole, experiencing separation, returning", in her vocabulary.

Her karma is impersonal. It is a law of harmony, and "we reward or punish ourselves". The Glossary says karma neither punishes nor rewards, and the Mahatma Letters say the ultimate is "law", not a God. The same SD passage on karma, as I remember it, says its ways would not be inscrutable if people worked in union rather than strife, which is close to Alex's fingers.

The Key to Theosophy supplies the lives machinery: personality as a mask over a lasting individuality, devachan as a rest between lives, and a reply to "why do we not remember". The Voice of the Silence and Light on the Path supply the ethics of separateness ("Kill out all sense of separateness"; compassion that refuses private liberation).

Four cautions. Isis Unveiled (1877) rejected general reincarnation and the doctrine developed later. Her borrowing from secondary sources was heavy. Her own texts say they contain "blinds". A board of "Lords of Karma" is mostly a later popular layer [check pass, change 20: she already has the Lipika as recorders of karma, so the difference is one of emphasis and personification]. Also, in Theosophy the whole cycle repeats, and the return gives a conscious individual, not a dissolved one.

## 4. How they compare

Genealogy, not only resemblance. Blavatsky's weight falls on descent. She says the traditions descend from one archaic "Wisdom-Religion", and the Key traces the name Theosophy to Ammonius Saccas (F12). She also treats the one Principle as what they all describe, but the proof she offers is descent and the Masters. The differences are then explained as later damage: priestly corruption, mistranslation, and deliberate concealment ("blinds", F26). [Check pass, change 22: the colleague wrote that she "does not say that traditions converge because they describe the same reality". That is too absolute.]

Term-by-term equations. The Glossary and Voice match a term from one tradition to one from another: devachan with the Land of Bliss, alaya with the universal soul, the Over-Soul with Emerson's usage. Her exposition adds viraga to the paramitas. These are the places where specialists object.

Symbol reading. In Part II of the SD she reads egg, lotus, tree, serpent and triangle symbols across cultures as cipher for one cosmogony, and says symbols have several "keys" (number U).

Living witness. Her Masters stand as the claimed source, so the comparison rests on testimony as well as texts.

Eclectic quoting. Her Preface calls the book a nosegay (F25), and Coleman shows the flowers came from secondary books.

A contrast for Alex [rewritten in the check pass, change 22]. Blavatsky's explanation of agreement is descent from one hidden source, with Masters as the authority. The Traditionalist perennialists (Guenon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) also argue from one central or primordial tradition, kept in regular lineages, and from metaphysical principle; Guenon wrote a book-length attack on Theosophy (Le Theosophisme, 1921) while sharing the idea that one central tradition lies behind the religions. They are not Theosophists and must not be credited with Theosophical views, nor she with theirs. The argument from the convergence of mystical reports belongs to a different group: William James (1902), Evelyn Underhill (1911), W. T. Stace (1960) and, in part, Huxley (1945). Alex leans on neither team's explanation. He says the likeness follows from an axiom and invites the reader to check the examples. He can say that the explanations offered by earlier comparers (a common revelation, a common transmission, a common experience) all have their critics, and that he needs none of them.

## 5. What scholars say

Scholars treat Blavatsky as a major figure of modern Western esotericism with mixed credibility (Hanegraaff, New Age Religion and Western Culture, 1996; Godwin, The Theosophical Enlightenment, 1994; Washington, Madame Blavatsky's Baboon, 1993 [two subtitles, U]; Lachman, 2012). The Hodgson Report (1885; SPR Proceedings vol. 3) judged the Mahatma letters fraudulent. Harrison (Journal of the SPR 53, 1986, about pp. 286 to 310) disputed its method, and the SPR did not retract it [M; the repo notes record the Harrison reference from an earlier search]. K. Paul Johnson (The Masters Revealed, 1994) has proposed real historical persons behind the Masters; he argues that the Theosophical picture was constructed, which is not a defence of the Letters' own claims. Campbell (1980) is the standard history and not an argument for authenticity; defenders of the Letters are mostly Theosophical authors (T09). Chajes (2019) gives the best account of how her reincarnation teaching changed. Coleman (1895) documented her borrowing. Max Muller objected that the Buddha taught nothing esoteric (1893). The standard Buddhist reply to any "esoteric Buddhism" is the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16, chapter 2; 2.25 in Walshe's numbering as I recall [U]), where the Buddha tells Ananda he has taught without "the closed fist of a teacher" [M]; the line is the general Buddhological reply and is not claimed to be Muller's. Tantric and Mahayana traditions do have restricted teachings, so the reply holds for the Pali canon's own claim, not for every school. Her root-race scheme (SD Vol. II) is widely criticised as a racial hierarchy, though she also preached universal brotherhood. Specialists in Buddhism and Advaita reject several of her equations (alaya, maya as illusion, karma and the nidanas). I judge that she is best used as a witness to the idea of unity in nineteenth-century English, and not as an authority on any tradition.

## 9. Perennialist books beyond Huxley (added in the check pass on the user's remark; every item [M] or [U], none searched, no ranking)

Why this section exists. The user's remark, relayed with this task: there are plenty of other books in the perennial philosophy movement beyond what Huxley speaks about, and he is not even the best. The essay's plan treats "the perennial philosophy and Theosophy" as the two movements that compared religions systematically. The first should not be reduced to one 1945 anthology. "Best" is a judgement. What the sibling files and my recall support as fact is that Huxley's book is an anthology with commentary, the best known under the name; that the Traditionalist school began with Guenon in 1921, before it; that Schuon's first book (1948, English 1953) came after it; and that a larger, stricter anthology (Perry, 1971) exists. So below is "best for what", not a ranking. Titles and years are from memory (and from the sibling files, also memory); a library record should be checked before any appears in the essay.

9.1 The name and the idea.
- Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), the first use of the phrase [M; sibling record [C], P01]. Leibniz used it in a letter to Nicolas Remond of 26 August 1714 [M; sibling record [C], P01; the repo notes agree]. Huxley's Foreword credits Leibniz with coining it, which is Huxley's own error and not a fact to repeat [M]. Charles B. Schmitt, "Perennial Philosophy: From Agostino Steuco to Leibniz", Journal of the History of Ideas 27 (1966) is the standard scholarly history of the term [M on the paper, U on the year]. The older form of the claim is the Renaissance "prisca theologia" (Ficino, Pico) [M].

9.2 The Traditionalist (Perennialist) school, in the strict twentieth century sense. Not Theosophists, and hostile to Theosophy. For Alex's method, these are the authors who argue the claim rather than illustrate it.
- Rene Guenon (1886 to 1951): Introduction generale a l'etude des doctrines hindoues (1921); Le Theosophisme, histoire d'une pseudo-religion (1921); L'Erreur spirite (1923); L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vedanta (1925); La Crise du monde moderne (1927); Les Etats multiples de l'etre (1932). Good for: the claim of one primordial Tradition behind the regular religions, the outer and inner (exoteric and esoteric) distinction, and the metaphysics of the Infinite and Universal Possibility, which is the sharpest philosophical form of Alex's axiom found in this research (sibling P02). Trap: Guenon rejected reincarnation in the Western or Theosophical sense and wrote against the Theosophy Alex also uses. He is a witness for caution on lives and karma, not for them.
- Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947): The Transformation of Nature in Art (1934); Hinduism and Buddhism (1943); Time and Eternity (1947); "On the One and Only Transmigrant" (Journal of the American Oriental Society, Supplement 3, 1944) [U on the year and binding]. Good for: scholarship from the Sanskrit, Pali, Greek and Latin texts. The one-transmigrant essay is the closest Traditionalist text to Alex's one whole going through many lives. His Pali readings (no-self as denying the empirical self and not the Self) are disputed by Pali specialists.
- Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998): De l'unite transcendante des religions (1948; English as The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953; a revised edition of 1975 with an introduction by Huston Smith [U on the years]). Good for: the exoteric and esoteric rule, which is the strongest worked version of "the religions differ in public form and agree at the inner level". It is the nearest thing to Alex's own rule for Part two. It also sets the limit: Schuon holds that the unity lies above the forms, not in their overlap, and does not say the outer forms are equal. He preferred "sophia perennis" to "philosophia perennis" [M, from P02 and P08]. Note: his first book followed Huxley's, though he wrote in the school's journal from the 1930s [M].
- Titus Burckhardt (Introduction to Sufi Doctrine, English 1959 [U]), Martin Lings (The Book of Certainty, 1952; What Is Sufism?, 1975 [U]), Marco Pallis (A Buddhist Spectrum, 1980 [U]), Leo Schaya (The Universal Meaning of the Kabbalah, French 1958, English 1971 [U]), Seyyed Hossein Nasr (Knowledge and the Sacred, Gifford Lectures 1981). Good for: the same metaphysic read in Sufi, Buddhist and Kabbalistic vocabulary, which is what Alex's "the traditions say it in other words" claim needs.
- Whitall N. Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971 [M]): a very large thematic anthology from the Schuon circle, sorted from the Principle through manifestation to the return. Good for: the strictest quotation source inside the movement, and a rival in kind to Huxley's anthology, larger and with source notes. Trap: Perry compiled it; Huston Smith wrote a Foreword to a later reissue.
- Huston Smith: The Religions of Man (1958; later The World's Religions, 1991); Forgotten Truth (1976, subtitle The Primordial Tradition; reissued 1992 with the subtitle The Common Vision of the World's Religions [M]). Good for: the most readable map of the whole (levels of reality and of the self), taken largely from Schuon, and an argument that an Infinite which could not express itself as finite would not be infinite. That is a philosophical answer to why a whole would experience separation.
- Histories of the school: Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (OUP, 2004); Harry Oldmeadow, Journeys East (2004) and Frithjof Schuon and the Perennial Philosophy (2010) [M].

9.3 Other comparers (not Traditionalists).
- William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902); Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, West-oestliche Mystik (1926; English Mysticism East and West, 1932), which sets Eckhart beside Shankara; W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy and The Teachings of the Mystics (both 1960), the best philosophical statement of the common-core thesis; Alan Watts, The Supreme Identity (1950); Reza Shah-Kazemi, Paths to Transcendence (2006), Shankara, Ibn Arabi and Eckhart from the Traditionalist side; Ken Wilber (The Atman Project, 1980; Spectrum of Consciousness, 1977), the developmental or stage version, which is closest to Alex's arrow and is contested.
- Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945): an anthology of mystics and saints under themes, with his commentary, the readable entry for English readers. It is one voice, it works from translations, it is not Traditionalist and it is not Theosophical. One of his ideas does real work for Alex: that knowledge is a function of the being of the knower (Foreword). His reducing-valve picture is in The Doors of Perception (1954), not in the anthology.

9.4 Critics, so that the essay can say where a specialist objects.
- R. C. Zaehner, Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957), who denies that all mystical experience is one and takes Huxley as his target; Steven T. Katz (ed.), Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978), the constructivist argument that mystical experience is shaped by the tradition; Robert Forman (ed.), The Problem of Pure Consciousness (1990), a reply; John Hick, An Interpretation of Religion (1989), who accepts mediation and holds one Real known in different responses; Richard King, Orientalism and Religion (1999); Stephen Prothero, God Is Not One (2010) [all M].

9.5 What this means for G1-2.
- The two movements Alex names disagree. The Traditionalist founders called Theosophy a counterfeit and rejected Theosophical reincarnation and karma, while sharing the idea of one central tradition. Say so in one clause. Do not say "all perennialists attacked Theosophy": Huxley did not (he was friendly with Krishnamurti) [M, T08].
- Attribute each view to the author who held it: Guenon's attack on Theosophy; Schuon's exoteric and esoteric rule; Coomaraswamy's one transmigrant; James and Stace on convergent reports. Do not credit a Traditionalist with Blavatsky's descent claim, or Blavatsky with their metaphysics. Do not merge vocabularies: Masters, root races and the Wisdom-Religion are Theosophical; exoteric and esoteric, and the Primordial Tradition, are Traditionalist.
- Line 53 of essay/part-2-spiritual.md says Aldous Huxley "made it famous in 1945". That is defensible as a claim about reach. What it lacks is the other books. A fair version names Guenon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon as the school that made the comparison a project, Smith as the readable map, Perry as the large anthology, and Huxley as the 1945 door for English readers. Line 55 begins "The other group is the Theosophists", which is right; add one clause that the two groups did not like each other.
- Quotation traps in this area [M]: "Huxley coined the phrase" is false; "Leibniz coined it" is Huxley's own error; "The Doors of Perception" is Blake's phrase, and the line "there are things known and things unknown, and in between are the doors" circulates credited to Huxley and is a Morrison line; "He who knows one religion knows none" is Max Muller's (echoing Goethe on languages); "Truth is one, the sages call it by many names" is the Rigveda (1.164.46) and is often credited to Gandhi or Vivekananda, who quoted it. Oneness quotations credited to "Blavatsky", "Huxley", "Einstein", "Rumi" or "Eckhart" on quote sites are often misattributed: quote only from a printing that has been opened.
