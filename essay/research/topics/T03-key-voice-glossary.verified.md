## 1. The picture in brief

The Key to Theosophy is Blavatsky's plain handbook, written in London in 1889 as a companion to the dense Secret Doctrine (1888). It is a dialogue between an Enquirer and a Theosophist, covering the one Reality, the self, death, rebirth, karma and brotherhood. The Voice of the Silence, the same year, is a short devotional text in three Fragments, said to be translated from a Book of the Golden Precepts, on the inner path, the illusion the mind builds, compassion, and the final choice between private liberation and staying to help. The Theosophical Glossary (1892, posthumous, edited by G. R. S. Mead) is her cross-religion dictionary: Sanskrit, Tibetan, Hebrew and Greek terms, each glossed in English and placed in the system.

How she looks at the subject. One boundless principle stands behind everything, with no personal face. Individual souls are rays of it that make a long pilgrimage through matter, through many lives, under a law of harmony called karma, and return to it. The separate self is the passing personality, a mask, and the illusion is mistaking the mask for the whole. Brotherhood is a fact of nature, so ethics follows from unity.

How she compares. She claims a single archaic Wisdom-Religion as the source of all religions, explains difference as corruption, literalism and symbol, and shows agreement by equating terms and tabulating systems. The useful contrast for Alex: she explains agreement by a lost original and hidden teachers, he explains it by logic from an axiom. Her authority, the Masters, is the weak point. Her material is rich, quotable and public domain.

2. The meaning of the name, "not a religion", and the "substratum".
Claim: She derives Theosophy from Greek theosophia, divine wisdom, meaning the wisdom the gods have, not "wisdom of God" in the monotheist sense. She ties it to the Alexandrian school of Ammonius Saccas (the "Eclectic Theosophical School", the Philalethians [spelling U: possibly "Philaletheians"], lovers of truth), whose aim she gives as reconciling all religions and nations under a common ethics, following church historians such as Mosheim. In the Key Theosophy is not a religion and the Society is not a sect. In the Glossary entry "Theosophy" (recalled, not for quotation) she calls it the substratum and basis of all the world religions and philosophies, taught by a few elect since man became a thinking being, and in practice divine ethics. Every religion has an outer form for the many and an inner meaning for those who look.
Who: The Key, first two sections; Glossary "Theosophy"; Isis Unveiled (1877) for the Ammonius material.
Traditions cited: Alexandrian Neoplatonism, early Christianity, "all sects".
Tier: [M] for the gist and the derivation; [U] for exact wording, Mosheim as source, the spelling of "Philaletheians", and which book carries "substratum".
Scholars: Ammonius left no writings; "Eclectic Theosophical School" is an eighteenth century historians' label (second recall agrees, [U]; Brucker and Mosheim are the names I associate with it).
Use: Her "not a religion" is Alex's move in paragraph two. Her "substratum" is the thing to avoid: it is a historical claim, and he wants a logical one.

3. The one Reality, and God.
Claim: She rejects a personal, limited God and affirms a universal divine Principle, the root of all, from which all proceeds and within which all is absorbed at the end of the great cycle of being (recalled, wording [U]). It is unknowable and without attributes; her Eastern name for it is Parabrahm. What people call God within is the Higher Self, a ray of it. Prayer as begging is rejected, prayer as inner communion accepted, and she reads the Gospel line about praying in the closet to the Father in secret as pointing to the inner God.
Who: The Key, the section on the fundamental teachings; the first Proem proposition of the Secret Doctrine (1888) is the same one principle.
Traditions cited: Vedanta (Parabrahm), Christianity (Matthew 6:6), Kabbalah (Ain Soph, mostly elsewhere).
Tier: [M] for content; [U] for the exact sentence and Matthew 6:6.
Use: The "all proceeds from it and is absorbed in it" sentence holds clauses one and three of Alex's axiom. Verify and quote it.

4. Illusion (Maya) and the personality as a mask.
Claim: Glossary "Maya" (recalled near-verbatim): illusion; the cosmic power that makes phenomenal existence and perception possible; in Hindu philosophy only what is changeless and eternal is real, and everything that changes, and so has a beginning and an end, is called Maya. In the Key she treats the personality (Latin persona, a mask) as the passing self and the individuality as what persists.
Who: Glossary "Maya"; the Key, the sections on the principles and on reincarnation.
Traditions cited: Hinduism (Maya), Buddhism (impermanence), Latin (persona).
Tier: [M] for the Maya gist (second recall agrees, not verification); [U] for the persona etymology appearing in the Key.
Scholars: Her definition makes "illusion" mean "impermanent", closer to the Advaita mithya than to "unreal".
Use: A bridge for Alex: the wall is real inside the system, and it is not final.

5. The origin of separation, and the pilgrimage.
Claim: The Monad (Atma joined to Buddhi) is a ray of the Universal Soul. It descends into matter, individuates through a long chain of personalities, a pilgrimage, and returns. As I recall her argument, unconscious divine purity becomes conscious divinity only through experience. The Secret Doctrine's third Proem proposition (paraphrase): all souls are identical with the universal Over-Soul, and every soul must make an obligatory pilgrimage through the cycle of incarnation under cyclic and karmic law.
Who: The Secret Doctrine (1888), Proem; the Key restates it in plain form in the sections on the principles and on reincarnation [U which].
Traditions cited: Hinduism (the jivatman as part of Brahman); Gnostic descent of the soul [U]; Plato [U].
Tier: [M] for the third proposition and the pilgrimage (second recall agrees); [U] that the "experience makes it self-conscious" argument is in the Key itself.
Use: The nearest classic statement to the axiom. It is received teaching, not derived. Alex derives it, and can say so. The word "Over-Soul" echoes Emerson's essay "The Over-Soul" (1841) [M, added by the fact-checker].

6. The seven principles and the comparison table.
Claim: Man is sevenfold. Her list: sthula sharira (physical body), prana (life), linga sharira (astral double), kama (desire), manas (mind, higher and lower), buddhi (spiritual soul), atma (spirit). She sets this beside other systems: the Vedantic five sheaths (annamaya, pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, anandamaya, with the atman beyond; the source is the Taittiriya Upanishad, Brahmananda Valli [M, added]); Taraka Raja Yoga with three upadhis (sthula, sukshma, karana) plus Atma; and Sinnett's English names in Esoteric Buddhism (1883): body, vitality, astral body, animal soul, human soul, spiritual soul, spirit.
Who: The Key, the section on nature and man; T. Subba Row's articles in The Theosophist (1881 to 1887); the Secret Doctrine.
Traditions cited: Vedanta, Taraka Raja Yoga, Sinnett. A Kabbalistic column (nephesh, ruach, neshamah, chayyah, yechidah) is often matched to the scheme in Theosophical writing, but I cannot place it in the Key.
Tier: [M] for the seven names and the comparison; [U] for the table's place and columns; the Kabbalah column [U].
Scholars: Here the comparer equates what specialists keep apart. Hindu texts have no standard sevenfold scheme, and her buddhi and manas do not match the Sanskrit senses (buddhi is the discriminating intellect, not a "spiritual soul"). Subba Row, the Brahmin scholar in her circle, preferred a smaller Vedantic count, and the two disagreed in print [M gist, U detail].
Use: Traditions count the layers of a person as three, four, five or seven and say one thing: a core exists that is not the passing self. Do not print the seven as Vedanta.

7. Individuality, personality, and the thread through lives.
Claim: The Key separates the individuality, the reincarnating Ego (manas united with buddhi), from the personality, the passing memories, desires and habits of one life. Only the individuality reincarnates. Her circle's image is the sutratman (thread-soul) on which lives are strung like beads.
Who: The Key, the sections on reincarnation and on the thinking principle; Glossary "Sutratman".
Traditions cited: Advaita Vedanta, where sutratman is a term in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (the Antaryamin passage, 3.7) for the thread on which all beings are strung.
Tier: [M] for the split and the thread; [U] for the Upanishad locator and for who brought sutratman in (I believe Subba Row). Second recall agrees on 3.7, where the thread is the wind (vayu), not a soul.
Scholars: In Advaita the thread links all beings at one time, not one being's successive lives. Her use is a reassignment.
Use: Terminology trap: her "Ego" is the lasting self, and her "personality" is what Alex calls ego. If the lives figure uses "the thread", say it is a Theosophical image with a Vedanta word.

8. After death, and why rebirth.
Claim: After death the lower principles are shed in kama-loka (place of desire), leaving "shells" which she says are what mediums meet. The Ego then rests in devachan (Tibetan bde-ba-can, which renders Sanskrit sukhavati, Land of Bliss; she spells it Deva-chan and glosses it as a dwelling of the gods, a Sanskritizing reading of the Tibetan [M for direction, U for the exact Tibetan spellings]), a dreamlike state in which the best of the last life is fulfilled, and returns. I recall the interval as ten to fifteen centuries [U]. Her reasons for rebirth: it explains unequal starts, gives time for the pilgrimage, and is just because karma carries over. We forget because memory belongs to the personality's brain, not to the Ego. She rejects return into animals and the Spiritist (Kardec) return of the personality.
Who: The Key, sections on post-mortem states and reincarnation; Glossary "Devachan". Isis Unveiled (1877) differs: it allowed return only in exceptional cases and opposed Kardec.
Traditions cited: Tibetan and Pure Land Buddhism (sukhavati); Catholic purgatory for kama-loka [U]; Hindu, Buddhist and Pythagorean rebirth; Gospel passages (Elijah and John, being born again) [U which].
Tier: [M] for the structure and arguments; [U] for the interval and the passages.
Use: The Theosophical form of "the soul rests between lives". Her equation of devachan with sukhavati is a comparer's move: a Pure Land is a place to practise toward awakening, not a rest. Also check line 55 of part-2-spiritual.md, which names Isis Unveiled as a route for karma and reincarnation. My memory is that Isis did not teach reincarnation as the rule; the Key, Esoteric Buddhism (1883) and the Secret Doctrine did. Second recall agrees; still unverified.

9. Karma as the law of harmony.
Claim: Karma is the law that adjusts effect to cause on every plane and restores a harmony that action has broken. No judge administers it. We reward and punish ourselves, and each person weaves a destiny thread by thread around them like a spider's web (recalled image). It is not fate. There is collective karma of families, nations and humanity. She calls it the ultimate law of the universe.
Who: The Key, the section on karma; Glossary "Karma".
Traditions cited: Hindu and Buddhist karma; Greek Nemesis ("Karma-Nemesis"); Christian sowing and reaping [U].
Tier: [M] for content and the spider image; [U] for wording and the Galatians reference.
Use: "Harmony restored" is closer to Alex's fingers picture than a courtroom is. As I recall she ties karma to unity: harming another is harming the whole. Verify that sentence.

10. Brotherhood and duty.
Claim: The Society's first object is to form a nucleus of the Universal Brotherhood of Humanity without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour. In the Key brotherhood is a fact in nature and not a sentiment. Duty is what is owed to humanity, especially the poorer and more helpless; an unpaid debt leaves us spiritually insolvent in the next life; Theosophy is the quintessence of duty (recalled near-verbatim). The root evil is separateness, which Theosophical writers call "the heresy of separateness".
Who: The Key, the sections on practical Theosophy and the mission. I cannot locate "heresy of separateness"; it may be in the Voice. (Fact-checker: I cannot place it either, [U]. It is credited to Blavatsky on many sites with no locator. Do not credit it to her until a primary text is found.)
Traditions cited: the shared ethics of the religions; the Mahayana bodhisattva ideal.
Tier: [M] for the object and the duty passage; [U] for the origin of the phrase.
Use: The phrase labels Alex's axiom exactly. Find it before using it.

11. The Q&A on Buddhism, Hinduism, Christianity and Spiritualism.
Claim: Asked in the Key whether the Society is Buddhist, Hindu, Christian or Spiritualist, she answers no to each. Buddhism: not Buddhist, though she and Olcott took the five precepts in Ceylon in 1880 (Galle, 25 May 1880 [M, added]); Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism should have been "Budhism" (budha, wisdom) [U]; the Buddha's no-self denies a personal soul, not the higher individuality; nirvana is not annihilation. Hinduism: the Society draws on Eastern literature without being Hindu. Christianity: against dogma, eternal hell, vicarious atonement and a personal God, not against Christ, whom she treats as an initiate or the Christos principle. Spiritualism: the phenomena are real, the spirit hypothesis wrong because the "spirits" are shells, and rebirth should be added.
Who: The Key, the sections on the working system and on the Society's relations to Theosophy [U which].
Traditions cited: Buddhism (Theravada and Mahayana), Hinduism (Vedanta), Christianity, Spiritualism.
Tier: [M] for the gist; [U] for the order, wording and the "Budhism" remark.
Scholars: Her reading of anatta is the clearest case of harmonizing. Most Buddhist scholars hold that the Buddha denied any lasting self, higher or lower. Say "she reads", not "Buddhism teaches".
Use: A template for saying what the essay is not while using each tradition as a witness.

12. The Voice: origin and form.
Claim: Three Fragments, "The Voice of the Silence", "The Two Paths" and "The Seven Portals", 1889, "dedicated to the few". The Preface says it comes from "The Book of the Golden Precepts", one of the works put into the hands of mystic students in the East, that she knew many precepts by heart, and that it belongs to the "Heart Doctrine" (inner) as against the "Eye Doctrine" (outer) and to the Mahayana. She links it to the series of the Book of Dzyan [U].
Who: The Voice, Preface and notes.
Traditions cited: Mahayana Buddhism (Yogacara and Tibetan flavour), Raja Yoga.
Tier: [M] for the Fragments and the Golden Precepts claim (second recall agrees, including the three titles and the dedication); [U] for the Dzyan remark.
Scholars: Tibetologists have not identified a Book of the Golden Precepts or a text called Dzyan. Her materials were Western translations of her day, her reading, and her claimed teachers.
Use: Present it as a Theosophical text in Mahayana dress, not as a Tibetan scripture.

14. The Seven Portals.
Claim: Fragment III names seven gates, each a virtue: dana (charity and love), shila (harmony in word and act, the key that balances cause and effect), kshanti (patience), viraga (indifference to pleasure and pain, illusion conquered), virya (dauntless energy), dhyana (meditation; in my recall its "golden gate", once opened, leads toward the realm of eternal Sat [U for the gloss; the colleague's "golden gate to the narrow path" is not in my recall]), prajna (wisdom, the key that makes a Bodhisattva). Mahayana lists six perfections (paramitas): dana, shila, kshanti, virya, dhyana, prajna. The Voice inserts viraga as the fourth.
Who: The Voice, Fragment III.
Traditions cited: Mahayana paramitas (six standard, expanded to ten in the Dasabhumika [M]); Theravada parami (the Pali list has ten [M]).
Tier: [M] for the list and order; [U] for the glosses' wording.
Scholars: The seven are not a standard Buddhist list. The sevenfold form is Theosophical (principles, planes, rounds). Viraga is a Buddhist term but not a paramita.
Use: A stage map to set beside the arrow from minus four to plus four, with viraga as the portal nearest to letting go.

15. The Two Paths and the Great Renunciation.
Claim: Fragment II sets two ways side by side. One is private liberation: the Pratyeka Buddha, in her usage a Buddha for himself, who enters nirvana in the Dharmakaya. The other is renunciation: one who has earned release refuses it (the "Great Renunciation") and takes the Nirmanakaya state to stay in touch with humanity as a protector, a "Guardian Wall" [U]. The last pages put the choice to a disciple who has passed the seven portals.
Who: The Voice, Fragments II and III and the closing notes.
Traditions cited: Mahayana (Pratyekabuddha, bodhisattva, the three bodies).
Tier: [M] for the structure and choice; [U] for "Guardian Wall" (second recall agrees that the phrase is in her notes; not verified).
Scholars: Pratyekabuddha is a real Buddhist category (a solitary realizer); "selfish" is Mahayana polemic, which she follows. Her Nirmanakaya, an adept who stays, differs from the Mahayana trikaya, where the nirmanakaya is the manifestation body in which a Buddha appears.

## 4. How they compare: the method

1. A single source story. The Wisdom-Religion is the trunk, the world religions its branches. The proof is the agreement itself plus a lineage (Ammonius, the Neoplatonists, the Masters). A historical claim, and the weakest part.

2. Equating terms. The Glossary labels each term by language and lines it up with a gloss and a counterpart. Example: Alaya, the "storehouse consciousness" of Yogacara, becomes the Universal Soul, then Anima Mundi and the Over-Soul. Scholars say alaya-vijnana is an individual stream carrying karmic seeds, not a universal soul. That is the comparer equating what specialists keep apart.

3. Tabulating systems. The seven principles are set beside the five sheaths, the three upadhis plus Atma, and Sinnett's seven names. The method assumes that the count of divisions only shows how finely each tradition cuts.

4. Esoteric against exoteric. Where traditions disagree she says the outer forms are literal, corrupted and priest-made, and the inner core is the same (example: no-self read as denying only the personality). This lets her harmonize almost anything, so the method cannot lose.

5. Quote and gloss. She quotes Gospel, Gita, Upanishad and Buddhist texts in the notes to the Voice and the Secret Doctrine and glosses them in Theosophical terms. Of her method she says, in the Secret Doctrine's Preface (borrowing Montaigne), that she made a nosegay of culled flowers and brought only the string that ties them [M, wording U]. The string is the Theosophical scheme. (Second recall agrees on the Montaigne image; the locator, Preface or Introductory, is [U].)

How she explains differences: corruption of an original, literal reading of allegory, translators who miss the inner meaning. Alex needs none of this. He can say the same few ideas turn up in different vocabularies and decline to explain why.

## 5. What scholars say, short

- Campbell (1980), Godwin (1994), Washington (1993): the standard histories. Theosophy as a major carrier of Eastern ideas to the West, rooted in earlier esoteric currents, with a sceptical but fair view of her influence.
- Bevir (1994), Hanegraaff (1996), Hammer (2004): a bridge that reworked Eastern ideas in a Western occult frame, using the language of science and comparative religion to claim authority.
- Lopez (1998), Prisoners of Shangri-La: the Tibetan Masters and the Book of Dzyan belong to Western imagination.
- Max Müller (1890s): Buddhism has no esoteric core. He wrote on Theosophy in his Gifford Lectures "Theosophy, or Psychological Religion" (1892) and on Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism in The Nineteenth Century (1893) [M for titles, U for exact years]. The standard Buddhological reply to "Esoteric Buddhism" is that in the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16, section 2.25 in Walshe's numbering [U]) the Buddha says he has no closed fist of a teacher, holding nothing back [M, locator DN 16 only]. That line is not claimed to be Müller's.
- Guénon, Le Théosophisme (1921) and L'Erreur spirite (1923): the Traditionalists treated Theosophy as a counterfeit, so Alex's two comparers disliked each other.
- Hodgson (1885) against her phenomena, Harrison against Hodgson (the paper is Journal of the SPR 53, 1986, pp. 286 to 310; the book is 1997 [M]): her honesty is still argued, and scholars mostly study what the movement did.

My judgement: a skilled synthesizer with a poor way of settling disputes between sources, and good primary evidence of what Theosophists believed and equated.

## 9. Perennial philosophy is wider than Huxley (added on the user's instruction, 2026-10-07)

Status: outside slice T03, written from memory by the fact-checker, no search, all [M] unless marked [U]. The point from the user: there are many more books in the perennial philosophy movement than the one Huxley wrote, and Huxley is not even the best of its authors. "Best" is a judgement, and I have not ranked anyone. What can be said as fact is below.

Huxley's own place. The Perennial Philosophy (1945) is an anthology of passages with his commentary, the best known book under that name. Its three-part description (a metaphysic, a psychology, an ethic) is in the Foreword; part-2-notes.md says it has been checked only through secondary quotations. Huxley himself leaned on earlier perennialists: Coomaraswamy is cited often in the book [U on the extent]. He is a popularizer of a body of work that existed before him.

Roots, before the twentieth century.
- Marsilio Ficino (Corpus Hermeticum translated 1463; Plato translated 1484) and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (the 900 Theses, 1486): the "prisca theologia", an ancient wisdom said to run through Hermes, Zoroaster, Plato and Moses. [M]
- Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia (1540), the first use of the phrase; Leibniz, a letter of 26 August 1714 (both already in part-2-notes.md). [M]

The Traditionalist school, where the doctrine was worked out.
- René Guénon (1886 to 1951): Introduction générale à l'étude des doctrines hindoues (1921), L'Homme et son devenir selon le Vêdânta (1925), La Crise du monde moderne (1927); also Le Théosophisme (1921), which attacks the Theosophy this file covers. [M]
- Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (1877 to 1947): Hinduism and Buddhism (1943), Time and Eternity (1947), The Transformation of Nature in Art (1934). [M]
- Frithjof Schuon (1907 to 1998): De l'unité transcendante des religions (1948; English The Transcendent Unity of Religions, 1953), also Understanding Islam [U for the year]. [M]
- Followers: Titus Burckhardt (Introduction to Sufi Doctrine) [U for the year]; Martin Lings (The Book of Certainty, 1952; A Sufi Saint of the Twentieth Century, 1961); Marco Pallis (The Way and the Mountain, 1960; A Buddhist Spectrum, 1980) [U for the years]; Whitall Perry, A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom (1971), a long thematic anthology of the same kind as Huxley's but larger. [M]
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr: Knowledge and the Sacred (1981, Gifford Lectures); Religion and the Order of Nature (1996). Huston Smith: The Religions of Man (1958; later The World's Religions), Forgotten Truth (1976). [M]

Authors in the wider "common core" line, who are not Traditionalists.
- William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902); Evelyn Underhill, Mysticism (1911); Rudolf Otto, Mysticism East and West (German 1926) [U for the English year]; W. T. Stace, Mysticism and Philosophy (1960); Alan Watts, The Supreme Identity (1950); Ken Wilber, The Spectrum of Consciousness (1977). [M]

The critics, so the picture is fair (already in part-2-notes.md): Katz (1978), Proudfoot (1985), Zaehner (Mysticism Sacred and Profane, 1957), Forman (The Problem of Pure Consciousness, 1990), Hick (An Interpretation of Religion, 1989) [M].

Why it matters for Part two. (1) Alex's two comparers are a feud: Guénon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon rejected Theosophy as a counterfeit, so "the perennial philosophy and Theosophy" are not one family. (2) The perennialist argument that traditions differ at the outer level and agree at the inner is Schuon's thesis, and it is close to Alex's "the popular face against the inner face" correction of 2026-10-07; the essay can credit Schuon and the Traditionalists for it and not only Huxley. (3) Alex's method differs from all of them: he derives the common core from an axiom and does not argue that a primordial tradition exists.
