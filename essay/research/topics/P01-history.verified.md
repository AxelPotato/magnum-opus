## 1. The picture in brief

The idea has four stages. First, a lineage claim. Plethon (1438), Ficino (1463), Pico (1486) and Steuco (1540) said that one true wisdom was given early, by God or by Zoroaster or Hermes or Moses, passed down to Plato and the Church fathers, and that all later religions and philosophies are echoes or corruptions of it. The proof was genealogy and the method was concordance. Second, a peace claim. Cusa (1453) and Dara Shikoh (1650s) used the same sameness to argue for tolerance, with the two traditions set side by side term by term. Third, the lineage collapsed. Casaubon redated the Hermetica in 1614, and Leibniz (1714) reused the phrase but turned a fixed deposit into a slow progress, "seeds of truth" that grow. Fourth, the claim moved to philology and experience. Jones, Anquetil, Schopenhauer, Emerson, Müller, Ramakrishna and Vivekananda compared texts and states of mind, and the Parliament of 1893 staged the comparison in public. Lessing in between said the sameness cannot be proved and that the proof is the life. A fifth stage, outside this slice, comes after the Parliament: the twentieth-century schools that took Steuco's phrase as their banner (the Traditionalists from 1921, Huxley's anthology in 1945, Smith in 1976). It is only outlined in findings 15 and 16; the sibling slices P02 to P09 cover it.

Three kinds of explanation for the agreement keep returning: common revelation, common transmission, common experience. Alex proposes a fourth: common logic, the agreement follows from one axiom. That is a real difference from every figure here, and a strength, because the first two explanations have lost most of their historical support.

Most of these comparers had a home team: Ficino, Cusa and Steuco put Christianity at the top, and Dara read Vedanta in Sufi words. Alex is not religious, so he has no team.

## 2. Findings

1. The ancient seeds (before the Renaissance)

Claim: Early Christian and Platonist writers already held that Greek wisdom borrowed from Moses, or that the true religion existed before Christ. Who: Numenius (second half of the second century; the colleague's "mid" is loose [M]), quoted by Clement of Alexandria, Stromata I.22.150.4 [C: Stanford Encyclopedia Numenius entry and Philip Harland's blog both appeared in results]. The saying is "what is Plato but Moses speaking Attic Greek" (the English wording varies by translator [M]). Augustine, Retractationes (c. 427), is reported to say that the thing now called the Christian religion existed among the ancients and never did not exist from the beginning of the human race [S: quoted in secondary sources in results, not checked in the original; locator Retr. I.13.3 from memory]. My memory of the Latin continues "...nec defuit ab initio generis humani, quousque Christus veniret in carne, unde vera religio, quae iam erat, coepit appellari Christiana" [M, wording to be checked]: the sentence ends by putting Christ at the turning point, which is the home-team move. Traditions: Hebrew, Greek. Muslim side: Suhrawardi (d. 1191) identified the wisdom of the Zoroastrian sages with Hermes, Pythagoras and Plato, and is called in modern writing a reviver of "perennial wisdom", which one result says existed among Hindus, Persians, Babylonians, Egyptians and Greeks [C: philosophy.tabrizu.ac.ir and al-islam.org results; the list with Hindus reads like a modern gloss]. Tier: C and S. Use: the genealogy move is ancient. Do not quote Augustine until checked. Added in the verification pass, from memory [M], not searched: the claim of one truth under many names is older still. Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride 67 (about 120 CE), says the gods are not different for different peoples, just as sun, moon and sky are common to all under different names. Justin Martyr (First Apology 46, Second Apology 13) speaks of the seed of the Logos in the philosophers, so that Socrates counts as a Christian before Christ. Symmachus (Relatio 3, 384 CE) said that so great a mystery cannot be reached by one road ("uno itinere non potest perveniri ad tam grande secretum"; gist and locator [M], Latin from memory, check). Leibniz's "semina veritatis" later echoes Justin's seeds. Tier for the additions: M.

2. Plethon, Florence 1438 to 1439

Claim: The Byzantine philosopher Gemistos Plethon came to the Council of Florence and taught Plato and Zoroaster to the Florentines, and argued that the oldest sage was Zoroaster. Who: Plethon, De differentiis, Summary of the doctrines of Zoroaster and Plato, Laws, Magian Oracles edition [C: Wikipedia Gemistos Plethon, New World Encyclopedia, and the Prometheus Trust Patrizi result]. He described a golden chain from Zoroaster to Plotinus, Porphyry and Iamblichus [C]. His Laws was condemned by Gennadios [C]. He is said to have moved Cosimo de' Medici to found the Platonic Academy under Ficino [C; some scholars treat this as legend, [M]]. Traditions: Persian (Magi), Greek, Neoplatonist. Tier: C. Use: the earliest source for one chain from the East to Plato. Reincarnation in Plethon: [U]; my memory is that his Laws teach the immortality of the soul and its repeated return in human bodies, and that Gennadios condemned this, but I cannot place the passage (check Woodhouse, George Gemistos Plethon: The Last of the Hellenes, 1986).

3. Marsilio Ficino, 1463 to 1492

Claim: One ancient theology, "prisca theologia", runs from Hermes through Orpheus, Aglaophemus and Pythagoras to Plato, and Christianity completes it. Who: Ficino, Argumentum to the Pimander (the Corpus Hermeticum translation of 1463, requested by Cosimo, first printed at Treviso in 1471) [C: Rome La Sapienza research pages]. The Argumentum calls Hermes the "first author of theology" and says one consistent sect of ancient theology was built "in wondrous order" from six theologians, beginning with Mercury and perfected by divine Plato [C: hispadoc and Brandeis results; the English is a search paraphrase, so do not quote it as his words]. The six, from memory [M]: Mercurius (Hermes), Orpheus, Aglaophemus, Pythagoras, Philolaus, Plato. The five names given elsewhere in this file omit Philolaus. Later he leaned toward Zoroaster as first [C]. Ficino named his Platonic tradition "pia philosophia" (pious philosophy) and Christian religion "docta religio" (learned religion) [C: Docta religio and pia philosophia article results]. In De christiana religione (written 1474, printed 1476 [M]) he holds that belief is universal among peoples while salvation lies in Christ ("religio naturalis") [C: akademika and unito results]. Also Theologia Platonica (written 1469 to 1474, printed 1482 [M]), Plotinus with commentary (1492) [C]. He rejected Averroes' single intellect for all humans in Theologia Platonica book 15 [C: results on Averroism]. Traditions: Egyptian, Orphic, Pythagorean, Platonic, Hebrew, Christian, with Zoroaster. Tier: C. Use: the lineage method in its clearest form. Note the contrast with Alex: Ficino fought the idea of one shared mind, because he wanted every soul to be a lasting individual.

4. Nicholas of Cusa, De pace fidei, 1453

Claim: After the fall of Constantinople Cusa wrote a vision of a heavenly council in which wise men of many nations agree that there is "one religion in a variety of rites". Who: De pace fidei (1453); English by Jasper Hopkins, 1990 [C: St Andrews Cusanus Society, National Humanities Center, Stanford Encyclopedia result lists]. The Latin formula is "Non est nisi religio una in rituum varietate" [C: philarchive Aiken result]. Caution [U]: I am sure of the fragment "una religio in rituum varietate" but not of the word order and verb around it; copy the sentence from the Latin edition (Klibansky and Bascour, Heidelberg Academy edition, vol. 7, 1959 [M]) before quoting. Method: the sages are brought to agree that all worship one God under different names and rites, and the Word (Logos) is the bridge; a scholar reads him as saying the Gospels and the Qur'an both affirm Jesus as the Word [C]. His deeper metaphysics is the One unfolding into the many: complicatio (enfolding) and explicatio (unfolding), the world as the "contracted maximum" in De docta ignorantia book 2 [C: Alfsvag and Brage results]. Scholars: the "una religio" is read both as a rational universal religion and as Christianity in disguise; Cusa also wrote Cribratio Alkorani (1461), a hard attack on Muhammad, and took part in crusade planning [C]. The participant list (Greek, Italian, Arab, Indian, Chaldean, Jew, Scythian, Persian and others) is from memory [M]. Tier: C. Use: the earliest Western text that does what Alex does in Part two, and its metaphysics (one unfolding into many) is his axiom in fifteenth century Latin. Check Hopkins for exact sentences.

5. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, 1486 to 1489

Claim: Pico gathered 900 theses from Greek, Hebrew, Arabic and Latin writers to be defended in Rome, to show that the schools agree. Who: Conclusiones (1486) and the Oration, later called "On the Dignity of Man" [C: Wikipedia, Britannica, New World Encyclopedia]. Sources: Hermes, Zoroaster, Orpheus, Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle and commentators, Avicenna, Averroes, Aquinas, Scotus, Jewish kabbalists [C]. His friends called him "Princeps Concordiae" [C]; it is also a pun on his title, since the Pico family held Mirandola and Concordia [M]. He thought Plato and Aristotle used different words for the same ideas, and that the Hebrew and Hermetic sources gave the same view of God as the Old Testament in other words [C: Wikipedia and New World results]. He founded Christian Kabbalah [C]. In 1487 a papal commission condemned thirteen theses; he wrote the Apologia in 1487 (the colleague had 1489 [M]) and the pope then condemned the whole book by a bull of August 1487, after which Pico left for France and was briefly arrested there in 1488 [M]. One of the thirteen condemned points was that it is more reasonable to believe Origen saved than damned [M, New Advent result per the colleague]. The colleague's gloss "Origen, excommunicated for teaching reincarnation" was removed: Origen was condemned long after his death (543 and 553) for the pre-existence of souls and universal restoration, and he argued against transmigration into other bodies [M; P16 finding 16 says the same]. Heptaplus (1489) and De ente et uno (1491) are from memory [M]; the Catholic Encyclopedia snippet gives other dates and is old. One result claims the Oration contains "Plato is Moses in Attic Greek"; that is Numenius via Clement and I could not confirm it for Pico [U]; my memory has no such line in the Oration. Tier: C. Use: the technique is "same idea, different words". His fate shows the cost of saying so inside a church.

6. Agostino Steuco, De perenni philosophia, 1540

Claim: There is one first principle of all things and one knowledge of it among all peoples, given from Adam on, preserved in Hebrew scripture, and echoed by Chaldean, Egyptian, Greek and Roman sages. Who: Steuco (c. 1497 to 1548), Vatican librarian from 1538, bishop of Kisamos on Crete, Counter-Reformation polemicist [C: Wikipedia]. The work is De perenni philosophia libri X (1540) [C]. Words: "one principle of all things, of which there has always been one and the same knowledge among all peoples" [C: Wikipedia perennial philosophy and Sage encyclopedia results; an English rendering, not the Latin]. Doctrines he found shared: the trinity of God, creation, immortality of the soul, end of the world and renewal [C: Murcia Daimon article result]. He worked from Ficino and Pico [C]. Schmitt (1966) showed the phrase is Steuco's, not Leibniz's [C]. Steuco's own meaning: a standing deposit of revealed truth whose guardian was the Church, in an age when Luther was attacking it [C for the polemic, S for the guardian reading]. Tier: C and S. Use: when the essay says the phrase "goes back to Steuco", add one clause that he was a papal librarian proving that Christian doctrine was known to everyone. That is far from Alex's use.

7. After the collapse: Casaubon, Cudworth, Walker

Claim: In 1614 Isaac Casaubon showed that the Corpus Hermeticum was late antique, not Mosaic-age Egyptian, and the idea of a datable first revelation lost its footing. Who: Casaubon (1614); Frances Yates (1964) called 1614 a watershed and said it "shattered the basis of all attempts to build a natural theology in Hermeticism" [C: First Things Egyptomania result; the quote is Yates as quoted there]. The wording is Yates as quoted in a magazine and not checked against the 1964 book, and I do not know the page [U]; do not quote it until the book is open. D. P. Walker, The Ancient Theology (1972), traces the claim that pagan wisdom derived from Moses, or from Noah, Enoch or Adam [C]. Ralph Cudworth (True Intellectual System, 1678) kept ancient theology alive among the Cambridge Platonists; Mosheim's Latin translation (1733) took it to Germany [C]. Tier: C. Use: the historical claim (a common source) did not survive. Alex should say so in one sentence: he does not claim one original revelation.

8. Dara Shikoh, 1650s

Claim: A Mughal prince and Sufi argued that Sufism and Upanishadic Vedanta are the same knowledge in two vocabularies. Who: Majma al-Bahrayn, "Mingling of the Two Oceans" (c. 1655; the title is from Qur'an 18:60, the meeting place of the two seas where Moses finds Khidr [M]), twenty or twenty-two short sections [C: Matheson Trust, New Age Islam, Wikipedia]; Sirr-i Akbar, "the Great Secret" (1657), fifty-two Upanishads into Persian [C]. Words: he held that "apart from the manner of exposition" there was no essential difference between Islam and Hinduism [C: paraphrase in New Age Islam result]. Concretely he sets terms side by side: the five elements against panch bhut; jamal and jalal (beauty, majesty) against the three gunas [U: two Arabic terms against three gunas, so the summary is incomplete; a third term is missing and which guna pairs with which attribute is unchecked]; Brahma, Vishnu and Mahesh against the angels Jibril, Mika'il and Israfil [C]. In the Sirr-i Akbar introduction he said the "hidden book" of the Qur'an (kitab al-maknun) is the Upanishads [C]. His Persian text reached Anquetil-Duperron in 1775 and became the Latin Oupnek'hat of 1801 and 1802 [C]. The 1775 manuscript was given to Anquetil by the French resident Jean-Baptiste Gentil, and the Latin was printed at Strasbourg [M]. His execution in 1659 is from memory [M]. Scholars: Supriya Gandhi, The Emperor Who Never Was (2020), and others read his translation choices as pushing Advaita and Sufi compatibility [C for the gist]. Tier: C. Use: the clearest worked example of comparison by translating terms. Alex's "different words, same thing" could use Dara as the first system to do it.

9. G. W. Leibniz, 1714

Claim: The truth is spread across all sects and can be panned out. Who: letter to Nicolas Remond, 26 August 1714 [C: several results agree on date and addressee]. Words (English, as quoted in results, translator not shown): one would "draw the gold from the dross, the diamond from its mine, the light from the shadows; and this would be in effect a kind of perennial philosophy" [C: Bhaktivinoda Thakura/philosophia perennis article result and Muse result]. The Latin phrase in the letter is "perennis quaedam Philosophia". He also praised the "Orientals, who have beautiful and grand ideas of the Deity" [C; the place inside the letter is unknown, and the line may come from another Leibniz text on China]. Amberger (Lexicon Philosophicum 7, 2019): Leibniz took Steuco's term but changed it; truth was not given once at the start, mankind approaches it gradually; the primordial truths are "semina veritatis" that must grow; so the idea implies "eternal progress" [C: abstract]. Traditions: Plato, scholastics, Orientals (China in particular) [S]. Tier: C. Use: Leibniz is the best fit for Alex's stance. He does not say every school is right. He says there is gold in all of them. The check: read the Rutherford translation at dss-sites.ucsd.edu (RemondVII1714.pdf).

10. Jones, Anquetil, Schopenhauer: the East enters philosophy

Claim: William Jones (Asiatic Society, 1784) compared Hindu and Greek gods inside the biblical frame of Noah's descendants, for example Saturn, Manu and Noah [C: UCL Vasunia chapter]. His "common source" sentence ("sprung from some common source, which, perhaps, no longer exists") is about the languages Sanskrit, Greek and Latin, not about religions, and comes from the Third Anniversary Discourse of 1786 [M for the date and the scope; the wording is [C as quoted]]. Do not use it as a religious comparison. The comparison of the gods (Saturn, Menu, Noah) is the essay "On the Gods of Greece, Italy, and India", written in 1784 and first printed in 1788 [M]. Anquetil-Duperron's Oupnek'hat (1801 to 1802) reached Schopenhauer [C]. Schopenhauer said it "has been the solace of my life and will be the solace of my death" (Parerga und Paralipomena II, §184 per the result) [C: Mainz Schopenhauer-Jahrbuch result]. The wording that circulates online is longer ("In the whole world there is no study so beneficial and so elevating as that of the Oupnekhat...") and I cannot name its translator, so treat the long form as [U] until it is checked against the German or a named translation. He first met the Oupnek'hat in 1814 through Friedrich Majer in Dresden [M]. He used maya, tat tvam asi and the principle of individuation in his system [C]; named Nirvana as "extinction" [C]; cited Eckhart by the third edition of 1859 [C]; and wrote that true Christianity, the Christianity of the New Testament and of the mystics, "agrees both with Buddhism and with Brahmanism in ultimate aim" [C: Fussell BPS essay result; the chapter locator is unknown, probably World as Will vol. 2]. He took up rebirth as "palingenesis" in the second edition (1844) [C]. Tier: C. Use: the philosophical hinge. He found the veil (maya), the sameness of all beings (tat tvam asi) and rebirth in India and in the mystics. Caution: his conclusion was denial of the will, Alex's is return, so do not suggest he agreed with the pessimism.

11. Lessing, 1779 and 1780

Claim 1 (ring parable, Nathan the Wise, 1779): three sons hold three identical rings; the true one cannot be proved, "almost as little as today the genuine faith"; each should act as if his ring were true and prove it by love and goodness [C: Illinois, Pitt and philpapers results; wording in English is from a translation not named]. The German, from memory [M]: "Fast so unerweislich, als uns itzt der rechte Glaube" (Nathan der Weise, III, 7). The story predates Boccaccio, with Muslim roots [C: medievalists.net]. Claim 2 (Education of the Human Race, 1780, §§93 to 100): "why should not every individual man have existed more than once upon this World?" and "Why should I not come back as often as I am capable of acquiring fresh knowledge, fresh expertness?" [C: education-uk.org result; paragraph numbers per snippet]. Caution [U]: from memory I would put "is this hypothesis so ridiculous merely because it is the oldest" at §94 and the "come back as often" sentence at about §97 or §98, so the colleague's numbers may be off by one; the first 53 paragraphs appeared in 1777 and all 100 in 1780 [M]. Open the Robertson translation before giving a number. Revelation is a school that teaches the species in stages. Traditions: Judaism, Christianity, Islam; the oldest idea of rebirth is called a natural thought of the human understanding (§95 per the colleague, §94 in my memory [U]) [C]. Tier: C. Use: Lessing is the one thinker in this slice who ties the sameness of religions to lives as school. His honest position matches Alex's: no proof, only examples that fit.

12. Emerson and the Transcendentalists, 1841 to 1860

Claim: The soul is one whole in which each person takes part; separation is how it looks from inside. Who: The Over-Soul (Essays, First Series, 1841). Words: "We live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles. Meantime within man is the soul of the whole; the wise silence; the universal beauty, to which every part and particle is equally related, the eternal ONE" [C: standardebooks, FIU and Shmoop results]. Sources: Plato, Plutarch, Plotinus, Proclus, Swedenborg [C]; Vedanta shows plainly, though Emerson read the Gita and Vishnu Purana in earnest only from 1845 [C: Hinduism Today and others]. Poem Brahma (1857) rests on the Katha Upanishad and Gita [C]. "Illusions" (Conduct of Life, 1860): "I find men victims of illusions in all parts of life" [C]. Caution [U]: my memory has the singular, "victims of illusion", followed by "Children, youths, adults and old men, all are led by one bawble or another"; the plural may be a paraphrase. Check the text before quoting. Also check the punctuation of the Over-Soul sentence in the edition used (a comma or a semicolon before "the eternal ONE") [U], and that "Over-Soul" is Emerson's own coinage [M]. Thoreau on the Gita at Walden [C]. Tier: C. Use: the best single quotation for the axiom in English, public domain. Emerson claimed to hear the sameness, not to prove it.

13. Max Müller and the science of religion, 1873 to 1893

Claim: Religions can be compared with the methods of philology, and the comparison is good for tolerance. Who: Introduction to the Science of Religion (1873), "He who knows one, knows none" (his rewording of Goethe's remark about languages [M]) [C: Hartford and Essential Max Müller results]; Sacred Books of the East (begun 1879) including the Upanishads [C]; he never visited India [C]. Words: comparative study of religion as a remedy for intolerance; a unity that ought to comprehend them all, the unity of toleration, the unity of love [C: a search-result paraphrase, shown in quotation marks in the first version but not verified as Müller's wording; do not quote]. 14. Ramakrishna and the Parliament of 1893

Claim: Every path leads to the same goal, and each faith should grow by its own law. 15. The later life of the phrase

Claim: After Leibniz the phrase went quiet. Per a result reading like Schmitt, Neo-Thomists revived "philosophia perennis" early in the twentieth century for their own tradition, and by mid century many schools used it with many definitions [C: scanned copy at jainqq.org; locator unknown]. Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris (1879) lies behind that revival [C; the link to the phrase is [S]]. Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy (Harper, 1945) is one anthology that gave the phrase its modern meaning for a general readership. The error that Leibniz coined the phrase is in Huxley's own Foreword [M; the sibling files P07 and P08 report the same, also from memory], and the colleague's search result only repeated it; Steuco used the phrase first (Schmitt 1966). Tier: C and S. Use: the twentieth-century movement borrowed a Renaissance term, and it is not one thing. The Traditionalists (Guénon, Coomaraswamy, Schuon) built on Vedanta, Sufism and Neoplatonic and scholastic metaphysics and attacked Theosophy (Guénon, Le Théosophisme, 1921; L'Erreur spirite, 1923) [M]; Huxley drew on mystics of many traditions; Theosophy ran in parallel with its own comparing. The first version's line that the movement was filled with Theosophical content was wrong for the Traditionalists and was removed.

## 4. How they compare: the method

Five methods, with examples.

Genealogy. Claim a chain from a first sage to the later schools, so that agreement is inheritance. Plethon (Zoroaster to Plotinus), Ficino (Hermes to Plato), Steuco (Adam to the Church), Jones (Noah's descendants). Differences are explained as decay or loss.

Concordance by translating terms. Put a term from tradition A beside a term from tradition B and declare them equivalent. Pico set Plato beside Moses beside the Kabbalah. Dara did it line by line: elements, attributes, angels and gods, states of the soul. This is the method closest to what Alex does. The risk is that the comparer decides which word in B matches which word in A. Dara's Vedanta came out in Sufi vocabulary.

Council or dialogue. Stage a meeting in which each side agrees: Cusa in a vision, Lessing in a parable, the Parliament in a hall. The agreement is in the script.

Philology and history. Compare texts as texts (Jones, Müller, Anquetil). Sameness here is a finding about language and diffusion, and Müller used it against the Theosophists.

Inner experience. Appeal to what the sages report. Ramakrishna lived several religions; Vivekananda said the streams all reach the sea; Emerson claimed to describe what he found within himself.

How they explain differences. In outline: (a) the rites differ and the faith is one (Cusa); (b) the words differ and the idea is one (Pico, Dara); (c) the stage of education differs (Lessing; Leibniz's "growth"); (d) corruption, so older is purer (Plethon, Steuco); (e) different paths up one mountain (Ramakrishna, Vivekananda); (f) the truth is in the practice and cannot be shown in words (Lessing's ring). Each of these is a claim by the comparer and not by the tradition. Cusa's one religion is still Christian at its heart on one reading (scholars disagree, see finding 4); Barrows' universal was still a larger Christianity.

How the claim changed over time. 1438 to 1540: one hidden truth, kept by a chain, completed by the Church. 1453 and 1650s: sameness as a reason for peace. 1614: the chain fails. 1714: a method of picking gold, with progress and no fixed deposit. 1779: sameness cannot be shown. 1800s: Asian texts become the main evidence and the Church loses its top place. 1893: Hindu and Buddhist speakers state the claim in their own voices, partly answering missionary pressure. Twentieth century: a timeless metaphysic (findings 15 and 16).

## 5. What scholars say

Yates and Walker: the Renaissance lineage was mistaken history that shaped Western thought for two centuries [C for the gist]. Schmitt: the phrase is Steuco's and later meanings differ [C]. Amberger: Leibniz transformed it [C]. On Cusa, scholars disagree whether his "one religion" is universal or Christian, and point to the Cribratio Alkorani [C]. On Dara, historians stress his Sufi framing of the Upanishads [C for the gist]. On the Parliament, historians note the Christian frame and the exclusions [C]. Of Müller, critics say he projected a Protestant model of religion on India [M]; Theosophists said he was unqualified [C]. Critics of the whole project (Steven Katz on experience shaped by each tradition, Richard King on Orientalism and "mysticism", Wouter Hanegraaff on "Platonic Orientalism") say comparers stripped context [M; not searched; another slice]. On the twentieth-century schools, Mark Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (Oxford University Press, 2004), is the standard history of Traditionalism [M]. Fair summary: the agreement is partly real, partly made, and the makers had a stake.
