## 1. The picture in brief

This slice is the twentieth-century psychological wing of the comparative project. Theosophists and Traditionalists compare doctrines. These people compare experiences, stages and patterns: Wilber with a stage table, Jung by amplification (a symbol set beside its parallels in myth, alchemy and religion), Eliade by morphology of the sacred, Campbell by one abstracted story, Grof by clinical reports, Maslow by interviews.

Five things matter for Alex. First, several state his axiom's shape, some more than others: Campbell's cosmogonic cycle (the one becomes many and goes back), Grof's Cosmic Game (Absolute Consciousness hiding from itself in play), Wilber's Atman project (a search for wholeness carried out from inside separation), Jung's Answer to Job (the whole needs separate beings to become conscious). Campbell's monomyth (separation, initiation, return) matches the shape of the three clauses only loosely, since its "return" is to the world and not to the One. Jung's Seven Sermons of 1916 (pleroma and creatura) match clauses one and two and warn against the third (see correction 13). Second, they explain why the separate self feels real (Wilber's drawn boundaries, Jung's ego against Self). Third, they supply the best cautions: the pre/trans fallacy, Engler's point that you need a self before you can let go of one, Jung's warning against imitating Eastern methods, Katz's claim that mystical experience is shaped by tradition, Ferrer's pluralism. Fourth, none is neutral: Wilber ranks, Jung psychologises, Eliade and Campbell are accused of imposing universals. Fifth, the movement is wider than Huxley. Wilber's own authorities for the "perennial" core are, as I recall, Huston Smith, Schuon, Coomaraswamy and Guenon [M]; Huxley's place in that list is [U]. The Eranos circle (Jung, Eliade, Scholem, Corbin and others; Campbell as editor of the English volumes) is an independent comparative school. See section 9.

### 2.1 Wilber, the spectrum of identity (1975, 1977, 1979)

Claim: consciousness is a spectrum of identities, from unity at one end to a narrow self at the other. Each step down is made by drawing a boundary, the first being the split between self and everything else. Therapies and traditions work at different levels.
Who: Wilber, "Psychologia Perennis: The Spectrum of Consciousness", Journal of Transpersonal Psychology, 1975 (vol. 7, no. 2 [M]; a reprint in Eye to Eye is [U], see correction 8); The Spectrum of Consciousness (Quest Books, 1977); No Boundary (1979; Center Publications, later Shambhala [M]).
Words: the "perennial psychology" is the psychological half of the perennial philosophy. The deepest identity is the ground of all awareness (Mind, Atman, Brahman) and ordinary identity is a contraction of it. Levels as I recall, top down: Mind, transpersonal bands, existential level, biosocial bands, ego, shadow [names and count U; the 1977 and 1979 books differ in how they list the lower levels, as I recall].
Traditions: Advaita Vedanta, Zen, Madhyamika, Plotinus, Eckhart; Western therapies on the lower levels.
Tier: [M]; level names [U]; reprint [U].
Use: the nearest structural twin of "the wall is real inside the system and gone at the processor": a boundary that is drawn can be undrawn. Side fact: Quest is the Theosophical Publishing House imprint (Wheaton, the publishing arm of the Theosophical Society in America) [M]. ### 2.2 Wilber, the Great Chain becomes the Great Nest (1995, 1996)

Claim: reality has levels (matter, body, mind, soul, spirit). Spirit is both the highest level and the ground of every level. Each level transcends and includes the one below, hence nest and not ladder.
Who: Wilber, Sex, Ecology, Spirituality (1995); A Brief History of Everything (1996). Great Chain of Being as a history of ideas is Arthur Lovejoy's 1936 book [M].
Words: premodern traditions saw the levels but lacked modern differentiations (science, morality, art), so the old maps need updating.
Traditions: Vedanta, Buddhism, Neoplatonism, Christian, Jewish and Islamic mysticism as one core. His authorities are, as I recall, Huston Smith (Forgotten Truth, 1976; subtitled "The Primordial Tradition", later "The Common Vision of the World's Religions"), Schuon, Coomaraswamy and Guenon [M]; Huxley's place [U]; his ranking among them [U]. Trap: the Great Nest is evolutionary and the Traditionalists' view of history is the opposite (correction 11).
Tier: [M]; Huxley and ranking [U].
Use: Spirit as the ground of every level is Alex's one machine under all windows. It is a second skeleton beside Huxley's three parts. The ranking invites Ferrer's critique (2.10). Lovejoy's chain is a history of the ideas of plenitude and gradation, not the same as the mystical levels Wilber uses; say "the Great Chain of Being" for the old picture and leave Lovejoy for the history.

### 2.3 Wilber, the Atman project (1980)

Claim: every stage of growth is underneath a search for the whole (Atman, Brahman). The search starts from inside separation, so it settles for substitutes: approval, power, possessions, even spiritual experiences. That is the Atman project. The real direction is the Atman telos, the actual return.
Who: Wilber, The Atman Project (Quest, 1980).
Words: each level has its own substitute and its own fear of dying to the level below, so growth always includes a small death. The drive that makes people grab is the drive that can take them to the real thing.
Traditions: Vedanta as frame; Plotinus, Aurobindo and the chakra and kosha schemes alongside [details U].
Tier: [M]. Early-phase Wilber (correction 21): cite as "Wilber in 1980".
Use: Part one's Driven room with a name: taking from outside what cannot be felt inside is the wish for wholeness aimed at the wrong target. Older cousin: Ernest Becker's immortality projects (The Denial of Death, 1973) [M]; that Wilber leans on Becker [U].

### 2.4 Wilber, translation and transformation

Claim: change inside a level (translation) rearranges the furniture of the current self. Change of level (transformation) is the death of that self and a rebirth. Most self-help is translation.
Who: Wilber, early 1980s (The Atman Project or A Sociable God, 1983). Locator unknown. The pair is used again in his later integral books as "translative" and "transformative".
Tier: [M] idea; first appearance [U].
Use: a near-exact match for Alex's polished program against the sandbox escape, and for Part one's driven person using meditation to be slightly more effective.

### 2.5 Wilber, Up from Eden (1981)

Claim: the story of culture mirrors that of a person. Early humanity lived in undifferentiated union (Eden is the state before the self was split out, not enlightenment). The Fall is the emergence of the separate ego, a necessary gain that came with dissociation. The way out is forward to an integrated, higher unity.
Who: Wilber, Up from Eden (1981), building on Jean Gebser (The Ever-Present Origin; German 1949 to 1953, English 1985 [M]) and Erich Neumann (The Origins and History of Consciousness, German 1949, English 1954 [M]).
Words: stages run roughly from uroboric through typhonic, magical and mythic to mental-egoic, then the centaur, an integrated mind-body self [names U]. Traditions: Genesis read psychologically, Greek myth.
Tier: [M]. Early-phase Wilber (correction 21): do not state this stage story as fact; Wilber revised parts of it.
Use: it answers "if we were one at the start and one at the end, what changed?": a differentiated self has been through the middle. Gebser's ever-present origin is close to Alex's claim [M].

### 2.6 Wilber, the pre/trans fallacy (1980, 1983)

Claim: states before the rational self (pre-personal) and beyond it (trans-personal) are both non-rational, so people confuse them. Reductionism reads the trans as the pre (mystical union is infant fusion). Elevationism reads the pre as the trans (childhood or primitive unity is already enlightenment).
Who: Wilber, "The Pre/Trans Fallacy", ReVision, 1980 (vol. 3, no. 2 [M]); the later book reprint is [U] on the edition (correction 9).
Words: the standard reductionist is Freud, who read the oceanic feeling (Romain Rolland's phrase, from his December 1927 letter; in Civilization and Its Discontents, 1930, chapter 1 [M]) as a leftover of infancy. Wilber sees elevationism in parts of Jung and in romantics who praise regression.
Tier: [M]; reprint edition [U].
Use: it names the objection a first-camp reader will raise (the unity feeling is a regression) and a trap on Alex's side (calling Drifting freedom).

### 2.7 Wilber, Eye to Eye (1983): how to check a mystical claim

Claim: valid knowledge in any field has three strands: an injunction (if you want to know this, do this), an apprehension (you look and see), and a confirmation or rejection by others who did the same. Contemplation is a method with a community check.
Who: Wilber, Eye to Eye (1983). He adapts the medieval three eyes (flesh, reason, contemplation) from Hugh of St Victor and Bonaventure [M, attribution U].
Words: he also argues that physics does not prove mysticism, and that books joining quantum theory to Eastern thought confuse two levels of knowing.
Tier: [M].
Use: a frame for "what you can check": the experiment is letting go, you run it yourself, you compare notes. That this is science in the strict sense is disputed.

### 2.8 Wilber, charts, deep and surface structure, states and stages, two idioms

Claim: traditions differ in surface structure (symbols, stories, gods) and share deep structure (levels of consciousness). A passing state (gross, subtle, causal, nondual) can occur at any stage of development, so a beginner can touch a high state without being at a high stage. Advaita (Shankara) and Madhyamika (Nagarjuna) point to one nondual realisation in two idioms, Self and emptiness, and the path is a paradox because the goal is what you already are.
Who: Wilber, Integral Psychology (2000), with long comparison charts of developmental models; Integral Spirituality (2006), with the states-and-stages grid (the Wilber-Combs lattice, after Allan Combs) [M]. The nondual claim runs through his later books [locators U].
Words: the nondual is not the top level but what every level is made of. Gross, subtle and causal map on waking, dreaming and deep sleep from Vedanta.
Traditions: Vedanta, Yogacara, Kashmir Shaivism, Plotinus, Aurobindo, Teresa of Avila, Dzogchen, Zen [which appear in which chart: U].
Tier: [M]; chart contents [U].
Use: states against stages explains "everybody visits, nobody stays". Here the comparer equates things specialists keep apart (Self and no-self), so say so, as Alex does for Buddhist interdependence.

### 2.9 Washburn, the spiral path

Claim: the ego arises from a dynamic ground (the source of psychic energy) by repressing it. Mature spiritual life returns to that ground without destroying the ego (regression in the service of transcendence), then integrates at a higher level. The path is a spiral, not a ladder.
Who: Michael Washburn, The Ego and the Dynamic Ground (1988); Transpersonal Psychology in Psychoanalytic Perspective (1994).
Traditions: Jung, Neumann, John of the Cross, alchemy [specifics U].
Tier: [M].
Use: Alex's return as a spiral through what the separation pushed down; his arrow can be read as ladder or loop. Wilber answered Washburn with the charge of the pre/trans fallacy (elevationism) [M]; Washburn is a critic of Wilber as well as a neighbour.

### 2.10 Ferrer, the participatory turn (2002)

Claim: transpersonal theory treats spiritual experience as a private inner event (Cartesian), assumes one pre-given ultimate that all mystics reach (perennialism), and ranks traditions by closeness to it (nondual above theistic). Ferrer proposes participatory pluralism: spiritual realities are enacted together by people and the mystery, so several ultimates and liberations can be valid.
Who: Jorge N. Ferrer, Revisioning Transpersonal Theory (SUNY, 2002); earlier, "The Perennial Philosophy Revisited", Journal of Transpersonal Psychology, 2000 [M].
Words: Ferrer does not say the traditions are unrelated; he says agreement of experience is weaker than perennialists claim and differences in goals are real (nondual Hindu and Buddhist schools against the Abrahamic theistic ones).
Tier: [M].
Use: the best scholarly form of the objection to "they all say the same thing". Alex's correction (convergence at the inner layer) answers part of it; he should say "many" and name what specialists object to.

### 2.11 The constructivist challenge: Katz, Stace, Zaehner, Forman

Zaehner (Mysticism Sacred and Profane, 1957) had separated nature, monistic and theistic mysticism; Robert Forman (editor, The Problem of Pure Consciousness, 1990) answered Katz with the pure consciousness event, awareness without content. Donald Rothberg wrote on the field's epistemology [specifics U].
Who: Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", in Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978) [M]. A sibling file recalls the line as "There are NO pure (i.e. unmediated) experiences" at about p. 26 (P08, memory); not cleared.
Tier: [M]; Rothberg [U].
Use: "the same things in different words" survives Katz if "the same things" means the structure Alex lists (a built self, a veil, a return) and not identical experiences.

### 2.12 Jung, the Self, the collective unconscious, individuation

Claim: under the personal unconscious lies a layer shared by all (the collective unconscious), made of archetypes. The Self is the archetype of wholeness, conscious and unconscious together. The ego is a small part of it. Individuation, the lifelong becoming whole, is the opposite of isolation.
Who: Jung, The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious (CW 9i); Aion (CW 9ii, 1951); Psychological Types (1921); Memories, Dreams, Reflections (German 1962, English 1963; correction 6). The individuation-is-not-isolation passage is in CW 7 [locator U; I recall the "individuation is not individualism" passage in the essay on the relations between the ego and the unconscious, near §267, and "individuation does not shut one out from the world, but gathers the world to oneself" in the same volume, U].
Words: Jung likened life to a plant on a rhizome, the living part below, the visible part brief [M, Prologue of Memories, Dreams, Reflections; see correction 7 on how far to read it]. He set Atman, the Tao and the Christ image beside the Self while insisting he described psychic facts, not metaphysics. Mandalas in Tibetan Buddhism and in Western patients' drawings were his picture of the Self.
Tier: [M]; CW 7 locator [U].
Use: the collective unconscious is a psychological "one machine under the processes", and the rhizome is a picture of what endures beneath a short visible life. Jung is agnostic about metaphysics, so call him a witness of the pattern, not of the axiom. Note that the collective unconscious is inherited structure shared by all, which is weaker than one whole that experiences.

### 2.13 Jung, the Seven Sermons (1916) and Answer to Job (1952)

Claim: in 1916, in the voice of the Gnostic Basilides, Jung wrote Septem Sermones ad Mortuos. The pleroma is the fullness where opposites cancel, the creatura is what is distinct, and the human being is both: made of the pleroma yet distinct through the principle of individuation. In Answer to Job he reads God as an unconscious totality of opposites that human moral consciousness exceeds, so God must enter human life to become conscious.
Who: Jung, Septem Sermones (privately printed 1916; appendix to Memories, Dreams, Reflections, number [U; my recall says Appendix V, which also appears in The Red Book (2009), but the same-kind-of-evidence rule keeps it [U]]); Answer to Job (1952, CW 11).
Words: the Sermons say that distinctiveness is the essence of the creatura and that we must strive for it, against the danger of falling back into the indistinctness of the pleroma. They do not say that the whole becomes conscious through beings, and they do not offer a return (correction 13). Answer to Job holds that the whole (God) becomes conscious through the human. Elsewhere Jung says the sole purpose of human existence is to kindle a light in the darkness of mere being [M, recalled wording, not cleared; correction 4].
Traditions: Gnosticism (Basilides, Abraxas), Christian incarnation, the Old Testament, alchemy.
Tier: [M]; appendix number [U]; locator for the "light" line [U].
Use: the Sermons are a Western twentieth-century statement of the first two clauses (a fullness, and distinction as what makes the creature) and run against the third. Answer to Job is the nearest Jungian answer to "why would the whole do it". Both are Jung's mythic writing, not clinical results. Say which clause each one speaks to.

### 2.14 Jung, the Eastern commentaries and his warning

Claim: Jung wrote psychological commentaries on Eastern texts and read their symbols as pictures of inner processes, while warning Westerners not to copy Eastern methods.
Who: commentary on The Secret of the Golden Flower (Wilhelm, 1929); on the Bardo Thodol (written 1935; in English in later editions of Evans-Wentz's book, the third edition of 1957 as I recall [M]); Yoga and the West (1936); foreword to D. T. Suzuki's Introduction to Zen Buddhism (1939); the 1932 Kundalini seminar (published 1996).
Words: in the Golden Flower commentary he praises the art of letting things happen and ties it to Meister Eckhart's letting go of oneself, and to action through non-action (wu wei) [recalled wording, not cleared; correction 12]. In the Bardo commentary the deities are archetypes of the unconscious. His warning: the Eastern way works by dissolving the ego, which is risky for a fragile Western ego, and imitation can be an escape.
Traditions: Daoism, Chinese alchemy, Tibetan Buddhism, Hindu yoga and Kundalini, Zen; Eckhart as Western parallel.
Tier: [M]; Eckhart link [U on place].
Use: a ready equation for letting go (wu wei, Gelassenheit, bittul, kenosis). His caution fits Part one's "ease back to the middle first" and Engler (2.21). Jung translates every symbol into psychology, which specialists reject.

### 2.15 Jung, alchemy, the one world, rebirth and karma

Claim: alchemy is a Western record of individuation: nigredo (blackening), albedo, rubedo, and the coniunctio, the union of opposites. In Gerhard Dorn, as Jung read him, the last union is with the unus mundus, the one world, which Jung linked to synchronicity and to a level where psyche and matter are not yet apart.
Who: Jung, Psychology and Alchemy (1944, CW 12; the dream series was the physicist Wolfgang Pauli's, identified later [M]); Mysterium Coniunctionis (1955 to 1956, CW 14); Synchronicity (1952). On rebirth: "Concerning Rebirth" (CW 9i), which lists five kinds (metempsychosis, reincarnation, resurrection, rebirth or renovatio, and participation in the process of transformation; correction 10) [list U in detail].
Words: Jung left open whether reincarnation is literal but said the idea belongs to the oldest layer of human thought. In Memories, Dreams, Reflections (chapter "On Life after Death" [M]) he wondered whether he lived to answer questions left open by earlier lives (recalled wording, correction 22) and linked it to karma [karma link 75 percent, U].
Tier: [M]; karma passage [U].
Use: unus mundus is the nearest Jungian match to "one machine". On rebirth and karma say Jung treated them as psychic realities and left the literal question open.

### 2.16 Grof, maps of the psyche and the Cosmic Game

Claim: the psyche has biographical, perinatal and transpersonal domains. The perinatal domain has four matrices tied to stages of birth: union with the mother, no exit, the death-rebirth struggle, and delivery with ego death and rebirth. Beyond lie identification with other beings, past-life scenes, archetypes, cosmic unity, the Void. In The Cosmic Game he states that Absolute Consciousness creates the universe as a game to explore itself, forgetting its nature as it plays.
Who: Stanislav Grof, Realms of the Human Unconscious (1975); Beyond the Brain (1985); The Holotropic Mind (1992); The Cosmic Game (SUNY, 1998); Spiritual Emergency (1989, with Christina Grof). Holotropic means moving toward wholeness.
Words: the death-rebirth sequence in his sessions, he says, matches initiation rites, Eleusis, shamanic dismemberment, the bardo stages and the dark night. He reports past-life and karmic scenes as experiential evidence of open status [cautiousness U].
Traditions: Hindu lila and karma, Buddhist Void, Kabbalah, Sufism, Gnosticism, Daoism, Eckhart, the Tibetan Book of the Dead. Alan Watts's The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are (1966) is a non-clinical relative with the same hide-and-seek picture [M; correction 2].
Tier: [M].
Use: a clinician's version of lila, a companion to Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 in the "why would the whole do it" passage. Say the data come from psychedelic and breathwork sessions. His perinatal "birth" is not the school's "birth" after plus four; avoid the pun.

### 2.17 Maslow, peak, plateau, transcendence

Claim: the core of religion is the peak experience of founders and later mystics, which is natural and can be studied. Organised religion tries to preserve it in doctrine and often loses it. Later Maslow separated brief peaks from the calmer, lasting plateau experience, and divided self-actualisers into merely healthy people and transcenders.
Who: Maslow, Toward a Psychology of Being (1962); Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences (1964; 1970 edition adds the plateau); The Farther Reaches of Human Nature (1971); "Theory Z", Journal of Transpersonal Psychology, 1969 [M].
Words: differences between religions are largely local language and myth; the core experience is shared. His later model puts self-transcendence above self-actualisation.
Traditions: founders (Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, Moses), Zen, Sufism.
Tier: [M].
Use: peak against plateau fits "everybody visits, nobody stays". It also gives, in a psychologist's words, Alex's point that a tradition's popular layer misses the inner idea: the institution keeps the form and loses the peak.

### 2.18 Assagioli, psychosynthesis

Claim: the person is an egg-shaped field: lower, middle and higher unconscious, a field of awareness, a conscious I, and a transpersonal Self joined to the I, the I being a reflection of the Self. The egg's boundary is permeable like a cell membrane (psychological osmosis), and outside lies the collective unconscious.
Who: Roberto Assagioli, Psychosynthesis (1965); The Act of Will (1973); Transpersonal Development (1991).
Words: his disidentification exercise has the person say they have a body, feelings and thoughts but are none of them, and find the centre that remains. It resembles neti neti; that is my comparison.
Traditions: Vedanta, raja yoga, Buddhism, Christian mystics, Sufism; Theosophical and Alice Bailey circles [M, weaker; correction 18].
Tier: [M]; osmosis wording [U].
Use: osmosis through a membrane is Alex's Meltdown and Spectre point (the wall leaks). Disidentification is a tidy, checkable letting-go method for Part three.

### 2.19 Eliade, patterns, initiation, return to the origin

Claim: religious phenomena are comparable as patterns: the manifestation of the sacred (hierophany), the world axis, sacred time, the coincidence of opposites (from Nicholas of Cusa). Initiation is everywhere a ritual death of the old person and birth of a new one. Myths often tell of an original unity that broke (heaven and earth parted, the high god withdrew: the deus otiosus) and rites aim to return to the beginning.
Who: Eliade, Patterns in Comparative Religion (French 1949; English 1958); The Myth of the Eternal Return (French 1949; English 1954 under that title, later reissued as Cosmos and History; correction 3); Shamanism (French 1951; English 1964; correction 5); Yoga: Immortality and Freedom (French 1954; English 1958); Birth and Rebirth (1958) [M].
Words: in the yoga book samadhi is enstasis (standing within), not ecstasy, and the liberated yogi holds opposites together beyond time and rebirth. Indian liberation is freedom from the cycle, which differs from the archaic return to the origin through ritual.
Traditions: Hindu yoga and Tantra, shamanism, Greek mysteries, initiation rites worldwide.
Tier: [M].
Use: initiation as death and rebirth describes passing the door, and the deus otiosus is a mythic form of "the whole forgot itself". Indian exit from the cycle is not Alex's return; do not blur them.

### 2.20 Campbell, the monomyth and the cosmogonic cycle

Claim: hero stories worldwide share one pattern in three movements: departure (separation), initiation, return. The crisis of the middle is atonement, read as at-one-ment, in the chapter "Atonement with the Father". The return, bringing the gift back to the community, is the hardest part. At the large scale the same pattern is the cosmogonic cycle: the one emanates into the many and the many dissolve back into the one. In the monomyth proper, "return" means return to the ordinary world, not to the One (correction 14).
Who: Campbell, The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), including Part II on the cosmogonic cycle; The Masks of God (1959 to 1968); The Power of Myth (with Bill Moyers, 1988). Monomyth is a word from Joyce's Finnegans Wake [M].
Words: stages (section titles from memory, not quotations): the call, the first threshold, the belly of the whale, the road of trials, atonement with the father, apotheosis, the ultimate boon, refusal of the return, master of two worlds. Part II chapters, as I recall: Emanations, The Virgin Birth, Transformations of the Hero, Dissolutions [U]. Campbell called myth the penultimate truth, since the ultimate cannot be put into words [recalled; locator unknown; correction 15]. He read Western God-and-man duality as metaphor taken literally, and said mystics who experienced identity (Eckhart and the Sufi al-Hallaj, I believe) were punished for it [naming U].
Traditions: Buddha (enlightenment and hesitation to teach), Jesus, Moses, Osiris, Prometheus, Navajo myth, the Upanishads, the Rigvedic line that the one has many names (Rigveda 1.164.46 [M]).
Tier: [M]; Part II chapter titles [U].
Use: the cosmogonic cycle is the strongest single match to the three clauses, and a pop-culture bridge (Star Wars [M]). The monomyth's return is a weaker match, so cite Part II for the axiom and Part I for the shape of a life. His point about metaphors taken literally supports the claim that the popular layer hides the inner one.

M. H. Abrams (Natural Supernaturalism, 1971) traces the out-and-return pattern (Neoplatonic exitus and reditus) from Plotinus through Christian scholastics to Hegel and the Romantics [M]. ## 4. How they compare: the methods

Wilber builds tables: a column is a level, a row is a tradition (Vedanta, Buddhism, Plotinus, Aurobindo, Teresa), and he fills the cells. Differences are explained by deep against surface structure, and by the claim that a tradition may map some levels well and others thinly, which is where his ranking comes from.

Jung amplifies. A dream or a patient's drawing is set beside parallels in myth, alchemy, religion and folklore until a shared pattern (an archetype) shows. Cultural dress explains the differences.

Eliade uses morphology. Take a theme (sky, water, tree, initiation), gather its appearances across religions regardless of date or place, and look for the structure of the sacred. Differences are historical valuations of one structure.

Campbell abstracts one story from myths of several continents, borrows Bastian's universal elementary ideas against local ethnic ideas, adds Jung's archetypes, and explains difference by culture and by metaphor taken as fact.

Grof sets clinical reports beside traditional maps. Maslow sets interviews beside founders' accounts. Assagioli sorts techniques from many traditions by which part of the person they train.

Common to all: they compare structure and sequence, not doctrine, and place differences in culture, vocabulary or the level at which a tradition speaks. The shared weak spot: the comparer decides what counts as the same.

## 5. What scholars say

Wilber: a system builder with a following, little cited in academic religious studies, self-taught outside the university [M]. Critics (Ferrer, Washburn) say he ranks traditions on a nondual scale taken from Vedanta and Mahayana and leans on contested Western stage theories. His undifferentiated infancy comes from Margaret Mahler, and Daniel Stern's infant research (1985) argues infants have a sense of self earlier [M]. He revised his own early books (correction 21).

Jung: archetypes are hard to test. Harold Coward (Jung and Eastern Thought, 1985) says Jung psychologises Eastern texts; Richard Noll (The Jung Cult, 1994) reads him as a religious founder. Defenders say he kept to empirical claims.

Eliade: Jonathan Z. Smith and Russell McCutcheon criticise the sui generis sacred and say universal patterns erase context. Ethnographers say his shamanism over-generalises from Siberia. His 1930s Romanian political ties are documented, their extent and meaning disputed [M].

Campbell: folklorists say the monomyth fits some stories and ignores others; Maureen Murdock's The Heroine's Journey (1990) answered its male bias; Brendan Gill's 1989 article ("The Faces of Joseph Campbell", New York Review of Books) accused him of conservative and antisemitic leanings, a charge his defenders reject and which is contested [M; correction 17].

Grof: the perinatal scheme is untestable, and memories of birth conflict with what is known of infant memory; Wilber disputes that perinatal states are transpersonal at all. Maslow: small informal samples, weak support for the hierarchy (Wahba and Bridwell, 1976) [M].

Fair summary: useful questions and patterns, no proof. They stand closer to the scholarly mainstream when they say traditions share structure than when they say they share experience.

## 9. The movement is wider than Huxley (added on the person's steer, 2026-10-07)

The person's point: there are plenty of other books in the perennial philosophy movement beyond what Huxley speaks about, and Huxley is not even the best. What this slice can add to what P01 (finding 16), P07 and P08 (section 9) already say, all [M] unless marked:

- Where this slice sits. The psychological comparers (Wilber, Jung, Eliade, Campbell, Grof, Maslow) are one wing. The Traditionalist school (Guenon from 1921, Coomaraswamy from the 1930s, Schuon from 1948) is another and is older than Huxley's book of 1945 in its beginnings, though Schuon's first book (1948) is later than Huxley's [M]. Huxley's anthology is in the middle of the line. Wilber leans on the Traditionalists and on Huston Smith for the "perennial" core of his Great Nest, while rejecting their view of history (correction 11). Eliade wrote on Coomaraswamy ("Some Notes on Theosophia Perennis", 1979, per P03 [C there]); that "Theosophia Perennis" is Eliade's phrase for a divine-wisdom sense of "theosophia" and not a link to the Theosophical Society (P03).
- Comparers who are not in the Traditionalist camp and not Huxley: William James (1902), Evelyn Underhill (1911), Rudolf Otto (1926; English 1932), W. T. Stace (1960), R. C. Zaehner (the best-known critic, 1957), Alan Watts (The Supreme Identity, 1950), Jung and the Eranos circle. They disagree among themselves, and Zaehner and Katz disagree with the whole project.
- Best for what (not a ranking; "best" is a judgement): rigor and source languages, Coomaraswamy and Guenon; the metaphysical argument and why the forms differ, Schuon; a readable map of the whole, Huston Smith (Forgotten Truth, 1976); the big thematic anthology, Whitall Perry (A Treasury of Traditional Wisdom, 1971); the philosophical statement of "same experience, different words", Stace; stages of a path from many traditions, Underhill; the clinical and stage-by-stage mapping, Wilber and Grof; one story for all, Campbell; a door for English readers, Huxley. Titles and years from memory; P08 section 9 carries a fuller list.
- What the wider movement means for Alex's essay. First, name the sources by job and not by one name, and say once that these authors disagree with each other (on rebirth, on method, on Theosophy, on whether history ascends or descends) and that he uses them as witnesses and not as a team. Second, the strongest scholarly objections come from inside the comparative field (Zaehner, Katz, Ferrer), so honesty about them costs little. Third, Theosophy and the perennial philosophy movement met in a few places in this slice (the Quest imprint, Assagioli, Eranos), and the Traditionalists attacked Theosophy (Guenon 1921 and 1923; P01, P02, P08), so Alex's pairing of the two movements is his own and should be presented as such.
