# Part two: Spiritual

## The door

Part one ended on a zoom out. The square we've been living in turned out to be a small box in the corner of a map ten times wider and ten times taller, and this part is about that map. You get into it through the middle of the chart, though, so that's where we start, and I have a confession about the middle.

In Part one I walked you through it more than once and never said what it was. I kept calling it "the middle," which is what you call something when you're saving its name for later.

Where I come from, it's called the door.

There's a word I could have used instead, and you've probably heard it: awakening. I'm going to leave it alone for a boring reason. It has been through too many hands. By now it arrives with a certain tone of voice, some incense and, usually, a link to a course. The door is smaller and more useful. It's a ring around the center of the chart, and it marks the place where a person stops developing by accident and starts developing on purpose.

Developing by accident is how most of us do it. A crash, a loss, a bill that can't be paid, a relative in the hospital: something shoves you toward the middle, you learn a little, and then life calms down and you walk back to your old room. Part one is full of those shoves. What the door adds is a decision. You may well have been pushed to it, but at some point you stop and look at what you've been doing to yourself, and you decide to work on it with both legs, for as long as it takes, and you mean it.

It's a ring and not a single point because a lot of people spend years circling it. They get a bit less driven here, a bit calmer there, and they feel like they're making progress, which they are, in the way that walking around a house is a little like going in. Crossing it is a different move, and it usually takes something bigger than a mood. Part one called that a reason bigger than yourself, and I'll get to mine in a minute.

There's a long tradition of doors in this territory. William Blake wrote that if the doors of perception were cleansed, everything would appear to man as it is, infinite. Aldous Huxley took the line for a book title, and a rock band took it from Huxley. Mine is a smaller door. It's a ring on a chart, and it has never been on an album cover.

## The mirror image

Part one gave you a friend who came off the wall. The door looks different from the other side, so here is a second friend, who came in from the Drifting room.

She spent years on the spiritual road in the full sense: India twice a year, and every method you can think of. Then at some point she decided that all of it was nonsense and that she was never going to make it work, and she did what a lot of drifters do. She swung all the way across. She started a company, threw herself into it, and was done with that whole world for good.

It went about the way Part one would predict. Her inner maturity, as I'd put it, was too high for the Driven room to hold her, and before long she felt the same emptiness that Jim Carrey and Michael Phelps described. Then she got lucky. She went to a talk by our teacher and sensed almost at once that she had gone too far. The answer wasn't the ashram and it wasn't the company. It was the middle, with both of them together. She teaches in our school now.

What I like about her story is the amount of ground in it. She had done a great deal of inner work and then a great deal of outer work, and both were real. The door only showed up on her map when she saw them as two legs of one walk. A founder with failing joints and a seeker who found herself empty inside a company ended up at the same ring from opposite directions, and I find that more convincing than either story would be alone.

## My reason

Part one said that what carries a person across the door is a reason bigger than themselves, and it would be cheap to say that and keep mine to myself. So here are both, the goal and the reason behind the goal, and then you can use the two questions from Part one on me.

The goal is the big map. Not the corner of the chart but the whole square, what I called full human potential at the end of Part one. A friend I teach with, Anna, has the same one, and the way we put it to each other is "for our lifetime," which sounds grand until you notice that a lifetime is the only unit of time anybody gets.

The reason is simpler. I want to be useful, with nothing standing between me and whoever is in front of me. I'd love to say it more impressively, but the plain version is the one that holds up. Useful doesn't mean busy, which is the Driven room's word for it. It means that when someone shows up with a problem, I'm the person who can look at the problem, instead of the person who is mostly managing how it makes me look.

In Part one I gave you two questions for telling a real reason from a counterfeit one. Would you still do it if nobody ever found out? And does it make you gentler with the person in front of you, or does that person turn into material for the cause? I'd like you to use them on me. The first is hard for me to check, since I'm writing this somewhere people can read it. The second one you can check better than I can, so if you catch me treating you as material anywhere in the rest of this essay, say so.

## The edge of the map

Now the part of the goal that sounds a bit crazy. I'll say it plainly and then spend the rest of this part defending it.

Every room on the chart rests on one quiet assumption: that I am a separate thing, a body with a person inside it, and that this thing has to look after itself because nobody else is going to. Everything on the chart is that thing getting better or worse at its job. The assumption has a shape, an outline around the body with me inside it and everything else outside, and I'll call that outline the border.

Default protects it. It braces and complains and keeps its dreams in a drawer, since a dream you never try can't fail. Driven goes out and takes, from the outside, what it can't feel on the inside, which is mostly approval. Drifting steps away from whatever pokes it and calls the stepping away peace. High agency does all of it well. It acts, it's honest and kind, and most of the fear is gone. It's the best version of the separate thing I know how to describe, and the thing is still there. That's why I called the top right corner the peak of the chart, and then told you the chart was a small box.

My goal is to find out first hand whether the assumption is true, and a belief in either direction wouldn't count. People have been telling me for years that it's false, and I've noticed that being told does nothing. I want to see it, the way you see that a room is lit.

If it turns out to be false, a surprising amount of the chart loses its reason to exist. There's nothing left to protect, so nothing to brace against, and nothing to grab. Fear, as I understand it, is the mind's version of the assumption, tension is the body's version, and grabbing is what the two of them make you do. That is what the word enlightenment was pointing at before it got covered in incense, and that's the one time you'll hear it from me.

And you don't vanish. The people I've read about, and the few I've met who live closest to this, seem to do more, since nothing is in the way anymore. Being useful with nothing standing in between is the whole reason I want it.

You can check the body part of this right now, and it takes ten seconds. Make a fist, a hard one, and tense your arm and your shoulder and your jaw while you're at it. Now try to feel glad about something. I'll wait. It's very hard, and I think the reason is that you've just turned into a small, hard object in a world of other objects. I end here, and everything past this point is other. That's the sentence the fist says. Let go of it, and the sentence goes soft.

Everybody has felt the soft version. It's lying back in the water after you finally learn to float: you kick and lift your head and sink, and then one day you stop fighting and the water holds you, and it was holding you the whole time. It's walking into a room full of people who have let go and feeling your body settle before anyone has said a word. It's a yawn going around a table. Calm seems to spread the same way, so I'm not surprised that a room changes when someone further along walks into it.

Part one called the visits flow, the zone, the muse and being in love. For a few hours the border goes soft, nothing outside you has changed, and the world is a friendlier place. I don't know anyone who argues that those hours were fake. What I'm saying is that they show you what the ordinary state is hiding. I read it as separation being something you do, and connection being what's there when you stop doing it.

A warning, from Part one. You can look at the border from a distance and call that seeing through it. That's the cold observer, watching a life from the side and calling it peace, and I don't think the border softens from outside the fence. It's why the line was open your heart, not just your attention.

The things the old books call illusions look to me like what fear draws on the inside of the border: the enemy, the scarcity, the "I'll be happy when." The finish line that kept moving in the Driven room is one of them. If the border goes, they go with it, or so I'm told by people who are further along. I can't verify that from here, and I'll try to be clear as we go about what I can check and what I can't.

## The child in the dark

There's a story I haven't told in public yet, and it's short.

A child lies in a dark room and sees monsters. Sees, in the strong sense: the shapes are as solid as the furniture, the heartbeat is real, and to the child the fear is no metaphor. Then somebody turns on the light, and there's a coat on the door, a pile of laundry and a chair. The room was empty the whole time. It was always safe, and the child just couldn't see it.

Pixar built a whole film on this, and in the film the monsters turn out to be just as frightened of the child. I'd call that the border, seen from both sides.

We do the same thing as adults, all day. We believe what the mind draws in the dark: what's possible for us and what isn't, what other people think of us, what we think of ourselves, what would happen if we said the sentence out loud. Every room on the chart is furniture arranged around those pictures, and the border, "I stop at my skin," is the biggest picture of all.

I should be careful here, because this is a story where it's easy to overreach. Nothing in it means that nothing can hurt you. Buses exist. The claim is smaller: most of what we're braced against is pictures, pictures are cheap to produce and expensive to carry, and when the light comes on, what arrives is safety, plain and ordinary. For a lot of the pictures it's the first real safety you've had, because the thing you were guarding against was never in the room.

That's the goal, said as small as I can say it: to live with the light on. Not to visit for a weekend and go back to the dark. I'd be an unreliable narrator if I told you it's on for me all day.

## What the lab can say

I can't test whether the assumption is true, and I'm not sure what the test would look like. Psychology can test something smaller: how solid the feeling of a border is, and what happens to people when it softens. That's the part of the claim that has data, so here it is, with the weak spots marked, the way I did it in Part one.

Start with the border itself. In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen sat people at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand in plain view, and stroked both with a brush at the same moment. Most people felt the touch in the rubber hand, and a lot of them said it felt like their own. Later studies timed it, and for most people it takes well under a minute. This is an excellent thing to do to a stranger at a party and a poor way to make friends. A video version followed in 2007: people watched their own back from behind while it was stroked in time with their real back, and many felt that the body in front of them was theirs and that they were standing closer to it (Lenggenhager and colleagues). The philosopher Thomas Metzinger builds a whole theory on results like these. His version is that the felt self is a model the brain builds of the body and then can't see as a model (Metzinger, 2003; Blanke and Metzinger, 2009).

Two caveats. What moves in these experiments is the felt ownership of a hand or the felt location of a body, which is a lot less than the whole sense of "me." And how strongly people report the illusion goes with how suggestible they are (Lush and colleagues, 2020), so part of what the questionnaire catches is expectation. It's a real effect with a soft measure attached. What it gives me is the smaller claim that I need: the border of the body, as you feel it, is something the brain works out from the senses, and it can be nudged. It doesn't show that the self is an illusion in any grand sense.

Now the other direction: what happens when the border softens by itself. Psychologists have a name for it. Self-transcendent experiences are brief states in which the sense of self fades and the feeling of connection grows, and on one influential account the category includes awe, flow, mystical-type experiences and some states in meditation (Yaden and colleagues, 2017). That paper proposes the category and tests nothing itself. It does put one useful thing on the record: people in very different settings report this, and the reports have a recognizable shape.

Does a softer border make people kinder? Some evidence says yes, with caveats. In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how much people said they would help someone tracked how "one" they felt with that person, and once that was counted, plain sympathy added nothing. Other researchers dispute that reading. In a small experiment in 2013, about half of the people who had taken an eight-week meditation course gave up their seat to a stranger on crutches, against about one in six on a wait-list (Condon and colleagues). That's 39 people and a single yes or no, and the wait-list group got no class at all, so I wouldn't hang much on it. A 2018 review of randomized trials found that meditation training raises compassion modestly, and the effect shrank when the comparison group was also given something to do, and when the teacher was not an author of the paper (Kreplin and colleagues). The direction holds. The size is smaller than the headlines.

Awe has a similar story. A 2015 paper reported that people who had just felt awe, among very tall trees for example, felt smaller and acted more generously (Piff and colleagues). Some later attempts to reproduce experiments of this kind have not succeeded, so treat that one as open. A 2024 review of 38 papers still finds that people who feel more awe tend to behave more prosocially (Li and colleagues), which is a correlation, though a steady one.

Then there's the brain. A small Yale study in 2011 scanned 12 experienced meditators and 13 beginners while they meditated. The experienced group showed less activity in regions linked to mind wandering and self-related thinking (Brewer and colleagues). That's 25 people, nobody was assigned to meditate, and the network in question is sometimes called "the self" in headlines, which is a bigger claim than the paper makes. It shows a difference and says nothing about cause. Part one had one more piece of this, a review of 226 effect sizes finding that being turned inward on yourself goes with a worse mood (Mor and Winquist, 2002).

One more thing, for the lab-minded. Psychologists have walked off the edge of their own charts before. Abraham Maslow, whose pyramid everyone has seen, wrote in his last years about a kind of living that goes beyond self-actualization, which he called transcendence (Koltko-Rivera, 2006, lays out the later Maslow). The familiar pyramid stops short of it, partly because Maslow never drew a pyramid. A later paper traced the shape to management writing of the late 1950s (Bridgman and colleagues, 2019). Viktor Frankl, who was in Part one, said it from the other side: self-actualization shows up as a side effect of self-transcendence. The map has been drawn past its own corner before, by people with better credentials than mine.

Here is what none of this shows. It doesn't show that the separate self is false in the sense the old books mean, that anyone has reached the far side of the map, or that the far side exists. The lab can watch the border move a little, and it can watch people get a little kinder when it softens. It can't see the room with the light on. For that I have to hand you back to your own experience, and to the people who have been walking longer.

## Names for the far side

People have been writing about this for far longer than psychology has existed, and they have given it a lot of names. Samadhi is a Sanskrit word for deep, steady absorption, in which the mind stops its usual chatter and the sense of a separate watcher loosens. Nirvana is the Buddhist word for the going out of the fires that keep suffering going, usually named as greed, hatred and confusion. Moksha is the Hindu word for liberation, release from the cycle of rebirth and from the ignorance that keeps a person bound to it. Satori is the Japanese Zen word for sudden insight, a moment of waking up to how things are. In Sufism, fana is the passing away of the ego's sense of being the centre of things, and baqa is the lasting that follows, a return to ordinary life with that recognition intact.

Christianity has its own set. In Eastern Orthodoxy, theosis is the lifelong process of coming to share in the life of God by grace, without ceasing to be a creature, and its best known anchor is the phrase "partakers of the divine nature" in 2 Peter 1:4. Then there's Luke 17:21, where the kingdom of God is "within you" in the old King James and "among you" or "in your midst" in most newer Bibles, because the Greek can mean either. I'd love it to be "within," since that suits this essay better, which is exactly why I'm leaving it to the translators. The Gospel of Thomas, a collection of sayings from the second century found in Egypt in 1945 and not part of the Bible, has Jesus say that the kingdom is inside you and outside you. In Genesis, Jacob dreams of a ladder set up on the earth with its top reaching to heaven, and wakes up calling the place the gate of heaven, which for an essay with a door in it I'm obviously going to count.

The list goes on. In Daoism the Dao is the Way, the unnameable source and pattern of how things go, and wu wei, often translated as effortless action, means acting in tune with that pattern without strain or forcing. Teachers in several traditions, most often in Vedanta, picture a person as a wave and the whole as the ocean, and "ocean of consciousness" is a modern English phrase for that old image. And since this is a list of names from many traditions, I owe you the one most of us learned first: Stairway to Heaven, Led Zeppelin, November 1971, the one song that Wayne's World established guitar shops would rather you didn't play.

I'm not claiming that these say the same thing, because they don't. In 1978 the scholar Steven Katz argued that mystical experiences are shaped by the tradition of the person having them, so they shouldn't be assumed to be the same everywhere. Aldous Huxley argued the opposite in The Perennial Philosophy in 1945, saying that mystics of many religions point at one underlying reality and differ mainly in the words they use. As far as I can tell, most scholars now sit somewhere in between.

My claim is smaller. Several contemplative traditions treat the ordinary sense of being a separate, sealed-off "I" as the main thing to see through, though they disagree about what is left. Buddhism says no fixed self can be found, Advaita Vedanta says the true Self is the whole, and Sufi and Christian writers speak of the self being emptied so that God can fill it. That's three different answers to a question about what the border is made of, and I'm only borrowing the question.

Several traditions also separate a first opening from a settled realization and treat the first as the start of the work. Zen speaks of kensho followed by long practice, early Buddhism calls stream entry the first of four stages, and Sufis speak of fana followed by baqa. That's the door and the far corner of my chart, give or take. I'd rather say "give or take" than claim that the traditions agree with my drawing.

The same goes for what happens afterwards. Several traditions describe the end of the path as a return to ordinary life and service. In Mahayana Buddhism the bodhisattva stays in the world to help others, the last of the Zen ox herding pictures shows the realized person walking back into the marketplace, and Teresa of Avila wrote that the purpose of spiritual marriage with God is good works. There's also a saying that circulates in Zen circles, in several versions, that you chop wood and carry water before the big realization and you chop wood and carry water after it. It echoes a verse by a Tang dynasty layman named Pang, who called drawing water and carrying firewood his supernatural power. I prefer the Tang version, because it has the better line, and because it matches what I said earlier: nobody in these stories vanishes. They get more done.

## How you would know

If this is real, it should leave marks you can check, so here are the ones I know. The first set is on the chart and you've met them: how many options you see in a hard moment, how long a reaction lasts, how quickly you say sorry first, and when you last did something nobody asked for, for somebody else's good.

The second set comes from the school I belong to, where people have been watching this change for a long time. It tends to arrive in an order. First a person starts to listen, really listen, as attention leaves the self it was protecting and lands on somebody else for the first time. Then giving shows up: time, attention, help, offered without being asked and without keeping score. Then perception clears, like a dirty window being wiped, because someone who no longer feels under siege doesn't need to find threats everywhere. That's what teachers in the school describe, and I've seen versions of it in people around me. It's observation and not a study, and I'd weigh it that way.

What I like about it is the measure. It isn't how much you know or how many years you've practiced. It's two questions: has giving appeared, and how clearly do you see? Both are ways of asking how much of the person is still on guard.

## Three things people ask me

The first is usually some version of: isn't that just being a vegetable, feeling nothing? The worry is reasonable, because there are people who look like this and are numb, and you met that version in Part one. Feeling is one channel. The same lock that keeps the anger and the tears down keeps the joy down with them, and you can't dam half a river. The person who cuts the channel to feel less pain feels less of everything, which is anesthesia. Take the border away instead and everything comes through, the hard things included. The volume goes up.

The second is: why would anyone want that? If the answer is "for the experience," I'd be careful. The people chasing the experience are the ones I've watched hit the plateau from Part one, where nothing is changing and it all looks like a waste of time. A better way to ask it is what you'd do if nothing felt impossible and nothing needed defending. Then do that, all day, for other people. That's the reason I gave you earlier, and it's the one Part one said carries people across.

The third is: how do you know it exists? I don't, not the way I know Paris exists. I know the direction, because each step toward it is measurable on this chart. I've also met people who are further along, and a room settles when they walk in. Beyond that, I'd rather spend my life walking toward something I'm not sure of than sitting in a room I'm sure I hate.

So the honest summary is that I don't know if I'll get there, and I know the direction.

## Where this goes next

That's the map as far as I can draw it honestly. What I haven't told you is how to walk it, and that's the next part, which has a lot less philosophy in it and a lot more schedule. The short version is that it uses stillness and work together, every day. The work shows you where you're still braced, the stillness lets it go, and the energy that comes back goes into the work. That alternation is the shrinking swing from Part one, put on a timetable.

Until then, here is a small experiment that needs no equipment. Next time you catch yourself braced, make the fist on purpose, hold it for ten seconds and let go. Then ask where the border of you is sitting right now. Don't quote me, but I'd bet it isn't where you think.
