# Part two: Spiritual

## Where we part ways

Part one was the polite part. Everything in it holds up whichever way you think the universe works. This part is where we part ways.

One thing first, because it changes how you read everything after it. I'm not religious, and I'm not asking you to adopt anybody's religion. What I will do is quote a lot of traditions: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Jewish, Christian, Muslim, Daoist, Greek, and a few people with no religion at all. They use different words, and sometimes different gods, and they keep coming back to a few of the same ideas. I'm using them as witnesses.

One more thing, about how I'll argue. A world, real or invented, has to be coherent within its own rules. The rules can be as odd as you like, and what makes the world believable is that everything that happens in it follows from them. This is the test I'll run on the picture of reality in this part. Take one assumption about how things are, and ask whether everything else follows from it without exceptions. You don't have to agree with the starting assumption for the test to work. Coherence is the first test, and your own life is the second, so the end of this part has a check and a prediction you can test on yourself.

Two ways of reading a life sit at opposite ends. Most people stand somewhere in between without noticing.

The first starts from observation. Look around and you see a world of things, and underneath them particles, or quantum fields if you want the physicist's answer. I look in a mirror, see one of those things, and feel like a person: me in here, everybody else out there. From that you get an assumption, that matter is the basic stuff, that a mind is something a brain does, and that consciousness depends on that activity. Follow it through and you get materialism, and often determinism, the idea that earlier causes fix everything that happens. When the brain stops, the experiencer stops. This view built hospitals and phones, and I live inside it for most of my day. In my body it feels like the plain truth. Me, Alex, a separate entity. Hello.

The second view sits at the other extreme, and it starts from a different assumption: that we are all one. Everything is part of a greater whole, and that whole is experiencing separation through the individual experience we call our lives. On this reading I'm not a thing inside the world. I'm the world, looking at itself from one position, with the memory of the rest switched off for a while.

Both of these are assumptions. Nobody has observed that matter is all there is, because every observation was made through the same mind we're trying to explain. The first view predicts well, and the working assumption that experience depends on brain activity has strong evidence behind it. What nobody has is an accepted account of why that activity feels like anything from the inside, which the philosopher David Chalmers called the hard problem in 1995. The second view finds experience easy, since it is the basic ingredient, and takes on the opposite question: if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing? That question is most of this part.

## Why this idea is worth a closer look

Here's why I think the second view deserves the effort. People were saying it long before anyone had a long weekend in the mountains, and something close to it turns up in each of the big religions. Hindu texts say it outright. Judaism, Christianity and Islam say it in their mystical layers and in a few plain verses, Daoism says that the many come from one, and Buddhism and Jainism come at it from another side.

#### The base, tradition by tradition

* Hinduism: in the Chandogya Upanishad, one of India's oldest philosophical texts, a father tells his son Svetaketu that the finest essence is the self of the whole world, and that he is that (6.8.7).
* Buddhism comes at it from another side. The early discourses teach that none of the five parts that make up a person is my self, and later schools say that nothing exists on its own. The Huayan school of Chinese Buddhism pictured that as a net of jewels in which each jewel reflects all the others.
* Jainism, the Indian religion of non-harm, starts from the idea that souls are alike in nature, and its ethic rests on that likeness. Its oldest conduct text, the Acharanga, tells the reader that whoever you intend to kill or torment feels as you would, and the Tattvartha Sutra, a standard Jain summary of the faith, says that souls render service to one another (5.21).
* Judaism: the Shema, the central prayer, says that the Lord is one (Deuteronomy 6:4), and Chabad, a Hasidic movement of Jewish mysticism, reads that unity in its founding book, the Tanya, as including the world: from God's own point of view the world is as nothing before him, while it is real from ours.
* Christianity: Jesus prays in John that his followers may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you (17:21), and Paul ends his account of the end with God being all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28).
* Islam: the Quran says that wherever you turn there is the face of God (2:115), and the influential Sufi (Muslim mystic) Ibn Arabi, whose followers took the idea furthest, called all things "He/not He," meaning that each thing is God and also not God.
* Daoism: in the Daodejing the Way gives birth to one, one gives birth to two, and the sequence runs on to the ten thousand things, the Chinese phrase for everything that exists (42).

People with no creed have said it too. Spinoza argued that there is only one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that everything else is a form of it. When a rabbi cabled Einstein in 1929 to ask whether he believed in God, Einstein said he believed in Spinoza's God, meaning the lawful order of nature, nothing that cares what we do. Erwin Schrödinger argued that the plurality of minds is only apparent and tied that to the Upanishads. And Carl Sagan, about as far from a mystic as a famous person gets, said on television in 1980 that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself.

These people don't hold exactly the view I'll use, and they share the base: what looks like many is not as separate as it looks. I'll take that base as the axiom and follow what comes out of it. If it's right, the ideas people usually file under "spiritual" come out as logical consequences, one after another. The traditions got there from many directions. Here the same ideas are laid out in the order they follow from one assumption.

## The axiom and the machine

The axiom has three clauses.

We are one. There is a single whole, and everything is in it.

The whole experiences separation. It looks at itself through individual lives, each one a position with its own point of view, which each of us experiences as "my life."

The separation ends. Being separate is a phase, and it returns to knowing itself as one.

The first two clauses describe how things are. The third says where they go, and it is the one the rest of this part leans on most.

I'll hold it with a computer, because Part one admitted that I spent years in cyber security, so I can't help it. Picture one machine running thousands of programs. Each program gets its own private memory and believes it has the whole thing to itself. The operating system maps all of it onto the same physical chips, and the hardware enforces the walls, which is what keeps a random app away from your password manager. The walls are real, though engineers built them, and in 2018 the Meltdown and Spectre flaws showed that information could leak across. Underneath, there is one machine.

Now picture the screen. Two windows sit in opposite corners, and there seems to be a distance between them. There is, on the screen. Both windows are computed on the same machine in the same moment, and the machine has never heard of that distance.

Under the axiom, you and I are programs. "Me over here, you over there" is the screen. My private memory is the self I feel from inside. The wall around it is the felt edge of me. The whole is the machine that was running both of us all along. The computer is a picture. It doesn't mean the universe is a simulation or that anyone is running it.

From these three clauses I want to build the rest one step at a time. Each step uses a clause of the axiom, an earlier step, or both, and in a few places I add something I have observed, and I'll say where. Under each step I'll show you what the traditions say, so you can judge for yourself whether they meant the same thing. Here is the whole chain on one page.

## 1. Separation is a way of looking

If everything is one, and the whole experiences separation, then being separate can't be a fact about what exists. It has to be a fact about how the whole is looking at itself. So the separation is real as an experience and misleading as a final description, and that's what I mean by illusion. The word gets misused, so let me be careful: it doesn't mean nothing is there. The distance between the two windows is real, and you can measure it with a ruler held to the screen. What's wrong is the reading, the thought that two separate things sit that far apart inside the machine. The walls between programs work the same way. From inside a program they are completely real, and they still don't mean the machine is in pieces.

Why would a whole do this at all? The traditions give answers, and they rhyme. Hindu texts call creation lila, play, an activity with no need behind it. A whole that looks at itself needs positions to look from, and a position is a separation. Knowing needs a gap.

Physics is a fun place to look here, though it comes with a warning label. For a few centuries the picture was easy. The world is made of tiny separate balls, atoms and electrons and so on, each one a little thing with its own properties, and everything else is those balls in different arrangements. Quantum field theory, the framework behind the Standard Model of particle physics, says something different. In it there are no tiny balls to begin with. There are fields, which you can think of as something that fills all of space, a bit like the ocean fills a bay. What we call an electron is a ripple in the electron field, and a photon is a ripple in the electromagnetic field. The ripple has a place and some energy, and we count it as a particle, the same way you might count waves. But a wave is not a thing separate from the water. It is the water doing something.

This also explains something odd. Every electron in the universe is exactly the same as every other electron, with no differences to find. A factory can't make two things this identical. If every electron is a ripple in the same field, they match for the same reason two waves in the same bay are made of the same water.

Fields are not the only strange thing. Take two particles prepared together and send them far apart. Measure them, and the results match up more strongly than any pair of private, pre-set answers could allow. John Bell showed in 1964 how to test that, the loopholes were closed by 2015, and the 2022 Nobel Prize went to the people who ran the tests. The pair behaves as one thing with a joint state, even when the two ends are far apart. It carries no message, so please don't use it to explain your cousin's premonition.

So I'm not claiming this proves that we are one. What I take from it is this. For centuries the picture was lots of separate things. The best physics we have now says that picture is wrong at the bottom. The many things turn out to be one world doing something in different places, and the most ambitious theories hope it comes down to a single kind of thing. That is the shape of the axiom, and I like that it turned up in physics without anyone asking it to.

#### Real as an experience, misleading as the last word

* Hinduism calls the appearance maya, and teachers of Advaita, the non-dual school of Hindu philosophy, warn that "illusion" translates the word badly: the world depends on something deeper for its being, so it is neither fully real nor simply unreal. Plato's prisoners in the cave take shadows for the world, and in the Heart Sutra, a short Buddhist text, form is emptiness and emptiness is form, which Buddhists read as saying that things have no fixed independent nature of their own.
* The Brahma Sutra (2.1.33), a classic Hindu text on the Upanishads, is where creation is called lila. Sufis quote a saying, "I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known," which scholars of hadith, the reported sayings of the Prophet Muhammad, do not accept as his. Its logic is the one I just gave: a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view.
* Ralph Waldo Emerson, who had left the pulpit, wrote in The Over-Soul in 1841 that we live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles, and that meanwhile within man is the soul of the whole.

## 2. Why we forget

Step 1 leaves a question hanging. If everything is one, why would the whole ever lose track of that? My answer is that the forgetting is no accident and no flaw. It is built into existence itself, on purpose, as part of that play.

Start with inertia. A moving object keeps moving until something pushes it, and a resting one stays put. Life in separation behaves the same way. A being that doesn't turn toward unity keeps experiencing disunity, and the experience feeds itself. Every day brings more experience that seems to confirm the view that I'm separate: a body that ends at the skin, a mind nobody else can read, a world full of things apart from each other. Each one makes the next easier to believe, and the wall grows thicker.

The body keeps the loop running. Clench a fist and you feel like a small hard object in a world of other objects, and when you let go that feeling softens. The belief that I'm separate is held as tension, and the tension keeps confirming the belief.

Attention works the same way. Left alone, it goes to whatever is in front of you, and what is in front of you is the world of forms. So without taking control of it, you drift further into that world, immersed, and you fall asleep to the rest. There is a saying that light is attractive but darkness is contagious. Attraction means you have to make an effort to move toward something. Contagion means you catch it by standing still. The darkness here is the pull of habit and distraction, and it works on everyone, me included.

Why would a whole build this in? Because a forgetting that lasted an afternoon wouldn't be worth anything. Step 1 said that knowing needs a gap. The gap also has to stay open long enough for a life to be lived, for something to be learned, and for it to be brought back. This is the lila of step 1 taken seriously: a game with real stakes can't be one you can quit by accident.

That has a consequence for everything that follows. Nothing returns by itself. Unity won't arrive as the default, because the default is the pull toward separation. Getting out takes a decision, and not just once: the same decision, made again in every situation that comes. The steps that follow describe that process. Step 5 shows how a single life learns what separation costs, and step 6 shows how the push against the inertia begins.

#### A default that can be undone

* The traditions describe the wall as built, veiled or forgotten. Buddhism says the self is built. What feels like one solid "me" is described as a bundle of parts: body, feelings, perceptions, intentions and habits, and awareness, working together, and the first thing the path asks you to drop is the belief that there is a solid, permanent "me" behind them.
* Jain teaching describes karma as fine matter that veils the soul's own natural knowledge, as clouds veil the sun, so the perfection was there all along.
* The letter to the Ephesians says that the non-Jewish world its readers left behind is alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them (4:18). In the Quran (7:172) God asks the descendants of Adam, "Am I not your Lord?", and Sufis read that moment as the origin the soul has forgotten and longs to return to.
* The Sanskrit word for this heaviness is tamas, the quality of inertia. The Bhagavad Gita (14.8) says it is born of ignorance, deludes all embodied beings, and binds them through heedlessness, laziness and sleep.
* The language of sleep is everywhere. Buddha means the awakened one. The letter to the Ephesians quotes what looks like an early hymn: "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead" (5:14).

## 3. One life is too short

So far that was the second clause, the separation, and what holds it up. The third clause says the separation ends. It doesn't say how, and here I add one reading of my own: the return is something each of us completes, from experiencing the world as many to experiencing it as one. One life is plainly too short for that. Most of us hold the wall up for a whole life without noticing, and even the few who reach the top right corner are, by the standard of the axiom, standing at the door of the next map. So if the return is real for each of us, it takes more than one lifetime. The idea is older than I am: near the end of The Education of the Human Race, in 1780, Lessing, a thoroughly Enlightenment sort of person, asks why he shouldn't come back as often as he is capable of acquiring fresh knowledge. How the next stretch looks, in a new body or in some other form, is where the traditions differ.

#### Further lives, or growth that continues

* In the Bhagavad Gita, the Hindu scripture in which the god Krishna advises the warrior Arjuna, he says that the yogi who keeps striving is perfected through many births and then reaches the highest goal (6.45). The Buddha says that samsara, the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, has no discoverable beginning and that it is not easy to find a being who has not at some time been your mother (Samyutta Nikaya 15, in one of the oldest collections of his talks).
* Jain teaching has each soul reborn until it is freed, Sikh scripture teaches rebirth with liberation by grace, and the Kabbalists, the Jewish mystics, taught gilgul, the cycling of the soul through new bodies.
* The three big Western religions mostly settled on one life, then resurrection and judgment. Even so, the story doesn't stop at death for them: the Catholic Church teaches purgatory, a purification after death, and Gregory of Nyssa, a fourth-century Church Father, taught that the soul's progress toward God never ends, because God is infinite.

Either way, development continues, which is all the axiom needs.

## 4. Something carries over

And if the run continues, something has to carry what was learned from one stretch to the next. In the traditions that use the word, that's the soul. Buddhism holds that there is no fixed soul and still says that something continues, and an old Buddhist dialogue has the image I like most for it: a lamp lit from another lamp, where nothing passes across and the second one still burns. In the machine it would be the saved state. The program shuts down, the window closes, and the session is gone with most of its detail. What carries over is the account, the part of the experience that was worth keeping. As I read the axiom, it builds up from one life to the next until the soul, or whatever you prefer to call it, is ready for the next step.

That leaves the question of why nobody remembers, and the answer I keep coming back to is a story Alan Watts used to tell. Watts was the British writer who spent his life explaining Eastern philosophy to Western audiences, and the talk is usually called "The Dream of Life." Suppose that every night you could dream any dream you wanted, and pack seventy-five years of experience into a single night. The first nights you would have every pleasure you could think of. After enough of those you would get tired of always knowing how it goes, so one night you would dream something you don't control, where things happen to you that you didn't plan. Each night you would hide a little more from yourself and take a bigger gamble, because the not knowing is where the excitement is. Keep going, Watts says, and eventually you would dream the life you are living today.

That's the second clause of the axiom told as a story. There is one dreamer under every dream, and a dream like that only works if the dreamer forgets. A life that still remembered being one with everything, or remembered every life before it, would hardly be separate at all. It would be the whole wearing a costume and knowing it. So in this picture the forgetting belongs to the design, the same one step 2 described: it is what makes the dream worth having, and what lets each life bring home something no other point of view could. It also means memory can't settle the question in either direction. If the picture is wrong there is nothing to remember, and if it's right the design hides it, so I don't count the missing memories in its favor. The checks at the end of this part are where I'd look instead.

There's a fair question here: if there is only one mind, who carries over? The Advaita answer is that individuality belongs to the level of appearance, which in the machine is the process, and the processor underneath is the same for everyone. So there are two pictures, a saved state for the process and a single witness for the processor, and each is true at its own level.

#### What carries over

* In the Gita, Krishna compares the self to a person putting on new clothes and discarding worn ones (2.22). In the Myth of Er that closes Plato's Republic, souls choose their next lives and then drink from the river of forgetfulness before they are born, which is one old answer to why you don't remember the last one.
* On the question of who carries over, Advaita points the other way. In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad the Self is the witness in every being, and there is no other witness but him (3.7.23). Gaudapada, an early teacher of Advaita, goes a step further and says that from the highest standpoint nobody is bound and nobody is freed (Mandukya Karika 2.32).
* The lamp comes from the Milindapanha, a dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nagasena, where the one who is reborn is neither the same nor another, like a lamp that burns all night without being the same flame from one hour to the next. The Tibetan Book of the Dead guides the dying through the bardo, the in-between, though Buddhist schools disagree about whether such a state exists.

Inside the axiom this follows, with one added assumption: that what continues is a thread of this particular run, which gathers the essence of each life as it goes, and the whole's general record is only part of it. The best-known research on it is Ian Stevenson's at the University of Virginia, continued by Jim Tucker: over 2,500 cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives. The cases are debated. Many of the best-known cases come from places where rebirth is part of the culture, and in many the two families had already been in contact before anyone recorded the child's statements, which gives critics ordinary explanations. Stevenson checked what details he could against records of the person the child named. Most people who hold this view hold it on the strength of experience, and an essay can't hand you the experience.

## 5. Karma, or the fingers

If we are one, there is nobody else. Everyone I could harm is part of the whole I belong to. Hold up your hand. If the index finger and the middle finger start fighting, pressing and scratching, they hurt the hand, and the hand is the only thing either of them can be a part of. Nobody had to appoint a judge.

Most of us have felt this from the inside at least once. You see a small child about to fall into a well, and you feel a jolt of alarm before you have thought anything. Nobody decided to care about that child. Wang Yangming, the Chinese philosopher, used this very example in 1527, and for him the jolt is what forming one body with the child feels like from the inside. For a second the wall isn't there.

This is, I think, the real meaning of what people call karma. The word is Sanskrit for action, and in everyday use it has turned into a ledger kept somewhere, with a judge and a sentence. The version I was taught has no judge. What happens to you works like a mirror. How you think and what you feel come back to you, and they come back right away, in the same life. Someone irritates you, something hurts, and that shows you a place in yourself that hasn't been mended yet. The mirror only shows, and it does nothing else. The machine has a picture for it too: a program that corrupts shared memory reads the corrupted data back and has nobody to blame, because it was the same memory all along. Over a life, this is how the mirror teaches the thing that matters most here: living in separation costs something, and sooner or later that makes a person want out.

The mirror also picks its material on purpose. The sharpest lessons tend to come from the people closest to you, family, children, parents, because that is where it hurts most. So the work starts on my own side. When I stop reacting the old way, the other person stops getting the old reaction, and very often the whole pattern between us shifts, which is what you'd expect if we are parts of one whole. That never means staying where someone keeps hurting me. Old and heavy things take time, and I may have to come back to them more than once. And this is no reason to wait. If someone close to me is sick or in trouble and I say it's their karma, I've stopped helping, and the word for that is indifference. The better reaction is to make what is happening better, because something in me is part of it too.

None of this is punishment. A system built to teach doesn't punish anyone, and it doesn't close a lesson before the lesson is learned. The people who teach it this way add that, seen from far enough up, there is no karma at all, only the work in front of you. Until you get there, it is a useful name for the pull of what you haven't mended. Living consciously and working on something bigger than yourself evens it out over time.

Many traditions stretch the mirror across lifetimes. In the machine picture, the thread that carries over from step four keeps meeting what it left unmended, and by the time it sees the whole it has stood in the place of everyone it once called other. That is my reading of the axiom, and it goes beyond what I was taught. If you take it up, take it as a reason to be kind, and never as an explanation for why someone else is in pain.

#### Deeds come back, and the other is me

* The traditions differ on who administers karma. In the Buddhist account nobody does: the Buddha said it is intention he calls karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63). In Advaita, karma belongs to the world of apparent individuals.
* The shape turns up elsewhere without any rebirth. Paul writes that whatever one sows, that will he also reap (Galatians 6:7), and the Quran says that if you do good, you do good for yourselves, and if you do evil, you do it to yourselves (17:7).
* The image of the fingers is old too. Paul says the church is a body with many members, and that if one member suffers, all suffer together (1 Corinthians 12:26). Shantideva, the Indian Buddhist, wrote in his Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life that the hand and the other limbs are many and distinct, yet all are one, and that beings are in the same way all one in wanting happiness (Bodhicaryavatara 8.91).
* The golden rule is the same logic read forwards: if the other is me, treating them as I want to be treated stops being a nicety and becomes consistency. Hillel, asked to teach the whole Torah while standing on one foot, is reported to have said: what is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. That is the whole Torah, and the rest is commentary (Shabbat 31a, in the Talmud). Most versions rest on reciprocity or empathy, but the Isha Upanishad goes further: the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, recoils from nothing.
* People with no creed get there too. Marcus Aurelius, who is in Part one as well, tells himself that difficult people are his kin, since we are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. Schopenhauer built his ethics on compassion, the recognition in another person of the same being you are, and put it in the Sanskrit formula tat tvam asi, that art thou. And Wang Yangming, whose child at the well is in the text above, wrote in the same passage that the great person forms one body with heaven, earth and all things.

One warning. The easy version of karma is blame: she had it coming, he reaped what he sowed. That's spiritual bypassing with a calculator. The axiom doesn't support it, because the person you're blaming is as much a part of the whole as you are. The version that follows is responsibility: what in me is this showing, and what's my next move toward the part of myself I've been fighting?

## 6. Coming home, and repair

So what takes the wall down, and mends the harm that went with it? Step 2 says nothing returns by itself, so the answer has to be an act. Punishment can't be it, because that would only be one more finger hitting another. What works is turning back toward what you were cut off from, which many traditions call home.

The wall is built over a lifetime, out of our ideas about ourselves and others, and it is kept up every day by how a separate self behaves. It takes, it controls, it treats other people as other, and its attention drifts to the world of forms. Hitting the wall head on makes it stronger. What thins it is behaving the way the axiom says things are, against the default, until experience agrees. Each of the three moves undoes one way the wall is kept up.

The first is to mend the cause on your own side. Treating a part of the whole as other is what built the wall, and the mirror in step 5 shows where. So the sorry, the first step in a conflict and the hard conversation, which in Part one's terms is inner agency, amount to acting as one in the very spot where I acted as two. The second is to turn toward the source and ask. If I am a part of the whole, the connection was never cut, only veiled, so I don't have to build it, only turn toward it. Attention won't go there by default, so it has to be turned on purpose. You say what you want, you say thanks, you open your heart, and you give your attention as a gift and don't take with it, because taking is what a separate self does and giving is what a part does when it acts for the whole. People who do this describe an answer, often a warm one, and some compare it to how things felt in childhood. The third carries that same giving from attention into action: take on something bigger than yourself, together with the people around you, at home or at work. A shared task is where giving becomes visible, because the whole acts as one body and nobody is working for themselves alone.

Letting go falls out of all three. The wall is held in the body as tension (step 2), and each move gives the body evidence that it isn't needed, so it loosens. When you stop controlling, the present moment gets deeper and home feels closer. Still, letting go is the doorway, and the house is the connection you keep returning to. That's also why you can't argue a person out of the feeling of separation. A body that holds a belief as tension needs evidence, and the evidence is the experience of turning back.

#### Stop holding, and turn back

* Hasidism calls it bittul, the nullification of the self, and the Baal Shem Tov, the founder of Hasidism, read a verse in Psalm 16 as teaching equanimity, so that praise and insult feel the same. The Sufis speak of fana, the passing away of the self in God, followed by baqa, living on in God.
* Christians have kenosis, Greek for emptying, their name for the emptying Paul writes about in his letter to the Philippians (2:5-8). There he says that Jesus, though he was in the form of God, did not treat equality with God as something to hold on to, and emptied himself, taking the form of a servant. Paul offers this as the attitude his readers should share. The medieval German preacher Meister Eckhart's word for letting go was gelazenheit.
* In the Buddha's instructions on breathing, one step is to breathe in and out contemplating relinquishment (Majjhima Nikaya 118). In the Sallatha Sutta he says that an untrained person feels two pains, the physical one and the mental one added on top, while a trained disciple feels only the first.
* Repair has its own rules. The Mishnah, an early Jewish law code, says that Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, atones for sins between a person and God, but for sins against another person only once that person has been appeased (Yoma 8:9). In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says that if you remember at the altar that your brother has something against you, you should leave your gift there and first go and be reconciled (Matthew 5:23-24). The words for repentance point the same way: the Hebrew teshuvah comes from the verb shuv, to return (it is also the everyday word for an answer), the Arabic tawba comes from a root for turning back, and the Greek metanoia means a change of mind.

## 7. The return, in stages

Put the chain together and you get a path: turn back toward home, repair the fingers, and over many lives come back to seeing the whole as one. What the chain gives me is that the return is gradual and that it ends. The picture below shows how far it can go. Part one's chart reads differently from here.

Every room on the chart is a separate program trying to run well. Default protects itself. Driven goes out and takes, from the outside, what it can't feel on the inside. Drifting steps away from whatever pokes it and calls the stepping away peace. High agency runs well: it acts with honesty and kindness, and fear no longer makes the decisions. That is the small square, the area available to a separate self when it gets as good as it can get at being separate, which is a full life by any human measure. At the top of the small square the strain is mostly gone. What is left is the assumption that there is a wall to be behind, and nobody has questioned that yet.

The big square, the zoomed-out map from the end of Part one, is something else. It begins when the separation itself stops being assumed. You can optimize a program for years and it stays a program, behind its own wall. At some point the question stops being how to run better and becomes whether the wall is real. In my field, cyber security, that move is called a sandbox escape, a program breaking out of the walled-off space it was locked into. It makes a researcher famous for a week and ruins the vendor's afternoon. It also explains why Part one asked for a reason bigger than you: a reason about you can't carry you out, because the reason is the thing you're trying to leave. What carries you out is a point of support, the thing you live for that is bigger than you.

Part one's chart can make the top right corner look like the finish. There is a longer scale behind it, which I call the arrow of consciousness. It's the same diagonal you've been looking at since Part one: the bottom left of the small square is the low end, the middle of the chart sits halfway along it, and the top right corner is where the line leaves the small square. Everything past that corner lives in the zoomed-out map. It shows the direction of your own life, and it ranks nobody.

The picture I use is space travel, and it's about speed. Nobody here is literally going anywhere. The picture is there to show how different each step is from the one before it, and how big the jumps get. The measure is how freely you can move, and gravity holds you back. Under the axiom, speed is how much of the whole you can take in at once, and gravity is the pull of the one point of view you started with.

At the low end, in Default, you can run, ride a bike and drive a car, and the ground decides where you go. Most of us live there, on the ground or just above it. As you let go, people learn to fly, and roads stop mattering. Then comes orbit. Astronauts in orbit are free of the ground, and the Earth still has them: they float because they are falling around the planet and missing it. In Part one's terms, this is about the middle of the line.

The top right corner of the small square is escape velocity, the speed at which you leave the Earth behind. Orbit and escape are not far apart in speed, and the difference in outcome is total: one circles forever and the other leaves.

Past the corner the picture keeps going, and each level is the speed that frees you from the next, bigger pull. The first leaves the Earth. The next leaves the Sun behind, and the one after that leaves the galaxy. The last has no pull left to leave, and every point in the universe is a place you can be at home, so the word every is doing some work.

That is the distance I want to convey. A life on the ground and a life among the stars are about as far apart as an ordinary human life and full human potential, and I believe a person can cross much of that distance in one lifetime. It is hard to feel this from the ground, which is why I use the picture. Nobody gets from the ground to the stars by wishing, either.

Every step up shows you more of the whole, and that includes more of what still needs fixing, so the higher you go, the more there is to do. And the whole that comes back at the end knows more than the whole that set out, because in between it has been each one of us.

After the last of these comes something like a birth. The whole universe on this scale is still the inside of something, like a womb or an egg. To whatever grows inside, that is the whole world until the moment it is born, and what comes after can't be drawn from in here. Many traditions have an image of a second birth for a decisive change in a person, and the old maps were scale maps as well.

#### Second births and staged maps

* Several traditions tell the end of the story. Origen, a third-century theologian, and Gregory of Nyssa read Paul's line that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28) as the restoration of all things. Zechariah has it that on that day the LORD will be one and his name one (14:9). The Quran has "indeed to Him we will return" (2:156). Buddhism calls the ending of greed, hatred and delusion nirvana, a word that means a flame going out.
* In John's gospel Jesus says that one must be born again, or born from above, since the Greek word can mean either. In the Hindu tradition the twice-born are those who received the initiation that counts as a second birth, and Meister Eckhart taught that God is born in the ground of the soul. The traditions use the image for different things, and I use it for the stage after the top of the scale.
* Other traditions draw staged maps too, so a scale like this isn't new. Early Buddhism has four stages of awakening, the Jains have fourteen steps of spiritual growth, and Teresa of Avila, the sixteenth-century Spanish mystic, described seven mansions of the inner castle. In Dante's Paradiso the pilgrim rises through the heavens without noticing, and Beatrice tells him it would be stranger if, freed of what held him back, he stayed below, like a flame sitting still on the ground.

Some physicists think our universe is one of many, and if so even the farthest point of this picture has something after it. In the spaceflight game Kerbal Space Program, reaching orbit feels like winning, right up until you look out of the window and see the moon (the game calls it the Mun) still sitting there, waiting for a visit.

## Why the paths differ

If one structure sits behind all this, a fair question follows. Why do the religions look so different from one another, and why do they keep changing?

The axiom answers both. The whole goes into separation through countless points of view, and the point of going is that each one comes back with something no other could have brought, which is where step 7 ended. No two points of view are alike, so no two people meet the wall in the same way. What holds it up, and what loosens it, depends on temperament, on culture, on the century, and on what the person has already learned. A path is a way back, drawn for particular people at a particular time. One drawn for herders crossing a desert will sound different from one drawn for monks on a mountain or merchants on a trade road, and it still describes the same thing: a whole that lost track of itself, and a way home. The core stays because it comes from the structure. The forms change because the people do.

That holds for our own time too. A form made for a village that shared one story and one calendar fits poorly in a city of strangers, where the day is cut into pieces by a phone, a job and an inbox. People are more educated and more skeptical than they used to be, they have less spare time, and they are used to checking things for themselves. Every tradition has met a moment like this before, with its reformers, its new schools and its new translations, each asking how to say the same thing to people who no longer hear it. The modern world asks it once more, and there are many answers to it. Part three describes the one I chose.

#### Many names for one thing

* A hymn of the Rigveda, among the oldest religious texts we have, says that to what is One, sages give many a title (1.164.46).
* In the Bhagavad Gita Krishna says that whenever dharma declines he takes birth again, age after age (4.7 to 8). The Hindu tradition reads this as the divine returning to every age, and I take it as a teaching that returns for each one.
* The Quran says that God gave each community its own law and way, and had God willed would have made them one community, and it tells them to race toward what is good (5:48). The verse also presents the Quran as the measure of the earlier books, so it speaks of many ways under one God without saying every way is equal.
* In the Buddhist Udana (6.4), a king has men born blind each touch a different part of an elephant, and each describes a different animal. The Buddha aims the story at rival sects, as a warning against one-sided views, and I borrow the picture: each one holds a real part of something larger.

## Why I want this

Part one's zoom out ended on full human potential, and that's what I'm after for this lifetime: the whole map, including everything past the corner of the chart. I don't want to believe it's there. I want to see it for myself, the way you see that a room is lit once you look up.

It started with questions. Why am I alive? What is happening here? How do the universe and the world work? Why am I, me? As I took steps on that road, I started losing some of my walls, and I felt, and then realized, how connected we are. That changed the way I feel toward people and the world. It grew into a strong, sincere wish to help others see the world the way I do, or more correctly, without the limitations all of us carry, and to make them happy, because that is what makes me happy. That is my point of support, the thing I live for that is bigger than me.

## What you can check

If the axiom is right, loosening the wall should leave marks you can check. In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how "one" people felt with someone in need predicted their willingness to help better than sympathy did. So the check for this part is the one in that study: how often do you feel at one with the person in front of you, and what do you do next when you do? In my experience, and in what people around me describe, the order is the same: first you start to listen, then giving shows up, unasked and without a tally, then perception clears.

The prediction is the interesting part. If the sense of separation is held by tension, then loosening it should make a person more generous without anyone telling them to be. It's an experiment you can run on yourself, which is the nice thing about this kind of claim. Neither of these proves the axiom. What they test is whether the moves are worth making, and they can be worth making either way.

## Where this goes next

That's the map as far as I can draw it honestly. What I haven't told you is how to walk it, and that is the next part, which has a lot less philosophy in it and a lot more everyday life.

Until then, one question that needs no equipment. Where, right now, does the border of you sit? Don't quote me, but I'd bet it isn't where you think.
