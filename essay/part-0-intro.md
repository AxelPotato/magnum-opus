# Part zero: Before you start

This essay has three parts, and each one has a different job. Here is what to expect from them, so you can set your own pace. The aim of the whole essay is to convey ideas. It isn't a course, and it doesn't try to teach you a method from the page.

Part one is the rational part, and I've kept it deliberately earthly. It uses no spiritual vocabulary and asks for no belief. It is about two things you can check in your own life, how much you make things happen and how much you let go of whatever holds you back, and why almost nobody works on both. If you have practiced for years, it may feel light, with sitcom characters and everyday chores in it. That is on purpose. It is the ground floor, built so that a friend who has never meditated can stand on it too.

Part two is the spiritual part, and it has its own role: it says where all of this comes from and where it leads. It starts from a view of reality that I call an assumption, it quotes many traditions as witnesses, and it says plainly where the reading is mine. You can disagree with it and still use the rest.

Part three is the practical part: how this is done in the reality of the 21st century. It starts with one thing you can try alone tonight, goes on to what needs other people, a team and a guide, and ends with the place where we live it at full strength, which I call a space port.

You can stop after any part, and each one makes the next easier to judge.

## Who is talking, and who taught me

I'm Alex. In my teens I had a lot of questions: why things are the way they are, and why I am the way I am. Over time those questions led to a spiritual search. I have worked with computers for about ten years, eight of them in cyber security, which I still do. My spiritual journey is a good deal longer, more than fourteen years.

Everything in this essay that goes beyond what you can check in your own life comes from one teacher, Genadiy Givin, and the method in Part three is the one he teaches. I want you to know something about him before you read anything that rests on him.

Genadiy was born in Riga. He swam from the first grade, and from the eighth he trained in boxing, sambo and karate and became a master of sport, while he studied at Riga Technical University. In the tenth grade he met people who had woken up, in the sense Part two describes. What he saw in them changed how he looked at life and gave him a huge wish to know what they knew. One of them sent him to a man who worked in nuclear physics, and that man became his teacher. The knowledge comes to him through a European line of teachers and students that goes back a long way, and he describes everyone in it as ordinary people without titles, himself included. He says that on his own he isn't worth much, and that the knowledge he passes on is what matters.

Here is what I can add from my own years as his student. He is a nonstop source of compassion and support. Whenever you talk to him you leave the conversation better than you came into it. On a personal level, I feel very deeply how much he cares about us, his students. Most of his teaching comes down to how we relate to other people, and he is an example of it. He rarely corrects one person at a time. He says it to the whole team, and each of us takes what applies. He brings together people who would not agree about much, which is harder than it sounds in a group of strong opinions.

What I can't give you is proof that he is right. What I can give you is what to look at: whether you want to spend time in the company of the people around him and feel better afterwards, whether they do what they say they'll do, and whether the people close to them, at home and at work, find them easier to live with than before. You can judge how it goes.
