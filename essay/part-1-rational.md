# Part one: Rational

## Two questions

Two questions first. Nobody is checking, so you can be honest.

How many things have you said you would do, and then didn't? Keep it to the last month. We're not here to ruin your evening.

And how often are you the first one to make a move in a conflict? The first "okay, that one was on me," the first message after the long silence?

Hold on to your two answers. They measure two different things, and almost nobody works on both. I call them DO and BE. DO is how much you make things happen. BE is how much you let go of whatever holds you back from the inside, which turns out to be how much you can be here, aware of what is actually happening right now. When both are high, I call it high agency, and the few people who get there live a different kind of life.

This part is one graph, drawn slowly.

## DO: making things happen

Start with the easy one, since everybody already agrees it matters.

Picture the steering wheel of your life. Either you're holding it or you're in the back seat. How much of your life happens because you made it happen, and how much of it just happens to you?

The simplest test is a boring one: do you follow up on your words? That's most of it. It doesn't need a loud personality, and it doesn't need a world-changing project. Making a plan for the day and then following it counts for more than most world-changing projects, which tend to live in the notes app.

The quiet kinds of DO tell you more than the loud ones. Learning something new is one, because for weeks you're bad at it in private. Organizing is another: somebody has to turn "we should catch up sometime" into Thursday at seven, and picking the restaurant is the hardest outer-agency test most groups ever face. Asking counts, out loud, for the raise or the help, instead of hoping somebody notices. So does finishing the form and the dentist appointment that has been "next week" for a year. And fixing the squeaky door. If you've been going to fix it for a year, you've been running a free experiment on yourself, and the results are in.

The big kind has a trap of its own. Dreaming about changing your whole life is a very comfortable way of not taking today's small step. You can't pick up the guitar yet, because first you'd have to quit your job and move somewhere with a music scene.

I call this outer agency. You did something on purpose, and afterwards your life has something in it that wasn't going to show up on its own.

## BE: letting go, and being here

BE gets talked about much less, and it's the more interesting of the two. DO is playing your instrument better. BE is improving the quality of the instrument itself.

BE is being at ease with yourself and being here. Letting go is how you get there. It's showing your inner world to another person without three days of rehearsal. It's how much you believe your own fears, and how much other people's opinions push you around. It's also where the kind part of a person lives, because that part switches off whenever you're braced.

It matters even if you couldn't care less about being a nice person. Fears and doubts sit in the body as tension, and tension is a tax on your ability to see solutions. Carry enough of it and there is a whole shelf of solutions you never look at, because they're unusual, or embarrassing, or kind of crazy, and you ruled them out before you noticed you were deciding anything. You fall back on the answer already in your head, and you pay the tax on every problem you meet. In 2016 a Go program made a move against one of the best players in the world that commentators took for a mistake, because no professional would play there. It helped win the game. Whatever else it had, it didn't have the belief that you don't play there. Jonny Miller's essay "How to Unclench" uses the same tax image and goes much deeper than I will.

Here is the connection to being here. Tension pulls your attention out of the moment, into replaying what happened, rehearsing what might go wrong, checking how you look. Your body is in the room and your attention is in a meeting that isn't happening. When researchers pinged people's phones at random moments, their minds were somewhere else almost half the time, and they were less happy for it (Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010). The more you let go, the more your attention stays here. The more you stay here, the sooner you notice a feeling while it's happening, which is the moment it can move. That's why letting go and being here sit on one axis.

I don't mean relaxed, because you can be relaxed and asleep. I mean released: free from your own internal limitations, and more here, not less.

So how do you move up BE? Meditation helps a lot. What moves it most is the thing nobody wants to do: admit you were wrong and take the first step toward fixing it. Say sorry, and mean it. Have the hard conversation, feel the "oh no, I really don't want to" go through you, and do it anyway. If you already say sorry for everything to keep the peace, your hard step is the other one: saying what you need, or saying no.

That is an act against your own resistance, and I call it inner agency. Outer agency moves you along DO and gets things done. Inner agency moves you up BE and gets you unstuck. Both are ways of expressing who you are, effectively, and that is all agency means here. So letting go, the one that sounds soft, is a high-agency skill.

## Four rooms

Draw them as a cross. DO runs left to right, from things happen to me to I make things happen. BE runs bottom to top, from braced and elsewhere to released and here. You get four rooms, and each has a few famous residents, because a chart with nobody on it is just algebra.

### Default

Bottom left: low BE and low DO. Things happen to you, and you're braced for the next one. You say a lot, and you follow through on a fraction of it. Most of us live here. From the outside a lot of it looks like laziness. It's usually fear, and fear is busy: it burns a lot of energy where nobody can see it, on doubt and regret and replaying.

Squidward is the most extreme case and the funniest. He complains constantly, all his problems live next door, and he dreams of being a famous artist and clarinetist. When he does try, it goes badly and he blames everybody else. In one episode he moves to a town full of people exactly like him, and the sameness drives him up the wall.

There's a line going around that you either die a SpongeBob or live long enough to become a Squidward. Kids are born relaxed. They're joyful, they do things, and nobody has told them yet what's impossible. Over time life adds ideas, doubts and fears, and we start taking them seriously.

George Costanza is the exit story. He lives with his parents, has no job and believes everything his head tells him. Then Jerry tells him that if every instinct he has ever had is wrong, the opposite must be right. George does the opposite of every fear he has, and by the end of the episode he has a girl, a job and a bit of respect. It isn't the perfect way out of this room. I also can't tell you it doesn't work.

Default has a nice corner too, near the middle, where the decent, functional people live. They keep their word most of the time and apologize sometimes. They have a job, a few friends and something they do on weekends, and nothing is on fire. A lot of them feel ok, just a little flat, and there's a word for the flat part: languishing, a term from the sociologist Corey Keyes that Adam Grant made popular. You're not depressed, and you're not thriving. You're meh. Thoreau noticed the same thing back in the 1850s. In Walden he wrote that most people lead lives of quiet desperation. That's this corner: people who went along with the life expected of them and never got around to asking whether it was theirs. The desperation is quiet because it hardly feels like desperation. It feels like Tuesday.

Hank Hill is that corner at its most lovable. He's responsible, kind and loyal, he has opinions about lawns that border on a belief system, and he can barely say "I love you" out loud. Jim Halpert is the relaxed version, good at a job he treats for years as temporary. Pam Beesly sits a few feet away, a receptionist who wants to be an artist and takes years to do anything about it.

What makes this room sticky is that it feels like making your own decisions. A retreat is three or four days away from the regular rhythm of life, a chance to practice mindfulness and look at where you're going. On one, people get a small instruction for a few days, something like not touching the phone for a few hours. I've watched people catch themselves with it in their hand anyway, without having decided anything, and on my first retreat I was one of them. The mind decided, and we just did it. That's Default from the inside: the body on autopilot and the attention somewhere else.

### Driven

Bottom right: low BE and high DO. Still tense, because nothing has been let go, but these people get things done. You know some. You may be one. I was.

With my personality tendencies, it was a natural fit. For a few years I gave myself to a career in cyber security: working all week, studying on the weekends, with a constant background tension of not being good enough and not much time for the questions I'd started with, or for my wish to help other people live happier lives. I did become quite good at it. Luckily, something stopped me, and it came from the inside.

The hours aren't what makes this room. Plenty of people work long days toward something they love. What makes it is the quality of the hours, and underneath there is usually an "I'll prove them wrong," or an "I'll get it done and then people will like me." It's a lack the person can't let go of, so they push harder in the one direction they know. Our culture pushes the same way. Almost every idea we have about success comes from this room.

This room builds companies and wins medals and answers its email. The cost is the tax from earlier. With enough tension, the only tool you can see is more force, so when a problem doesn't move you push harder in the same direction. For a long time that works. Then it stops working, and the person has no second move.

You can spot it by the finish line, which keeps moving: when I get this, then I'll relax, and when this arrives there's a new this. Which raises a good question: what happens when a driven person actually reaches the dream?

Jim Carrey is one answer. He reached about everything an actor can reach, and he has talked openly about years of depression. He has said everybody should get rich and famous and have everything they ever dreamed of, so they'd see it isn't the answer.

Michael Phelps is the same story, counted in medals: 28 Olympic medals, 23 of them gold, more than anyone in history. By his own account he crashed after every Olympics, and in 2014 he checked into a treatment center. He has said he had always seen himself as a swimmer and not as a human being. Going into London in 2012 he wanted nothing to do with the sport anymore, and he says Rio in 2016 felt nothing like that.

Simone Biles is the most decorated gymnast in history. In Tokyo in 2021, mid-air, something stopped working the way it always had. She pulled out of the team final and said why that same day: mental health. She came back for the beam and won bronze, and in Paris she won three golds. I won't tell you which room she lives in now. That's her business.

Tal Ben-Shahar has a name for what Carrey and Phelps ran into: the arrival fallacy, the belief that once you arrive you'll finally feel the way you've been chasing. You arrive, you get a few days of relief, and the old tension is still there, now with a nicer view. In my case the tension was "not good enough," and I can confirm it is a productive thing to run on, up to a point.

Busy is a very respectable way to never sit with yourself, and almost nobody gets an intervention for working too hard. They get promoted. Even the tools of letting go get recruited: meditation for focus, Stoic quotes on the lock screen. The engine underneath, a point to prove or approval to earn, keeps running. It just has a nicer morning routine now.

### Drifting

Top left: released and passive, high BE and low DO. I call them drifters, fondly, because I was a pretty good one. Until I was about 24, before my Driven years, I was a kind of spiritual drifter, with big ideas and not much done. The room is full of relaxed, kind people you'd happily spend an afternoon with, who do very little. The room is about DO, and income has nothing to do with it: a nurse on night shifts is doing plenty and lives nowhere near here. It happens when life rarely pushes you from the outside, which in my case it didn't, so I had plenty of time for big ideas. Several of them are still ideas, and they're very comfortable where they are.

The trap is that it feels good, and the good feeling is real. But look at what is producing the calm. Mostly it's that nothing is pushing on you: no deadline, no colleague who irritates you, no relative asking when you're getting a real job. Calm that depends on nobody asking anything of you lasts exactly until somebody asks. The explanations help it along: the timing isn't right, wanting things is just stress, everything happens when it's meant to. Each has a grain of truth, which is why they work. So does the long stay somewhere warm to figure out what I want. It can be exactly right, and it turns into the trap when it replaces the conversation you can't face.

The best-known example is the Dude from The Big Lebowski, which is my favorite film, so you can see how I ended up in here. He is the chillest person ever put on screen, and most of the plot is other people's plans happening to him. He is loved for it, and for good reason. I'd just like him to own a calendar.

Phoebe from Friends is the Dude with a guitar: kind, odd, loved by her friends, floating through life on instinct. She's the happiest resident of this room and still a resident.

John Welwood, an American psychologist and long-time Buddhist practitioner, coined a term in the 1980s for those escape moves: spiritual bypassing, using spiritual ideas and practices to avoid your own problems. Running off to an island for a long meditation so you never have to face one relationship is the obvious version. Notice what gets skipped: the sorry, the hard conversation, the first step in a conflict, the whole inner agency list. Bypassing is a very sophisticated way of never having to do any of it.

Almost every tradition that works on letting go gets shortened the same way, to the part that sounds like permission. Mainstream spirituality teaches people to live in this room. It tells you to be present and let go, which you need, and says very little about getting up and doing something, which you need just as much.

Take Stoicism, because the internet version is so popular. Some things are up to you and a lot aren't, so put your effort into the first and make peace with the second. The popular version adds one more step: then I shouldn't feel anything. Be a rock. Marcus Aurelius's private notebook is full of him reminding himself not to snap at difficult people, and a man without feelings wouldn't need the notes. Advaita Vedanta, the Indian school of non-duality, goes the same way. It says the separate self is an appearance, and read on a weekend that turns into: there is no doer, so nothing is my responsibility, and the world is an illusion, so why answer that message. The traditional route asked for preparation and ethical living first. The quick version keeps the punchline and loses the setup.

The extreme version of this room is the person who learned to watch their thoughts without getting pulled in, which is a real skill, and kept going until they were watching their whole life from the side, like a documentary about somebody else. Ask how they are and they'll tell you, sincerely, that they have no problems. Super calm. Boring calm. I was heading that way myself, and I met people who fixed my silly ideas about it before I got there. You need to open your heart, not just your attention. Calm that costs you your feelings isn't release. It's anesthesia.

### High agency

Top right: released and active, high BE and high DO. You've let go of enough to be here, and you act from there. It's also turbulent.

A quick way to find your own examples is a test from George Mack, who wrote the essay on high agency. You're in a third-world jail cell. Who do you call? Whoever you just thought of is the highest-agency person you know: capable enough to do something about it, and caring enough to bother.

Look at the person you picked. They treat a problem as solvable until it proves otherwise, so "what can I do about this" comes long before "whose fault is it." They don't wait for permission. They can be disagreeable when it's needed, and never for sport. They own their part in a mess without turning it into a punishment. And they care, which is what separates agency from ruthlessness. You can be capable without caring. You just wouldn't have called that person from the jail cell.

The turbulence comes from walking on two legs. In Driven you hop on the DO leg. In Drifting you hop on the BE leg. Here you finally put both down, and walking turns out to be hard. Every step swings to one side or the other until you find the center.

Here is why the turbulence is worth it: the two legs feed each other. BE widens what you can see, so you notice more things worth doing. Doing them tests the fears that kept you from looking, and most of those fears turn out smaller than they looked. Each one that fails the test loosens the tension a little more. George Costanza ran this experiment for one episode. A person who keeps it up for years looks less like someone balancing two things and more like someone doing one thing, with a bit of a dance in it.

Malala Yousafzai kept going to school under the Taliban, wrote an anonymous blog for the BBC and spoke out on national television, all at eleven. At fifteen she was shot in the head on her school bus. She survived, built a global movement for girls' education, won the Nobel Peace Prize at seventeen and then got an Oxford degree, which after that list sounds almost modest. In her speech at the UN she said she doesn't even hate the man who shot her. That's both legs in one person.

Marcus Aurelius ran the Roman Empire for nineteen years with a plague in it, a long war on the frontier, and a general who declared himself emperor behind his back. On campaign he wrote notes to himself that we now call the Meditations, a man with the largest to-do list on earth reminding himself to do the task in front of him and stop being run by other people's opinions. When the usurper was killed, the ancient sources say, Marcus was upset he'd lost the chance to pardon him. We also have a pretty cool statue of him.

Rosa Parks is a good one for anyone who thinks this room needs a loud personality. She was a soft-spoken seamstress who had worked with the local NAACP chapter for years before the day in 1955 when she refused to give up her bus seat in Montgomery. She said later that the only tired she was, was tired of giving in. Then came decades of steady, unglamorous work.

Fiction has one too: Sean Maguire in Good Will Hunting, a community college teacher with a quiet life and his own grief, who at the moment it matters finds the one sentence that gets through: "It's not your fault." The person you called from the jail cell lives here too, most likely with no statue and no prize.

## What the top right gives you

So what do you get for walking on both legs?

From the inside, you're here for your own life. You see more options, because the tax stops being collected, and more problems start to look solvable. Your reactions get shorter, and so does the time you stay angry at somebody. You say sorry faster, because the relationship matters more to you than being right. Emotions still arrive, and they go through you without a fight. Big challenges get more interesting and small things get more joyful, which is a strange combination and the best one I know. A lot of people who get here describe it as being in love with life.

You can receive, too: help, praise, a day off. And you say the nice things out loud. From a very young age I felt weird telling people to their faces how I feel about them, and unexpressed gratitude and love sit in you as tension, just like unexpressed anger does.

From the outside, people find you steady and warm. You say what you think, and you don't use it as a weapon. When somebody is having a hard moment you can help them through it, instead of climbing into the mess with them. And you become, mostly by accident, the person someone calls from the jail cell. Don't be surprised when the phone rings.

At the far corner of the chart is the peak, where nothing feels impossible. Almost everybody has visited. Programmers call it flow, athletes call it the zone, musicians call it the muse, and the rest of us call it a good day. Being in love is the preview: for a few weeks everything is good and you feel like you can do anything, and nothing outside you changed. An athlete can live in the zone for ninety minutes and lose it in the parking lot. Everybody visits, and almost nobody lives there.

## The math

Say BE and DO each run from 0 to 100, and how much of your potential you use is one times the other. With no DO, freedom changes nothing outside you. With no BE, all the action goes down the few paths your fears allow. Someone at 100 on DO and 1 on BE gets 100. Give up five points of DO, gain one of BE, and it's 95 times 2, which is 190, just from being slightly less busy.

Picture the whole chart as a square, 10,000 in all. Default takes up 100, Driven at the wall or Drifting at its mirror 1,000, and the corner all of it.

Driven people usually object that if they let go of the tension they'll lose their edge. The drive and the tension are two different things that most of us have glued together. You keep the drive and drop the brace. Phelps in Rio is the closest famous example I have, and I'm a smaller one. Most of the tension from my Driven years is gone, and the drive grew. I used to lose a lot of time to video games and shows, and now I want to work on my goals every day. I get more done, it satisfies me much more, and the goals got bigger.

On one project I was investigating an incident and couldn't find how the attacker had gotten into the system. In my Driven years that's where the panic would start, the feeling that I'm failing. This time there was a calm in me that just knew something would crack. It did. The answer was the very thing I had been searching for at the start, with one last twist, and it was quite simple. It just took me a while to see it.

## The line

Now draw a line from the bottom left corner to the top right. Every point on it has as much BE as DO. I call it the line of harmony.

On the line, what you're free to want on the inside is in step with what you can do on the outside. You don't need to be anywhere special on it. Someone at 30 and 30 is on the line, and so is someone at 70 and 70. Position along the line says how far you've come. Distance from it says how much is leaking.

Below the line, in Driven, the outside runs ahead of the inside, so the doing often points at something that was never really yours, and when the results arrive nothing inside says thank you. Above it, in Drifting, there's plenty of freedom inside, not much to show for it outside, and a very comfortable gap between the wishes and the doing.

## Why almost nobody stays

If the corner is so good, why isn't everybody there? Because you can't steer to it from an extreme.

The reason is inertia. Someone deep in Driven has years of momentum in one direction, and someone deep in Drifting has the same momentum the other way, so neither can turn and aim at the corner. The route goes back through the middle first. The driven person eases off, the drifter starts moving, and only from the middle can either of them climb. Nobody does that for fun, so usually something has to stop them first.

For a driven person it's usually a crash: burnout, depression after reaching the goal, a body that stops cooperating, or a shift in values when a relative ends up in the hospital. Jim Carrey made far fewer movies after 2014, said in 2022 that he'd done enough, and then came back for another Sonic movie. In my case it came from the inside. Whenever I had time to myself I felt an emptiness that none of my results could touch, and it made my weekends the worst part of the week. Under it was a wish to know myself more deeply.

For a drifter I've noticed three things. The first is that the money runs out. I know that sounds stupid, and I'm serious. When a person waits long enough for life to sort itself out, life arranges a situation where they can't cover what they need. The cause is the waiting, and money is just the most common way it shows up. No matter how spiritual a person is, reality has a way of reminding us about itself. It works from both sides, by the way: the driven person gets the same reminder when there is finally plenty of money and nothing inside has changed. The second is that something happens, usually in the family, and they can't stay passive anymore. The third is the plateau. Years of calm, nothing changing, and one day it all looks like a waste of time. Then, instead of stopping in the middle, they swing all the way across: screw this, I'm getting a job.

That's why people swing. Driven people swing hard into Drifting: burnout, then a one-way ticket to a tropical island. Drifting people swing hard back: I'm going full business, never again. I know people from both directions.

One friend of mine is the cleanest example: a founder who built a company and did very well, and who was also, in my friend's own words, one of the most tense people you could meet. Family trips to beautiful places were miserable every second, because there was no way to enjoy any of it. When the old way stopped working, my friend went on a retreat and, for a while, stopped. On the chart, that's coming off the wall back toward the middle, and then climbing, with the action kept the whole way. It shows at home. My friend has real patience now with the kids, neither of whom is very calm, and can talk with someone about what's going on inside, calmly and with care.

Learning to walk on two legs looks wonky for a reason. A toddler doesn't walk so much as fall forward in a controlled way, sometimes into the coffee table. Adults are the same. At the beginning we overshoot, hard one way and hard the other, and that's how you learn it. Trace a life on the chart and you see a zigzag that keeps narrowing.

## The reason bigger than you

Everything so far has been a push: the money runs out, the body stops cooperating, the plateau gets old. Pushes work, and then they end. The money comes back, the body recovers, and the person drifts back to wherever they were comfortable.

What gets someone across the middle on purpose is a pull. Plenty of people try to get there by being a bit less driven and a bit more relaxed, circling the edge of it for years. The result is usually a driven person who meditates and is slightly more effective. What carries a person across is a reason bigger than themselves. "I'll prove them wrong" turns into "I want to help people do something." Or "I have kids, and I want to be a better person for them." Anything that matters more to you than your own wants, needs and fears can do it, however small it looks from outside.

Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist who survived the Nazi camps, saw a human being as always pointed beyond themselves, at a meaning to fulfill or a person to love. Self-actualization, he wrote, comes only as a side effect of self-transcendence. Chase yourself directly and you miss. Serve something and you get yourself as a bonus. In the camps he saw that people who had a "why" could bear an astonishing amount of "how," a line he borrowed from Nietzsche.

Picture someone who freezes whenever they have to speak up for themselves: the awkward email, the raise. Now picture the same person at a school meeting, speaking up for their kid, calm and specific and a little scary. Same person, same nerves. The fear got outvoted. Tension is a vote against anything that puts you at risk, looking wrong, being rejected, losing control, and it usually wins because what's on the other side of the scale is small and about you. A reason bigger than you is a bigger vote. The fear can still be there. It just stops deciding.

Reasons also outlast goals. A goal about you ends when you reach it, and what's left can feel like nothing. Graduates who reached goals about growth, relationships and community were doing better a year later, and those who reached goals about money and image weren't (Niemiec and colleagues, 2009). A reason about someone else has no finish line. Nobody finishes raising a kid.

The same fix works for both rooms, because the drifter and the driven person have the same hole: their reason is about them. The drifter's reason is their own peace, which takes little action to keep. The driven person's is their own proof, which never gets enough. A bigger reason gives the drifter something worth getting up for and gives the driven person permission to stop proving. It also damps the swing. A swing is your direction set by your mood, and a reason doesn't have a mood. It won't make you less tired. It makes you less likely to quit the whole thing on a Tuesday.

#### What the studies say

* In two studies that followed college students week by week, students whose goals were about supporting other people got less distressed as the term went on, and students whose goals were about looking good got more distressed (Crocker and colleagues, 2010).
* In four studies with more than two thousand young people, a purpose aimed beyond the self helped them stick with a boring task and later improved their grades (Yeager and colleagues, 2014).
* In a small experiment, people nudged to think about helping others had lower stress-hormone responses to a public speaking test, though they didn't report feeling less anxious (Abelson and colleagues, 2014). Their bodies calmed down before their feelings did.
* University fundraisers spent five minutes with a student who had received a scholarship. A month later they were spending 142% more time on the phone and raising 171% more money than before, while callers who only read his letter showed no significant gains (Grant and colleagues, 2007).
* A long study of older adults found that volunteering went with a lower risk of dying over four years only when the motive was other people (Konrath and colleagues, 2012).
* A review of ten studies and more than 136,000 people found that those with a stronger sense of purpose had about 17% lower risk of dying during the follow-up (Cohen and colleagues, 2016). It's an association, so I'm not claiming that a bigger reason will add years to your life. The direction is the same in nearly everything I could find.

Look back at the high agency room. Malala had girls' education. Rosa Parks had a cause she'd been working on for years before the bus. Marcus Aurelius wrote that he got up in the morning to do the work of a human being. Different centuries, temperaments and jobs, and the same thing underneath: a reason that wasn't about them.

Bigger doesn't have to mean grand. It only has to be bigger than you: a kid, a team, a craft, a person who relies on you. A New Year's resolution is a reason about you, which is why it tends to expire in February.

It does have to be real, though. "I want to help people" can be the driven person's new costume, the same engine with a halo. Two questions tell them apart. Would you still do it if nobody ever found out? And does it make you gentler with the person in front of you, or does that person turn into material for the cause? Bigger than you doesn't mean instead of you, either: people who look after others to the exclusion of themselves report more distress (Fritz and Helgeson, 1998).

## Harmony as the road

Put it together. The road to the top right is a road to harmony between what you want on the inside, what you can do on the outside, and what you have to work with right now. The way there is to keep catching up the lazy leg until the two walk in step. Pushing the strong one harder only gets you a faster hop.

You can check which way you're moving at any time. How many options do you see right now, compared with a year ago? When did you last do something nobody asked you to do, for somebody else's good?

## Zoom out

One correction before this part ends. The top right corner is the peak of this chart, and this chart is a small part of a much bigger map.

Zoom out, and the whole square we've been using shrinks to a small box in the bottom left corner. A person can grow into all of that area, and I like the plain term for it: full human potential. Most people have stood at its edge for a moment without a name for it, under a sky full of stars or holding a newborn, when for a minute nothing felt separate. Those moments don't fit anywhere on this chart.
