# Part three: Practical

## Two things a life needs

Anything that moves needs two things: energy and direction. A car with a full tank and no steering is a bomb with a radio. A car with a perfect map and an empty tank is a very well informed parked car.

Most of us know both failures from the inside. Energy without direction is the founder from Part one, in the swing: plenty of drive, pointed at whatever was loudest, until his joints filed a complaint. Direction without energy is the person who knows perfectly well they shouldn't shout at their kids. They've read the book. They can explain why it's a bad idea. And then they shout, because knowing what to do and being able to do it turn out to be two separate supplies.

Part two gave a reason for this. The wall that holds us apart is held in the body as tension, and tension is a tax on energy, so the first source of energy is letting go. That isn't enough, and the second source comes from outside you: a reason bigger than you, and other people who share it. Direction has its own problem. You can't see your own blind spots, which is the whole meaning of the phrase, so direction has to come from someone who has already walked the road and can see you from the outside. So a life has two supplies to keep stocked. Direction comes from a guide, and energy comes from a higher goal and a team. Everything else is the daily work those two make possible.

If you would rather begin with something to do tonight, begin with stillness, which section 7 describes. It needs nothing but you: you close your eyes and give your attention to the body, and there is nothing more to it.

If you accept what Part two said, a life is one lesson in a much longer course, and the highest thing you can aim at is to graduate from it. How long the course is I can't tell you. What I can say is how the course nudges a person who hasn't learned the lesson. It does it in two ways. One is outside: the same failure turns up in a new costume, the same kind of boss, the same kind of breakup. The other is inside: everything works, the job is fine, the people are fine, and you are dissatisfied anyway. If neither happens, and your life is going well and feels well, you have no need of a method. A method is a way to do on purpose, and faster, what those nudges do slowly.

The method I'll describe comes down a lineage of teachers. My teacher received it from his teacher, who received it from his own, and he adapted it to the realities of life in the 21st century. It reached me through him. Doing this with other people magnifies what a person already brings, and that cuts both ways: turn up the energy before you have a direction and you get a louder version of the wrong thing. The ten parts below go in an order that suits most people: your own wish first, then a goal and its reason, then service and the people you do it with, then who points the way, then the quiet inner practices, then how people come to the method, and last the place where it can be lived in full. The order changes from person to person, because people arrive from different places, and a good guide reads where you are standing before deciding where you start.

## 1. Your own wish

Of everything in this part, this is the one that decides the rest, and it's the only one nobody else can supply. A mentor can steer, a team can lift, a teacher can try to wake it and pass it on, but none of them can want it for you. It gets made inside each person.

What is the wish for? These are the reasons people give for starting, and they are not promises the method makes. Life pushes people toward this kind of work in very different ways, and the reasons often change along the way. Most of them fall into a few groups.

Some come with questions: why we exist, how the world works and why it is the way it is. That is where mine started, with the questions from Part two, and over time it grew into a deep desire to help others live a more fulfilling and happy life.

Others come for steadiness. They want to feel calm and happy inside, with something solid to stand on whatever happens around them. They want to stop being run by old habits of mind, painful memories and negative thoughts, and to see life as it is, without the mind getting in the way.

Some start with something much more ordinary and pressing. They can't sleep, or the stress has gotten too high, or they are simply worn out. They aren't looking for anything spiritual. They want to feel better, and for some of them the wish grows from there.

And others come because they want to do more with their lives and be better to the people around them. They want to take their life into their own hands instead of being moved around by it, and to do some good for their family, their community and the world, starting with the people closest to them. For that they work on understanding others, handling their emotions, getting along better and opening their heart.

Most people recognize themselves in a few of these, and which one is yours matters less than that it is really yours.

You can see it from the outside, and quickly. A project that never gets built, a plan that stays a plan: the wish behind it was probably weaker than it felt. It shows in what you build with your hands far more than in what you say about it.

It also fades. Nearly everyone has days with a lift and days with a slump, and the wish needs feeding to survive them. Plenty of people wake up, take a few steps and stop. A low wish is no ranking of who is better or worse. It's a day, and days change.

The wish can be grown. It isn't equally strong in everyone from birth, and it gets brought up, partly by the people around you helping each other. A goal you love, sitting in the background of everything even when you never mention it, feeds it. So does real work for other people, done with your hands. Underneath those sits a decision, made on purpose and made again when it slips. A bigger wish also crowds out smaller ones, so you don't have to fight the small ones one at a time.

Two things quietly drain it. One is feeling sorry for yourself, which everyone does and which takes more from the wish than almost anything else. The other is a half-wish that bargains every Tuesday about how much it's willing to give.

So ask yourself, quietly, how much you want this, and take the answer as information. If the answer is low, nothing is wrong with you. Do something concrete, and decide again.

## 2. A goal, and a reason

Part one's chart said that inner growth and outer action are two axes and that you need both. This section makes that practical: you set goals, and you judge the inner growth by the outer ones.

A goal in this method has three properties. It's short, meaning weeks or months. It's measurable, so at the end you can say yes or no. And it's tied to reality, so it lives in the world and not in your head. Run a retreat. Finish a draft of an essay. The outer result is the check on the inner work, because it's very easy to feel like you're growing when nothing around you has moved. A person who has been meditating for years and still has trouble getting a small group to a meeting on time has learned something about their meditation, and it's worth hearing.

Inner work is real work, and from the inside it can be hard to tell whether it's working. A calm sit feels like progress whether or not anything has changed. A goal turns the calm into something that can be tested. It also gives you energy, which is its second job. A goal you care about pulls effort out of you that discipline never would.

Then there's the reason behind the goal. Part two called mine a point of support, the thing I live for that is bigger than me. The goal can be small as long as the reason is large. The reason keeps a person going after the first excitement has worn off, and it's what carries a person across the middle of Part one's chart, which nothing smaller manages.

One caution, since the outer goals can turn on you. Real results are among the wishes in section 1, and they are how you check the work. They are an indicator and never the aim. If you start chasing them for their own sake, you've rebuilt the Driven room with better vocabulary.

## 3. Service

By service I mean putting effort into the good of other people, or of something bigger than you, without counting what comes back. Part two ends up in the same place: if you accept that the separation is a way of looking, then helping you is helping me, as a matter of plumbing, with niceness left out of it. Service is the method taking that seriously.

It has two rules. Give without conditions: don't demand thanks, credit or a result in return, and don't hold the person hostage to the goal behind it, however big that goal is. The second rule is to give to something bigger than your own comfort, because that is where the second effect shows up: a person who serves a goal larger than themselves starts passing more through, like a wire that is part of a bigger circuit and no longer a loose coil in a drawer. In Part two's terms, the source is the whole that a separate self was never really cut off from, and serving something larger than yourself keeps the wall around you a little open, so more of the whole can pass through. That speeds up the one thing almost nothing else touches, the slow wearing away of the ego.

Inner work done only for yourself can go in a circle. You improve your own state, then you improve it more. That has real value, and it can turn into polishing the same stone. Service gives the energy somewhere to go, and there the work gets tested, because other people can tell you whether it helped.

## 4. The team

A team can be as small as two. It combines the other parts: you work on your attention, you meet your mirror, you pursue a goal and you give, all at once and with witnesses.

The first thing a team does is hold you up in the hard moments. When your mood falls through the floor, the other person's mood hasn't, and the pair holds longer than either half. I tend to be the one who holds others, but I have my moments too. Most often it is when we have set goals and haven't reached them, or a deadline is close and the task looks unmanageable. I used to slide into despair then, and my close friends helped me come back to myself, calm down, let it go and return to a more adequate state. That alone is worth more than most techniques.

The second thing is the one everybody expects: a team gets more done than the same people working alone. The reasons are old and ordinary. People tend to keep a promise made to another person more reliably than one made to themselves. A task that looks unmanageable gets split among people who can each see a different part of it. Somebody else has often already solved the problem you are stuck on, and watching how they did it teaches faster than being told. And effort is contagious, so it is harder to coast next to someone who is really trying. Put together, the result is more than the people would add up to separately, which is probably the oldest claim anyone has made about teams.

It comes with conditions. The people have to be aimed at something, because a group of people who simply like each other can have a lovely time and still go nowhere. The group also has to keep its conflicts short. A quarrel reverses the effect: the same closeness that lifts a team weighs it down when there is a wall between two of its members.

The people also have to want it, and this is where the team meets the wish from section 1. A team multiplies whatever is put into it, and anything times nothing is still nothing, a fact your math teacher and your guru would both confirm. A good team that doesn't want to play is a good roster with a bad season. A group of people who each really want to move gives everyone in it a good deal more than any of them could find alone, like a cable made of many strands.

The team does something for the wish itself, too. On your own it dies down faster, and far from the people who share it, it's harder to keep. So people meet to infect each other with it and to remind each other, and when your own spark drops, lighting up a partner gets some of it back. The company of people who want the same thing is one of the best things to feed it.

The modern world makes this harder. In an earlier age a village gave you the other people for free. Now you can order food, learn skills and find company from a phone without anyone seeing you need anything. That is convenient, and it also removes the one thing that did the work. You have to build the village on purpose.

## 5. The teacher and the method

A team has energy, and a team can still be going in a circle. Someone who has already walked the whole path can see where the team is growing and where it is stuck, which nobody inside the circle can.

My teacher is Genadiy, whom I introduced in Part zero, and what he does is easy to describe. It's like having a coach who has run the race. They don't run it for you, and they can't create your wish to run, which stays yours. What they can do is tell you when your form has drifted, and give you something to try on Monday. Genadiy rarely does this for one person at a time. He says it to the whole team, and each person integrates it. That is something you can check for yourself: if you ever sit in on a team meeting with him, watch who he is speaking to, and what each person does with it afterwards. They also carry a kind of steadiness that is hard to describe and easy to recognize, the way a calm, experienced guide on a mountain path steadies everyone walking behind.

A teacher does one more job. At the start, nobody in a group has learned to let go of their idea of what is right. Everyone carries a private opinion of who is wrong, and two people who are each sure they are right cannot settle it between themselves, because the one doing the settling is the one who is attached. A neutral third person helps, and a mentor often plays that part. The teacher is the larger version of the same thing: someone whose view none of us owns, so that a group of very different people can face the same way at once without any of us having to concede to another. Take the teacher out and a group tends to go one of two ways. It becomes a pleasant hangout, or it settles into a comfortable corner of its own and slowly turns into a swamp, a little more comfortable and a little less pointed every month.

The knowledge behind the method is older than he is. It came to him through a line of teachers, and what he did was adapt it to the realities of life in the 21st century. The way we have arranged ourselves around it is ours.

## 6. Guidance

I'll try to say this without sounding like a cult, and you may judge how it goes. The first thing to judge is the simplest one: do you want to spend time in the company of these people, and do you feel better after you've been with them? That tells you more than anything I can write. Two more things are worth checking, because you can see them for yourself: whether the people around the teacher do what they say they'll do, and whether you can leave without anyone making it hard.

Everyone has blind spots, and a blind spot is where you can't see that you can't see. Your own opinion about yourself comes out of the same head that produced the problem. Guidance is the tool for exactly that. A mentor sees you from the outside and can point at the limit you keep walking into without noticing, the habit that looks to you like your personality and to everyone else like a habit. They can be wrong about a lot of things and still be right about the one thing you can't see. So when feedback comes, it's worth giving it a fair hearing before deciding it doesn't apply to you.

I spent a long time on the wrong side of this. At first I argued with the feedback I got, or took it lightly because I was sure it didn't apply to me. Over time I saw that most of it had been right, and it took me so long because I hadn't taken it seriously when it was given. A lot of it was about attention. In the meditation practices mine was not directed correctly, and during the day it was not focused enough. When that was said, it dawned on me that I had been thinking a lot, that my attention was drifting into thinking far more than into being here, and that my everyday life needed a new level of collectedness. I had been under the impression that I was doing well.

None of this means doing whatever you're told. You keep responsibility for your own life. You can question what you hear, you can propose a better way, and a good mentor welcomes that, because a student who never pushes back isn't learning to think. A mentor is also checked: someone above them can remove them, and a mentor has no business in your private life. And you can leave.

That leaves the obvious question: who checks the teacher? Nobody inside the method does, and I'd rather say so than dodge it. I trust him, and here is why, from my own years as his student. He is a nonstop source of compassion and support, and whenever you talk to him you leave the conversation better than you came into it. I feel very deeply how much he cares about us, his students. Most of his teaching comes down to how we relate to other people, and he is the clearest example of it I know. That trust is why I take his direction as it comes, without testing each piece of it. What I look at instead is results, mine and my pair's and the team's, on the chart from Part one. If the results stop, I'd ask the question out loud.

## 7. Work with attention

This is the part you can start today, alone, with no equipment, and it has three jobs.

The first is to be here. In the body, in this room, with this breath, with no detour through later or through the story about it. Part one called the vertical axis BE, and this is its plainest meaning: how much of you is actually present for what is happening right now. It sounds trivial until you try to keep it for a minute.

The second job is letting go of the tension you've already built up. The wall in Part two lives in the body as clamps, and you can find them: a jaw, a shoulder, the muscles around the eyes. Attention is the tool. You put it on the clamp and keep it there, like a torch held on one spot, and you don't push, because pushing is more tension. Eventually the clamp goes.

Stillness is the simplest form of this: you close your eyes and direct your attention to the sensations in the body. If thoughts arise, you don't push them away. You simply return your attention to the sensations in the body, and you learn to enjoy this process. That is all of it. There is no breath to slow down and nothing to adjust. It's one practice among several, and the others are more active: relaxing a muscle group, reviving a part of the body, holding the breath for a moment, a plain sentence spoken inward toward the center of the chest, laughter when it comes naturally. Nobody needs all of them. Together they are the inner practices, and they are the quietest half of the method. I name them so you know what they are and why they are there. This essay is here to convey ideas, and it isn't a manual. Stillness is the one practice here you can try straight from the page. The others are learned with a person who can see what you're doing, which is a large part of why section 6 exists.

The third job is not to accumulate new tension. This is the one people forget, and it matters most. If you clear your shoulders in the morning and spend the day clenching, you've bailed a boat with the hole still open. Not accumulating mostly comes down to small choices. Go first instead of waiting for the other person. Say the sentence now instead of carrying it. Don't rehearse the argument in the shower. Each of those is a clamp that never forms.

Underneath all three there's a plain skill: seeing your own mind at work. Most of what you do in a day gets triggered, with very little choosing involved. Someone says a thing, something fires, and you've answered before you've decided to. Mental tools for watching that happen, to see the program and not run it automatically, are what turn attention from a relaxation exercise into a way of steering.

Then comes the rule that holds the rest together: regularity. How often you meet with others varies with where you live, and I won't pretend there's one correct timetable. What matters is that it happens, that you keep wanting to grow, and that you carry the method into the rest of your day, in the queue and in the argument, so that the daily sit is the smallest part of it. A person who sits for twenty minutes and then spends the other sixteen hours in the old reflexes has run a very small experiment.

If you already sit, you know the sitting. What this adds is what surrounds it: a pair who notices when your attention has drifted, a mirror that shows you the hooks you can't feel on a cushion, and a mentor who can see what you can't. In my own case, feedback from outside showed me that my attention was drifting into thinking, in my practice and through the day, far more than I had realized.

Part one's warning applies here. Practiced alone and with nothing else, this is exactly the Drifting room. The sitting can feel wonderful while the life underneath stays as it was. The inner practices need something to work on, and that is the first six parts.

## 8. Mirror work and ego work

Part two's fifth step said what happens around you works like a mirror. Someone irritates you, something stings, and it points at a place in you that hasn't been mended. This part takes that seriously as a working rule: I take responsibility for what happens around me.

It sounds like an insult and it's closer to a lever. If I can't control the world and I can control myself, then the only place I can ever change a result is on my side of it. Change my inner state, and the relationship changes, and the result changes with it. If a person hooks me, something in me is not in order. I don't need to work out whose fault it was. I go first, I say sorry first, I take the step in the conflict, and I check what shifted.

None of this means letting people hurt you. When someone harms you, you react, and sometimes the right reaction is to walk away or even fight back. What the mirror asks about is the state the reaction comes from. Do I do it because I see it as the best course of action, or because hate or fear is making the decision for me? The same step away can be a clear choice or a flinch, and from outside they look identical.

It doesn't mean beating yourself up either. Guilt is another clamp. Mirror work is looking, and a person who looks without flinching is in better shape to change than one who is busy punishing themselves. It also doesn't mean telling other people their problems are your mirror. The mirror is something you hold up to yourself, and handing it to someone else is a way of skipping the work.

The clearest case I have is a team member I worked with very closely, and we had regular conflicts. This person was very stubborn and very emotional, and once the emotion rose, nothing I said landed. It happened with many people, but I dealt with this team member more than most. In that period our teacher was trying to get across that if we make the internal decision to change our relationship to another person or to a situation, we can change it almost at once, and that learning to make this internal click is one of the biggest keys to the next step. I decided to take it seriously.

Then we had an enormous fight. I got crazy angry because once again the team member would not understand what I was trying to say, and I said we should stop and go our separate ways for a moment. I sat in another room, livid, asking how I could change my relationship to this and how I could let it go. It was close to prayer. It felt like the lever on a railway track in the cartoons, the one that is supposed to switch the rails and is so rusty it barely moves, except that this lever was inside me. Moving it was one of the hardest things I have done. After about ten minutes it moved.

I went to find the team member to apologize. Calming down quickly after a fight had never been this person's way, and the anger could last for days. But before I had said more than my first word, the team member was in a completely different state. So I learned that the flip is possible and that it works. I did it a few more times over the years, it got easier each time, and it was never that hard again. I haven't been that angry in many years.

That is also what repair looks like: quick and light. A person who has hurt someone and leaves it for a week has added a clamp. A person who says "I'm sorry, that was me" the same afternoon, without an essay, has removed one. The usual suspects behind the hook are pride, offense and the habit of judging, and the work on them has a name, ego work, which is a grand title for noticing them every time they show up.

Nobody can do this well alone, which is why it comes late in the list: you can't see which hooks you have until someone else leans on them.

## 9. How people come to it

None of this was designed by a committee. Students of the same teacher found that the method works better when it is practiced in a certain shape, and the shape is mostly practical: how a newcomer meets the method, who they do it with, and how it fits around a job and a family. Think of it as a good training arrangement, closer to a gym with decent partners than to an organization with a mission.

It starts small, with a talk or a workshop of a few hours, where a person can find out whether any of this rings true. If it does, there is a short retreat of several days, which I think of as days inside the top right room, because the whole day points in one direction and for once nothing in the surroundings pulls the other way. A longer one follows for those who want it. Then comes the step people underrate, a period of adaptation after the retreat, whose whole job is carrying the work home. Without it you go back to the same street, the same people and the same habits, like someone who quit drinking and moved back above the same bar.

The method works wherever a person lives. Many people go back to their own city after this process. The pair forms where they live, with at least two friends in it, a team grows around it, and it is natural that people want to build a community like that in their own place too. That is where the method stops being an event and turns into a way of living. Others choose to spend more time in a center.

The reason for starting small goes back to Part two: light is attractive and darkness is contagious. You can't pull anyone toward it. You can make it easier to approach, and then make sure the first steps can be taken by someone who has a job, a family and a normal amount of doubt. Nobody is asked to give those up. Ordinary life is the training ground, and the pair is what keeps you from sleeping through it.

## 10. The space port

Part two used space travel to show how large a change a person can go through, from a life on the ground to a life among the stars. Nothing gets from the ground to the stars without a place built to launch from, so the last piece of the practical side is a place. I call it a space port.

You can do this work in your own city, with a pair and a team around you, or in a center. The space port is the natural continuation of both: a settlement of people who wish to give their best to this world and to fully realize their potential, and who have chosen to become an integral part of sharing this knowledge with it, as I did.

It is a place where people join who arrive with a great wish and want to go through a rapid conscious development. In practice it is a group of people who live and work together for the benefit of humanity, and each of them unlocks their potential by putting everything in sections one to eight into daily use, and doing it intensively. The wish, the goal and its reason, the service, the team, the guide, the work with attention and the mirror all happen in the same place, every day, with people who see you at breakfast and know exactly how your morning went. A retreat is a few days inside the top right room, and a space port is the same room with a kitchen, a job rota and the occasional argument about whose turn it is.

The reason for a place goes back to Part two again. The environment decides what is easy, and in an ordinary town the easy thing is to forget. In a space port the default of the day points the right way, so working on yourself is the normal thing to do and no longer an extra on top of a full life. People who live and work there are getting ready to be useful to the world they came from, because you leave a space port with a crew and a reason, and nobody builds one to stay.

We, the students of Genadiy, have so far gathered in two centers, with over 120 people between them. The space port still has to be built and run, and the people who help do that are the ones who have chosen this work as their own. We would like to create more centers like these, in different places around the world, so that more people can take part in this kind of work. The settlement is meant to show what a community of conscious people can look like when it is lived at the scale of a town.

## What you can check

After a few months of working in a pair or a team, ask: how many options do you see in a hard moment, and how long does a reaction last? How fast do you say sorry first? Does the pair produce the thing it said it would? Does anything outside you change, and if it doesn't, why not? Put the answers on the chart from Part one, and ask again a month later.

And one question that needs no equipment, to take with you: if you had the energy of a whole team and no direction, what would you do with it? Then ask who in your life could tell you.
