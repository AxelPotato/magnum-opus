Where the room splits

Part one was the polite part. Everything in it holds up whichever way you think the universe works, which is why I could write it without anybody leaving the table. This part is where the room splits, so you’re allowed to disagree with it, and I’ll try to be clear about which steps I can show you and which I can only report.

One thing first, because it changes how you should read everything after it. I’m not religious. None of the traditions I’m about to quote is mine, and I’m not asking you to adopt any of them. What I will do is quote a lot of them: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Jewish, Christian, Muslim, Daoist, Greek, and a few people with no religion at all. They use different words, different stories and sometimes different gods, and they keep saying the same few things. I’m using them the way you’d use witnesses, and not as authorities.

Two ways of reading a life sit at opposite ends of the room. Most people stand somewhere in between without noticing.

The first starts from observation. Look around and write down what you see: a world of things, and underneath them particles, or quantum fields if you want the physicist’s answer. I look in a mirror, see one of those things, and feel like a person: me in here, everybody else out there. Put that together and you get an assumption, which is that matter is the basic stuff, a mind is something a brain does, and consciousness depends on that activity. Followed honestly, it leads to materialism and, if the laws leave no gaps, to determinism, the picture Laplace painted in 1814 of an intellect that knew every force and position and could compute the whole future. When the brain stops, the experiencer stops. This view built hospitals and phones, I live inside it for most of my day, and in my body it feels like the plain truth. Me, Alex, a separate entity. Hello.

The second view sits at the other extreme, and it starts from a different assumption: that we are all one. Everything is part of a greater whole, and that whole is experiencing separation through the individual experience we call our lives. On this reading I’m not a thing inside the world. I’m the world, looking at itself from one position, with the memory of the rest switched off for a while.

Both of these are assumptions. Nobody has observed that matter is all there is. We have observed a lot of patterns and built excellent models of them, and every observation was made through the same mind we’re trying to explain. The first view predicts well, and neuroscience’s working assumption, that experience depends on brain activity, has strong evidence behind it. What nobody has is an accepted account of why that activity feels like something from the inside, which the philosopher David Chalmers named the hard problem in 1995. The second view finds experience easy, since it is the basic ingredient, and has trouble with the opposite question: if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing? That trouble is most of this part.

Why this idea is worth a closer look

Here’s why I think the second view deserves the effort. It isn’t something people say after a long weekend in the mountains. A version of it sits at the base of every religion I know, in its own words.

  • Hinduism: in the Chandogya Upanishad a father tells his son Svetaketu that the finest essence of which the whole world is made is the self, and that he is that (6.8.7).
  • Buddhism: nothing stands alone, and the Huayan school pictured the universe as a net of jewels in which each jewel reflects all the others.
  • Jainism: it counts many souls, alike in nature, and builds its ethic of non-harm on that likeness. Souls render service to one another (Tattvartha Sutra 5.21).
  • Judaism: the Shema, the central prayer, says that the Lord is one (Deuteronomy 6:4), and Chabad Hasidism teaches that from God’s own point of view the world is as nothing before him.
  • Christianity: Jesus prays in John that they may all be one (17:21, ESV), and Paul describes the end of the story as God being all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28).
  • Islam: the Quran says that wherever you turn there is the face of God (2:115), and the Sufi Ibn Arabi called all things “He/not He,” in William Chittick’s rendering.
  • Daoism: the Way gives birth to one, and one gives birth to two (Daodejing 42).

People with no creed have said it too. Spinoza argued that there is only one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that everything else is a form of it. When a rabbi cabled Einstein in 1929 to ask whether he believed in God, Einstein answered that he believed in Spinoza’s God, though he meant the lawful order of nature and nothing that cares what we do. Erwin Schrödinger argued that the plurality of minds is only apparent, tied the idea to the Upanishads, and admitted that at that point he was doing religion and not science. And Carl Sagan, who was about as far from a mystic as a famous person gets, said on television in 1980 that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself.

None of this proves anything, and none of these people holds exactly the view I’ll use. What they share is the base: what looks like many is rooted in one. So I’ll take that as an axiom, the way a mathematician takes one, and see what follows. If it’s right, a set of ideas that people usually file under “spiritual” should come out as logical consequences, one after another, and under each I’ll show you how the traditions put it. That tests coherence and not truth, because plenty of consistent systems are false. But if one coherent chain turns out to be what traditions that grew up apart keep arriving at, I’d call it a sane place to stand. If you’re in the first camp, treat it as a thought experiment. Part one still works.

The axiom and the machine

The axiom has three clauses.

We are one. There is a single whole, and everything is in it.

The whole experiences separation. It looks at itself through individual lives, each one a position with its own point of view, which each of us experiences as “my life.”

The separation ends. Being separate is a phase, and it returns to knowing itself as one.

I’ll hold it with a computer, because Part one admitted that I spent years in cyber security, so I can’t help it. Picture one machine running thousands of programs. Every program gets its own private memory, and each believes it has the whole memory to itself. The operating system maps all that private memory onto the same physical chips, and the hardware enforces the walls, so by default one program can’t read another’s memory. That’s what keeps a random app away from your password manager. The walls are real, though they are an arrangement and not a law: in 2018 the Meltdown and Spectre flaws showed that information could leak across them. And underneath, there is one machine.

Now picture the screen. Two windows sit in opposite corners of the display, and it looks as if there is a distance between them. There is a distance, on the screen. Both windows are computed on the same machine, in the same moment, and the machine has never heard of that distance.

Under the axiom, you and I are programs. “Me over here, you over there” is the screen. My private memory is the self I feel from inside, the border, the felt edge of me that Part one called being braced, is the wall around it, and the whole is the machine that was running both of us all along. It’s a picture, and I’m using it as a picture. I’m not saying the universe is a simulation, or that anyone is running it.

From these three clauses I want to build the rest one step at a time. Each step has to follow from the ones before it, and under each I’ll show you what the traditions say, so you can judge for yourself whether they meant the same thing. Here is the whole chain on one page.

1. Separation is a way of looking

If everything is one, then being separate can’t be a fact about what exists. It has to be a fact about how the whole looks at itself. A whole that looks at itself needs positions to look from, and a position is a separation. Knowing needs a gap. So the separation is real as an experience and misleading as a final description, and that’s what I mean by illusion. The word gets misused, so let me be careful: it doesn’t mean that nothing is there. Take the two windows again. The distance between them is real, and you can measure it with a ruler held to the screen. What’s wrong is the reading, the thought that two separate things sit that far apart inside the machine. The walls between programs work the same way. From inside a program they are completely real, and they still don’t mean the machine is in pieces. Something real, read as more than it is.

Why would a whole do this at all? I can’t prove a reason, and the traditions are modest about it too, but several give answers, and the answers rhyme.

What the traditions say

  • Hinduism calls the appearance maya, and its scholars warn that “illusion” translates the word badly: it means something dependent, neither fully real nor simply unreal. Plato’s prisoners in the cave take shadows for the world, and in the Heart Sutra form is emptiness and emptiness is form, which Buddhists read as saying that things have no fixed independent nature of their own.
  • As for why, Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 calls creation play, lila. The Lurianic Kabbalists of sixteenth century Safed taught that the infinite contracted to make room for a world, that the vessels meant to hold its light shattered, and that gathering the pieces is humanity’s work. Sufis quote a saying, “I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known,” which hadith scholars do not accept as a saying of the Prophet, and the logic is the one I just gave: a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view.
  • Ralph Waldo Emerson, who had left the pulpit, wrote in The Over-Soul in 1841 that we live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles, and that meanwhile within man is the soul of the whole.

2. The wall is held by the body

If the separation is a way of looking, something has to hold it in place, or it would drop away the moment you noticed it. What holds it is the felt edge of me, the wall in the machine, and it turns out to be built from soft material. In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen sat people at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand in plain view, and stroked both with a brush at the same moment. Most people soon felt the touch in the rubber hand, and a lot of them said it felt like their own. What moves is the felt ownership of a hand, which is a lot less than the whole sense of “me,” so I won’t claim it proves the axiom. It shows something smaller: the border of the self, as you feel it, is made of the kind of stuff that can be edited.

The body keeps it in place. This is Part one’s tension, and I can show it to you in ten seconds. Make a fist, a hard one, and tense your arm and shoulder and jaw while you’re at it, and try to feel glad about something. I’ll wait. You’ve turned into a small hard object in a world of other objects: I end here, and everything past this point is other. My teacher Gennady keeps returning to three words in his lectures, tension, resistance and non-acceptance, and I read all three as the wall being maintained. Let go of the fist and the sentence it was saying goes soft. That’s BE, in the language of this part: the amount of wall you are currently holding up.

What the traditions say

  • Buddhism treats the self as built. The belief in a permanent personal self is the first fetter a stream enterer drops, and the self is described as assembled from parts, which is why it can be let go of.
  • Jain teaching describes karma as fine matter that veils the soul’s own natural knowledge, as clouds veil the sun, so the perfection was there all along.
  • The letter to the Ephesians says that people are alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them (4:18). In the Quran (7:172) God asks the descendants of Adam, “Am I not your Lord?”, and Sufis read that moment as the origin the soul has forgotten and longs to return to.

3. One life is too short

The third clause says the separation ends, so the process is a return, from experiencing the world as many to experiencing it as one. Here is the problem. Most people don’t get there. By Part one’s guess, 95 percent of us spend our lives in the Default room, and even the few who reach the top right corner are, by the standard of the axiom, only at the start of the next map. If the process is real and most people don’t finish it in a lifetime, there are two options. Either it fails for most participants, or it takes more than one lifetime. The first option makes the whole a bad designer: a whole that wanted to know itself and rigged the exercise so that almost everyone is unable to finish it. The axiom prefers the second. The run is longer than one life. The argument is older than I am: near the end of The Education of the Human Race, in 1780, Lessing, a thoroughly Enlightenment sort of person, asks why he shouldn’t come back as often as he is capable of acquiring fresh knowledge.

What the traditions say

  • In the Gita, Krishna compares the self to a person putting on new clothes and discarding worn ones (2.22). In the Myth of Er that closes Plato’s Republic, souls choose their next lives and then drink from the river of forgetfulness before they are born, which is one old answer to why you don’t remember the last one.
  • The Buddha says that samsara has no discoverable beginning and that it is not easy to find a being who has not at some time been your mother (Samyutta Nikaya 15). Jain teaching has each soul bound by karma and reborn until freed, Sikh scripture teaches rebirth with liberation by grace, the Kabbalists taught gilgul, and the Druze believe the soul passes directly into a new body.
  • The three big Western religions mostly settled on one life, followed by resurrection and judgment, though not without argument. Even so, they teach that growth doesn’t stop at death: the Catholic Church teaches purgatory, and Gregory of Nyssa taught that the soul’s progress toward God never ends, because God is infinite.

So the traditions are not unanimous, and I’d rather say that than bury it. The axiom needs only that development continues. Some traditions follow that into further lives, and some keep the growth and drop the return.

4. Something carries over

And if the run continues, something has to carry it between lives. In the traditions that use the word, that’s the soul. In the machine it would be the saved state: the program is shut down, the window closes, and what carries over is the account and not the session. Turning it off and on again fixes a surprising number of things, and The IT Crowd made a catchphrase of it. In this picture it’s also the policy on death.

There’s a fair question here: if there is only one mind, who is it that carries over? The Advaita answer is that individuality belongs to the level of appearance, which in the machine is the process and not the processor. So I have two pictures, the saved state at the level of the process and a single witness at the level of the processor, and I’d call it an open question.

What the traditions say

  • In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad the Self is the witness in every being, and there is no other witness but him (3.7.23). Gaudapada, an early teacher of Advaita, goes a step further and says that from the highest standpoint nobody is bound and nobody is freed (Mandukya Karika 2.32).
  • Buddhism holds that there is no fixed soul, and yet that something continues. In the Milindapanha the monk Nagasena compares it to a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same flame nor a different one.
  • The Tibetan Book of the Dead, revealed in the fourteenth century by Karma Lingpa, guides the dying through the bardo, the in-between. Buddhist schools disagreed about whether such a state exists, with some accepting it and the Theravada rejecting it.

I should say plainly that mainstream science has no accepted evidence for survival after death. Ian Stevenson at the University of Virginia, and later Jim Tucker, collected about 2,500 cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives, and critics point to cultural expectation, parental suggestion and selective reporting. I’m not leaning on any of it. This is the step where a rational reader gets to say “unfalsifiable,” and it’s a fair thing to say. Inside the axiom it follows. Outside, it is a claim many people hold on the strength of experience, and I can’t hand you the experience in an essay.

5. Karma, or the fingers

If we are one, there is nobody else. Everyone I could harm is part of the whole I belong to. Hold up your hand. If the index finger and the middle finger start fighting, pressing and scratching, they hurt the hand, and the hand is the only thing either of them can be a part of. Nobody had to appoint a judge. The harm lands on the one doing it, because there was only ever one of us.

That is what I think karma means once you take the courtroom out. The word is Sanskrit for action, and in the classical Hindu and Buddhist teaching it’s a law of consequence, which makes it a different thing from fate. In the machine, a program that corrupts shared memory can end up reading the corrupted data back, and it can’t pretend the failure belongs to somebody else, because it was the same memory all along. And because the thread from the last step runs on, the reading back doesn’t have to arrive in the same life.

What the traditions say

  • The traditions differ on who administers karma. In the Buddhist account nobody does, and the Buddha said that it is intention he calls karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63). In Advaita, karma belongs to the world of apparent individuals, to be transcended by knowledge.
  • The shape turns up elsewhere without any rebirth. Paul writes that whatever a person sows, that will he also reap (Galatians 6:7). The Quran says that if you do good, you do good for yourselves, and if you do evil, you do it to yourselves (17:7, in the Sahih International rendering). The Mishnah says that by the measure a person measures, he is measured (Sotah 1:7).
  • The image of the fingers is old too. Paul gave the church a body with many members and said that if one member suffers, all suffer together (1 Corinthians 12:26). A hadith in Bukhari and Muslim says that the believers are like one body, so that when one limb suffers the rest respond with sleeplessness and fever. Shantideva, the Indian Buddhist, wrote that the hand and the other limbs are many and distinct, yet all are one, and that beings are in the same way all one in wanting happiness (Bodhicaryavatara 8.91).
  • The golden rule is the same logic read forwards: if the other is me, treating them as I want to be treated stops being a nicety and becomes consistency. In the Talmud, Hillel is reported to have said, asked to teach the whole Torah while standing on one foot, that what is hateful to you, you should not do to your neighbor, and that the rest is commentary. Most versions rest on reciprocity or empathy. The Isha Upanishad goes further: the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, recoils from nothing.
  • People with no creed get there too. Marcus Aurelius, an emperor and a Stoic, tells himself that difficult people are his kin, and that we are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. Schopenhauer built his ethics on compassion, the recognition in another person of the same being you are, which he put in the Sanskrit formula tat tvam asi, that art thou. And Wang Yangming wrote in 1527 that the great person forms one body with heaven, earth and all things, and offered as his evidence the alarm you feel at the sight of a child about to fall into a well.

One warning. The cheap version of karma is blame: she had it coming, he reaped what he sowed. That is spiritual bypassing with a calculator, and the axiom doesn’t support it, because the person you’re blaming is as much a part of the whole as you are. The version that follows from the axiom is responsibility: what is my next move toward the part of myself that I’ve been fighting?

6. Letting go, and repair

And what undoes it? Not punishment. Repair, and letting go. The fingers stop fighting, which in Part one’s terms is inner agency: the sorry, the first step in the conflict, the unsaid sentence. And the wall from step two comes down the way it went up, through the body: you loosen what is held. That’s also why you can’t argue a person out of the feeling of separation. A body that holds a belief as tension needs evidence, and the evidence is the experience of letting go.

What the traditions say

  • Hasidism calls it bittul, the nullification of the self, and the Baal Shem Tov read a verse in Psalm 16 as teaching equanimity, so that praise and insult feel the same. The word Islam means submission, and the Sufis speak of fana, the passing away of the self in God, followed by baqa, living on in God.
  • Christians have kenosis, the self-emptying of Philippians 2, and Eckhart’s gelazenheit, letting go, and the Eastern church’s hesychasts taught a stillness joined to the breath.
  • In the Buddha’s instructions on breathing, one step is to breathe in and out contemplating relinquishment (Majjhima Nikaya 118), and in the Sallatha Sutta he says that an untrained person feels two pains, the physical one and the mental one added to it, while a trained disciple feels only the first. Jains practise samayika, a period of equanimity, and kayotsarga, a standing meditation that gives up attachment to the body.
  • The words for repair say the same thing. The Hebrew teshuvah means return, the Arabic tawba means turning back, and the Greek metanoia means a change of mind.

They differ about who or what you let go into, and they give the same instruction: stop holding. I’d call that the door, described from the inside.

7. The return, in stages

Put the chain together and you get a path: loosen the wall, repair the fingers, and over many lives come back to seeing the whole as one. Part one drew a chart, and I’d like to read it again through the axiom, because it says something I couldn’t say then.

Every room on the chart is a separate program trying to run well. Default protects itself. Driven goes out and takes, from the outside, what it can’t feel on the inside. Drifting steps away from whatever pokes it and calls the stepping away peace. High agency does all of it well: it acts, it is honest and kind, and most of the fear is gone. It’s the best version of a separate program that I know how to describe. That is the small square: the area available to a separate self when it gets as good as it can get at being separate.

The big square is something else. It begins when the separation itself stops being assumed, and not merely when the program improves. You can optimize a program for years and it stays a program, behind its own wall. At some point the question stops being how to run better and becomes whether the wall is real. In my old field that move is called a sandbox escape. It pays a bounty and ruins the vendor’s afternoon, and I like the term because it names the right kind of move: a different relationship to the walls, which no amount of polishing the sandbox gives you. This also puts Part one’s gate in a new light. A reason that is about you can’t carry you out, because the reason is the thing you’re trying to leave. It takes a reason bigger than yourself, and the axiom says why: you’re asking the program to act on behalf of the machine.

Our school has a scale for this, which it calls the arrow of consciousness, and it runs from minus four to plus four. It is the same diagonal you’ve been looking at since Part one. The bottom left of the small square is the low end. The door, in the middle, is zero. The top right corner is plus one. Everything past it lives outside the small square, in the zoomed-out map from the end of Part one: plus two, plus three and plus four, and past plus four my teacher Gennady describes a new stage of evolution that he calls birth.

I’ve found one picture that carries the whole scale, and it’s about speed. The measure is how freely you can move through space, and what holds you back is gravity. Under the axiom, moving to a place means being able to experience from another position, so the speed is how many points of view of the whole you can be at home in. Gravity is the pull of the one you started with.

At the low end, in Default, you can run, ride a bike and drive a car. Those are real speeds, but you move along the ground, so the ground decides where you go. Up to zero, people learn to fly: any point on the globe within about a day, and roads stop mattering. After zero, in the high agency half, you can orbit the Earth. At the altitude of the International Space Station, about 400 kilometers up, gravity is still about 88 percent as strong as on the ground. The astronauts float because they are falling around the planet at about 7.7 kilometers per second and missing it, once every ninety minutes or so. They are free of the ground and not free of the Earth. That’s how I’d describe the high agency room: light, quick, able to reach anywhere on the planet, and still inside the field of the separate self.

The top right corner of the small square is escape velocity, about 11.2 kilometers per second from the surface and about 10.8 at the height of the space station. Notice how little extra it takes: at any height, escape speed is about 41 percent higher than orbital speed there, and the difference in outcome is total, since in the ideal case one speed circles forever and the other goes. Part one’s math said that effectiveness is roughly BE times DO, and the corner is the maximum of that product, 100 times 100. Don’t quote me, but I like that the geometry of the small square and the physics of rockets agree on where the threshold is.

Past it, each step is a new order of freedom. Plus two is the solar system. Plus three is the galaxy, about 100,000 light-years across. Plus four is every point in the universe, which is about 93 billion light-years across as far as we can see, so the word every is doing some work. In the axiom’s terms, each step is a wider piece of the whole that you can be at home in, and at plus four no point in the universe is somewhere else.

After plus four, my teacher describes a new stage, which he calls birth. I’ll say what I can responsibly say. It’s a stage, so the arrow isn’t the end of the story. Many traditions have an image of a second birth for a decisive change in a person, and the old maps were scale maps as well.

What the traditions say

  • In John’s gospel Jesus says that one must be born again, or born from above, since the Greek word can mean either. In the Hindu tradition the twice-born are those who have received the initiation that counts as a second birth. Meister Eckhart taught that God is born in the ground of the soul, among the teachings that led to an inquiry for heresy. The traditions use the image for different things, and using it for the stage after the top of a scale is our school’s own extension of a shared image.
  • In Dante’s Paradiso the pilgrim rises through the heavens without noticing, and Beatrice tells him there is nothing to marvel at, since it would be stranger if he stayed low, like a flame sitting still on the ground. Jain teaching has a liberated soul rise by its own nature, like a gourd released from its clay, to the summit of the universe.
  • Other traditions draw staged maps too, so the scale isn’t unique to our school. Early Buddhism has four stages from stream entry to the arahant. The Jain tradition has fourteen, the gunasthanas, a path said to take many lifetimes, on which a soul can also fall back. Teresa of Avila described seven mansions of the inner castle, the last of which is the spiritual marriage.

Beyond that I’m at the edge of what I can say. Some physicists speculate that our universe is one of many, an idea nobody has confirmed and a hard one to test, and if so even plus four may be a stage and not an ending. In Kerbal Space Program, reaching orbit feels like winning, right up until you notice the Mun. One caution about the numbers: the arrow is our school’s scale, and I use it because it’s the one I know. Treat the labels as labels. The picture is what carries the point.

Why I want this

This is the goal I hinted at in Part one’s zoom out, and it’s the one Anna and I have for our lifetime: the whole map, not the corner of the chart. The reason is simple. I want to be useful, with nothing standing between me and whoever is in front of me. In the axiom’s terms, that’s a program that has stopped managing its walls and can look at the person in front of it as another part of the same machine.

I’d ask you to use on me the two questions Part one gave you for testing a reason. Would I still do it if nobody ever found out? And does it make me gentler with the person in front of me, or does that person turn into material for the cause? The first is hard to check, since I’m writing this somewhere people can read it. The second you can check better than I can.

People ask two things at this point. The first: isn’t this just becoming a vegetable? Part one met that version, the person who watches their whole life from the side and calls it calm. The feeling is one channel, and you can’t dam half a river, so a person who cuts the channel to feel less pain feels less of everything. Take the border away and everything comes through, the hard things included. The second: how do you know it exists? I don’t, not the way I know Paris exists. I know the direction, because each step toward it shows up in things I can check on this chart, and I’ve met people who are further along, and a room settles when they walk in. Beyond that, I’d rather spend my life walking toward something I’m not sure of than sitting in a room I’m sure I hate.

So the honest summary is that I don’t know if I’ll get there, and I know the direction.

What you can check

If the axiom is right, loosening the wall should leave marks you can check, and the first ones are on the chart. How many options do you see in a hard moment? How long does a reaction last? How quickly do you say sorry first, even when it costs you? When did you last do something nobody asked for, for somebody else’s good? The school I belong to has watched this change for a long time, and the order people describe is consistent: first a person starts to listen, as attention leaves the self it was protecting and lands on somebody else; then giving shows up, unasked and without a tally; then perception clears. That’s observation and not a study, and I’d weigh it that way.

The prediction is the interesting part. If the sense of separation is held by tension, then loosening it might make a person more generous without anyone telling them to be. The nearest lab results are mixed. In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how “one” people felt with someone in need predicted their willingness to help better than sympathy did, and other researchers dispute the reading. Feeling safe raised compassion and helping in some experiments, and a short breathing practice lowered the urge to make amends in others. Nobody has tested letting go of tension as such. It’s an experiment you can run on yourself, which is the nice thing about this kind of claim.

Where this goes next

That’s the map as far as I can draw it honestly. What I haven’t told you is how to walk it, and that is the next part, which has a lot less philosophy in it and a lot more schedule. The short version is that it uses stillness and work together, every day. The work shows you where you’re still braced, the stillness lets it go, and the energy that comes back goes into the work. That alternation is the shrinking swing from Part one, put on a timetable.

Until then, one question that needs no equipment. Where, right now, does the border of you sit? Don’t quote me, but I’d bet it isn’t where you think.
