Where the room splits

Part one was the polite part. Everything in it holds up whichever way you think the universe works, which is why I could write it without anybody leaving the table. This part is where the room splits, so I’d like to say up front that you’re allowed to disagree with it, and that I’ll try to be clear about which steps I can show you and which I can only report.

One thing before the two views, because it changes how you should read everything after it. I’m not religious. None of the traditions I’m about to quote is mine, and I’m not asking you to adopt any of them. What I will do is quote a lot of them: Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Daoist, Greek, and a few more. My claim is that when you lay them side by side, they speak of the same things. They use different words, different stories and sometimes different gods. The overlap is the point of this part. I’m using the traditions the way you’d use witnesses, and not as authorities.

Two ways of reading a life sit at opposite ends of the room. Most people stand somewhere in between without noticing.

The first starts from observation. Look around and write down what you see. A world of things, tables and bodies and planets, and underneath them particles, or quantum fields if you want the exact physicist’s answer. Billions of people, each inside a skull with a point of view on the far side of it. I look in a mirror, see one of those people, and feel like one: me in here, everybody else out there. Put those observations together and you get an assumption, which is that matter is the basic stuff, a mind is something a brain does, and consciousness depends on that activity. Followed honestly, the assumption leads to materialism, and, if you also believe the laws leave no gaps, to determinism, the picture Laplace painted in 1814 of an intellect that knew every force and position and could therefore compute the whole future. In its standard form quantum mechanics adds some dice to this, which gives you randomness and not freedom, though some interpretations keep the whole picture deterministic. When the brain stops, the experiencer stops. Free will is a story the brain tells, and meaning is something we make up, which we’re allowed to do, since nobody else is going to.

I’m not being sarcastic. This view built hospitals and phones, I live inside it for most of my day, and in my body it feels like the plain truth. Me, Alex, a separate entity. Hello.

The second view sits at the other extreme, and it starts from a different assumption: that we are all one. Everything is part of a greater whole, and that whole is experiencing separation through the individual experience we call our lives. On this reading I’m not a thing inside the world. I’m the world, looking at itself from one position, with the memory of the rest switched off for a while.

Both of these are assumptions, and I want to be careful about that, because everything else in this part depends on it. Nobody has observed that matter is all there is. We have observed a lot of patterns and built excellent models of them, and every one of those observations was made through the same mind we’re trying to explain, which makes it a poor judge in its own case. The first view is strong at prediction. Most neuroscientists work on the assumption that conscious experience depends on activity in the brain, and the evidence for that dependence is strong. What nobody has is an accepted account of why that activity feels like something from the inside, which the philosopher David Chalmers named the hard problem in 1995. The second view finds experience easy, since it is the basic ingredient, and has trouble with the opposite question: if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing? Most of this part is about that trouble.

The second view is less exotic than it sounds. Spinoza argued that there is only one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that everything else is a form of it. When a rabbi cabled Einstein in 1929 to ask whether he believed in God, Einstein answered that he believed in Spinoza’s God, though he meant the lawful order of nature and nothing that cares what we do. Erwin Schrödinger, who has a Nobel Prize in physics and an equation named after him, argued in 1944, and again in his 1956 lectures, that the plurality of minds is only apparent, and he tied the idea to the Upanishads. In those lectures he wrote that the overall number of minds is just one, and he added that at that point he was taking religion and not science, a religion he said was not opposed to science.

Closer to our time, the computer engineer turned philosopher Bernardo Kastrup argues that reality is one mind and that individual lives are like dissociated parts of it, and critics ask why and how a single mind would split. The cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman argues from evolution that perception works like the icons on a desktop, a useful interface and not the machinery underneath. Most scientists accept that perception is tuned for usefulness, and many reject his stronger conclusion. None of this proves anything, and none of these people holds exactly the view I’ll use. I mention them so you know the second view isn’t only something people say after a long weekend in the mountains.

So here is what I’m going to do. I’ll take the second view as an axiom, the way a mathematician takes one, and see what follows from it. The question is whether the main ideas of the world’s religions come out as logical consequences, one after another. That tests coherence and not truth, because plenty of consistent systems are false. But if one coherent system turns out to be what independent traditions reached separately, in different vocabularies, I’d call it at least a sane place to stand. If you’re in the first camp, treat this as a thought experiment. Part one still works.

The axiom and the machine

The axiom has three clauses.

We are one. There is a single whole, and everything is in it.

The whole experiences separation. It looks at itself through individual lives, each one a position with its own point of view, which each of us experiences as “my life.”

The separation ends. Being separate is a phase, and it returns to knowing itself as one.

None of this is new. The oldest statement of it that I know sits in the Upanishads. In the Chandogya, a father tells his son Svetaketu that the finest essence of which the whole world is made is the self, and that he is that. The Brihadaranyaka says that in the beginning this world was only Brahman, which knew only itself, thought “I am Brahman,” and so became the whole.

Several traditions say something near it in their own words. Chabad Hasidism, founded by Shneur Zalman of Liadi, teaches that from God’s own point of view the world is as nothing before him and has no independent existence, while it is real from ours, which is close to the processor’s view of the two windows. A saying in the Zohar has the Torah, the Holy One and Israel as one, which its commentators read as a union reached through the Torah and God’s will. The Quran says that wherever you turn there is the face of God (2:115) and that God is with you wherever you are (57:4), and the great Sufi Ibn Arabi called all things “He/not He,” in William Chittick’s rendering, a self-disclosure of God that is also not God. Meister Eckhart preached that the eye with which he sees God is the same eye with which God sees him, a line that was raised against him in Cologne in 1326. The Huayan school of Buddhism pictured the universe as a net of jewels in which each jewel reflects all the others. The Christian version is gentler. Paul writes that at the end Christ hands the kingdom to the Father so that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28), and Origen and Gregory of Nyssa read that as the restoration of all things.

People with no creed to defend have said it too. Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in The Over-Soul in 1841 that we live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles, and that meanwhile within man is the soul of the whole. And in the first episode of Cosmos in 1980, Carl Sagan, who was about as far from a mystic as a famous person gets, said that we are made of star-stuff and that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself.

I’ll explain it with a computer, because Part one admitted that I spent years in cyber security, so I can’t help it. Picture one machine running thousands of programs. Every program gets its own private memory, and each believes it has the whole memory to itself. The operating system maps all that private memory onto the same physical chips, and the hardware enforces the walls, so by default one program can’t read another’s memory. That’s process isolation, and it’s what keeps a random app from reading your password manager. The walls are real. Inside the system they are as solid as anything gets, though they are an arrangement and not a law: in 2018 the Meltdown and Spectre flaws showed that information could leak across them. And underneath, there is one machine.

Now picture the screen. Two windows sit in opposite corners of the display, and it looks as if there is a distance between them. There is a distance, on the screen. The computation for both windows happens on the same machine, in the same moment, and the machine has never heard of that distance. It’s a property of how things get drawn, and two windows side by side may even belong to programs that have nothing to do with each other.

Under the axiom, you and I are programs. “Me over here, you over there” is the screen. My private memory is the self I feel from inside, the border, the felt edge of me that Part one called being braced, is the wall around it, and the whole is the machine that was running both of us all along.

A quick word on “illusion,” because the word gets misused. It doesn’t mean that nothing is there. Take the two windows again. The distance between them is real: hold a ruler to the screen and you can measure it. What’s wrong is the reading, the thought that two separate things sit that far apart inside the machine. The distance is true on the screen and tells you nothing about how the machine is built. The walls between programs work the same way. From inside a program they are completely real, and they still don’t mean the machine is in pieces. That’s what I mean by illusion: something real, read as more than it is.

One more thing about the computer. It’s a picture, and I’m using it as a picture. I’m not saying the universe is a simulation, or that anyone is running it. It’s the best way I know to show how one thing can look like many without being many, and I’ll keep coming back to it.

The same things in different words

If we take the axiom seriously, a chain of ideas follows from it, and the chain has a shape you will recognize from religion: what is real, who we are, what happens to us, how we should live. The same few ideas turn up in traditions that grew up in different places, centuries and languages, many of them with no contact at all, and now and then in people who weren’t religious in the slightest. They use different words, which from far away looks like disagreement and from close up often looks like translation.

The old traditions noticed this about each other. A verse in the Rigveda (1.164.46) says that what is real is one and that the poets give it many names, Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni. In the Buddhist Udana the Buddha tells of blind men who feel an elephant and quarrel, as an image of sectarians who each hold one side. Jain thinkers later used the story for many-sidedness, and Rumi retells it with an elephant in a dark room. Rumi also has a story in the Masnavi of four men who are given one coin and fall out over what to buy, because each names the grape in his own language, angur, inab, uzum and istafil, and a man who spoke all four languages could have settled it. In the nineteenth century Ramakrishna pictured the faiths as the different landing steps of one lake, where Hindus say jal, Muslims say pani and Christians say water. I should be fair to these. The Rigveda verse is a riddle about gods, and the Buddha told his story to people who clung to their views, so none of them was written to say that all religions are one. I’m borrowing the pictures, and I’m saying so.

A word on where I stand, since somebody will ask. The nearest thing to my views among organized systems is Theosophy, the movement founded in New York in 1875 around Helena Blavatsky. I find its structure decent: one source, a long return, many lives, consequences that come back to the one who acts. I leave out the ascended masters and the parts that drift further off, and I think it carries more mysticism than I like, so I won’t be quoting it. For witnesses I’ll go to the religions themselves, and to a few people who weren’t religious at all, and you can read every one of them yourself.

So here is the claim of this part, and it is smaller than it sounds. I’m not trying to prove anything. I’m putting examples next to each other and asking whether they fit. The same few ideas keep turning up in different vocabularies: a self that is built and can end, a veil that lifts, a return, something that continues and grows across lives or beyond death, consequences that come back to the one who acts, and a way that runs through letting go. The Upanishads say it with atman and Brahman, the Buddha with emptiness and dependent arising, the Jains with the soul and the karma that veils it, the Lurianic Kabbalists with the shattered vessels and their repair, the Sufis with the passing away of the self, and the Christian mystics with union with God. I’m not saying these traditions reasoned their way here from an axiom. In history the ideas came first, and the axiom is a way of seeing why they fit together. Where a tradition’s everyday teaching doesn’t mention an idea, I’ll quote its mystics, its closely read scripture or its different vocabulary, and I’ll say which. Here is the whole thing on one page.

Why separation feels so real

The first consequence is the obvious one: if we are one, the separation we experience is an illusion, in the sense I gave it. Several traditions have a word for it, and the words are not interchangeable. Advaita Vedanta calls it maya, and scholars of Advaita warn that illusion translates the word badly, since it means something dependent, neither fully real nor simply unreal. It calls the ignorance that keeps it going avidya, a word Buddhism uses too, as the first link in the chain that keeps suffering going. Plato’s prisoners in the cave take shadows for the world. Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, woke up, and couldn’t tell whether he was a man who had dreamed of a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming it was a man, though the text adds that there must be some distinction, which it calls the transformation of things. In Genesis, the couple eat from the tree of knowledge, notice that they are exposed, and hide from the one they’d been walking with in the garden. The Heart Sutra says that form is emptiness and emptiness is form, which Buddhists read as saying that things have no fixed independent nature of their own: the Buddhist way of saying that the walls between us are not made of anything solid. Buddhism uses other words than the Upanishads for what is left when the ignorance ends, and the move is the same one, the veil coming off.

The other traditions describe the veil in their own terms. In Buddhism the belief in a permanent personal self is the first fetter a stream enterer drops, and the self is treated as something assembled from parts, which is why it can be let go of. Jain teaching describes karma as fine matter that veils the soul’s own natural knowledge, as clouds veil the sun, so that the perfection was there all along. In the Hasidic tradition the Baal Shem Tov is reported, through a disciple, to have taught that the hiding of God’s face is only apparent, a test of how we respond. In the Quran (7:172) God asks the descendants of Adam, “Am I not your Lord?”, and Sufis read that moment as the origin the soul has forgotten and longs to return to. The letter to the Ephesians says that people are alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them (4:18). And the Hymn of the Pearl, a poem inside the Syriac Acts of Thomas that many read as a Gnostic myth of the soul, tells it as a story: a prince is sent to Egypt to fetch a pearl, forgets his origin, falls asleep, and is woken by a letter from home.

The question I care about is why it feels so solid. In the machine, the wall is real inside the system. A program can’t read another program’s memory, so from inside, “I” and “you” are as different as two things can be. The feeling of separation is an accurate report of a local fact, this is my memory and that is yours, read as if it were a fact about the whole machine. That’s the difference between how we experience our lives and the claim that we are one: the screen against the processor.

It also turns out that the wall is built from soft material. In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen sat people at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand in plain view, and stroked both with a brush at the same moment. Most people soon felt the touch in the rubber hand, and a lot of them said it felt like their own, and a later study of the moving-hand version timed it at about half a minute on average. A video version with a person’s own back appeared in 2007 (Lenggenhager and colleagues). The philosopher Thomas Metzinger argues that the felt self is a model the brain builds of the body and then can’t see as a model, and in 2009 he and Olaf Blanke tied that idea to full-body illusions like this one. Two caveats. What moves in these experiments is the felt ownership of a hand or the location of a body, which is a lot less than the whole sense of “me,” and some studies find that how strongly people report the illusion goes with how suggestible they are, a link other researchers dispute. So I won’t claim that it proves the axiom. What it shows is smaller and I think useful: the border of the self, as you feel it, is made of the kind of stuff that can be edited.

The body keeps it in place. This is Part one’s tension, and it’s the part of the story I can show you in ten seconds. Make a fist, a hard one, and tense your arm and shoulder and jaw while you’re at it, and try to feel glad about something. I’ll wait. You’ve turned into a small hard object in a world of other objects: I end here, and everything past this point is other. My teacher Gennady keeps returning to three words in his lectures, tension, resistance and non-acceptance, and I read all three as the wall being maintained. Tension holds the outline in the muscles. Resistance pushes against what’s here. Non-acceptance refuses what has already happened. Let go of the fist and the sentence it was saying goes soft. That’s BE, in the language of this part: the amount of wall you are currently holding up. It also explains why you can’t argue a person out of the feeling. A body that holds a belief as tension needs evidence, and the evidence is the experience of letting go.

The traditions agree about the method too, and it is Part one’s: letting go. Hasidism calls it bittul, the nullification of the self, and the Baal Shem Tov read a verse in Psalm 16 as teaching equanimity, so that praise and insult feel the same. The word Islam means submission, and the Sufis speak of the passing away of the self in God. Christians have kenosis, the Greek word in Philippians for emptying oneself, and Eckhart’s gelazenheit, letting go, and the Eastern church’s hesychasts taught a stillness joined to the breath. In the Buddha’s instructions on breathing, one step is to breathe in and out contemplating relinquishment, and in the Sallatha Sutta he says that an untrained person feels two pains, the physical one and the mental one added to it, while a trained disciple feels only the first. Jains practise samayika, a period of equanimity, and kayotsarga, a standing meditation that gives up attachment to the body. They differ about who or what you let go into, and they give the same instruction: stop holding.

Why would the whole do this at all? Several traditions give an answer. Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 meets the question of why a complete God would create by calling creation play, lila. The Kabbalists of sixteenth century Safed taught that the infinite contracted to make room for a world, that the vessels meant to hold its light shattered, and that gathering the pieces is humanity’s work, a story of fragmentation and repair. Plotinus described a procession of the One into multiplicity and a return. Sufis quote a saying, “I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known,” which hadith scholars do not accept as a saying of the Prophet and which Ibn Arabi called sound by mystical unveiling, and not by a chain of transmission. My reading of all four is that a whole that wants to know itself can’t do it from nowhere. It needs a point of view, and a point of view is a separation. Knowing needs a gap. I can’t prove that this is why, and the traditions themselves are modest about it.

Not enough time in one life

The third clause says the separation ends, so the process is a return, from experiencing the world as many to experiencing it as one. Here is the problem. Look around. Most people don’t reach it. By Part one’s guess, 95 percent of us spend our lives in the Default room, and even the few who reach the top right corner are, by the standard of the axiom, only at the start of the next map. The chain only works if you grant that the aim is unity, which is the third clause of the axiom, and I haven’t argued for it. I’m following the chain, and not claiming to have proved the first link.

If the process is real and most people don’t finish it in a lifetime, there are two options. Either the process fails for most participants, or it takes more than one lifetime. The first option makes the whole a bad designer: a whole that wanted to know itself and rigged the exercise so that almost everyone is unable to finish it. The axiom prefers the second option. The run is longer than one life. The argument is older than I am: near the end of The Education of the Human Race, in 1780, Lessing, a thoroughly Enlightenment sort of person, asks why he shouldn’t come back as often as he is capable of acquiring fresh knowledge.

And if the run continues, something has to continue with it between lives. In the traditions that use the word, that’s the soul. In the machine it would be the saved state: the program is shut down, the window closes, and what carries over is the account and not the session. Turning it off and on again fixes a surprising number of things, and The IT Crowd made a catchphrase of it. In this picture it’s also the policy on death.

Look at how many traditions arrive here. In the Hindu texts the self that moves from body to body is the atman, and the Bhagavad Gita compares it to a person putting on new clothes and discarding worn ones. In Plato’s Phaedo, Socrates gives several arguments for the survival of the soul on his last day, and in the Myth of Er that closes the Republic, souls choose their next lives and then drink from the river of forgetfulness before they are born, which is one old answer to why you don’t remember the last one. Buddhism has a subtler version. It holds that there is no fixed soul, and yet that something continues, and in the Milindapanha the monk Nagasena compares it to a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same flame nor a different one. In the Samyutta Nikaya the Buddha says that samsara has no discoverable beginning and that it is not easy to find a being who has not at some time been your mother. Buddhist schools disagreed about whether there is a state between lives, with some accepting one and the Theravada rejecting it, and the Tibetan Book of the Dead, revealed in the fourteenth century by Karma Lingpa, guides the dying through the bardo, the in-between. Its English title and framing come from W. Y. Evans-Wentz’s 1927 edition, shaped by his own Theosophical background. Kabbalists taught gilgul, the turning of souls from life to life, though rationalists such as Saadia Gaon rejected it. Jain teaching has each soul bound by karma and reborn until freed, Sikh scripture teaches rebirth with liberation by grace, and the Druze believe that at death the soul passes directly into a new body.

The three big Western religions mostly settled on one life, followed by resurrection and judgment, though not without argument. Origen taught that souls exist before birth but rejected the transmigration of souls, and the sixth century condemnations of Origenism went after pre-existence and the final restoration of all, and not reincarnation as such. Muslim theologians who discussed tanasukh mostly rejected it, though some minority groups accepted it, and the Quran (23:99 to 100) has the dying ask to be sent back and be refused. So the traditions are not unanimous, and I’d rather say that than bury it.

Even the traditions that don’t teach rebirth teach that growth does not stop at death. The Catholic Church teaches purgatory, a purification after death for those who die in grace but are not yet fully purified, and the Orthodox pray for the dead and teach an intermediate state. Gregory of Nyssa taught that the soul’s progress toward God never ends, because God is infinite, a teaching scholars call epektasis, after a word of Paul’s. The Mishnah records Rabbi Akiva saying that the judgment of the wicked in Gehinnom lasts twelve months. The Persian philosopher Mulla Sadra taught that the soul is bodily in its origin and spiritual in its later life and keeps developing after death, while rejecting rebirth into this world. The axiom needs only that development continues. Some traditions follow that into further lives, and some keep the growth and drop the return.

There’s also a fair question that the axiom has to face: if there is only one mind, who is it that carries over? The Advaita answer is that individuality belongs to the level of appearance, which in the machine is the process and not the processor. In the Brihadaranyaka the Self is the witness in every being, and there is no other witness but him (3.7.23). Gaudapada, an early teacher of Advaita, goes a step further and says that from the highest standpoint nobody is bound and nobody is freed (Mandukya Karika 2.32). So I have two pictures, the saved state at the level of the process and a single witness at the level of the processor, and I’d call it an open question.

I should say plainly that mainstream science has no accepted evidence for survival after death. There is a research literature. Ian Stevenson at the University of Virginia published Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation in 1966 and went on, with his colleagues, to collect about 2,500 cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives, and Jim Tucker continued with Life Before Life in 2005. Critics point to cultural expectation, parental suggestion, selective reporting and testimony gathered after the fact, and most of the cases come from cultures that already believe in rebirth. Carl Sagan listed it among the few claims in that territory that deserved serious study, and said he doubted it would hold up. I’m not leaning on it. This part is about what follows from an axiom, and a conclusion shouldn’t borrow credibility from a disputed experiment.

This is the step where a rational reader gets to say “unfalsifiable,” and it’s a fair thing to say. Inside the axiom it follows. Outside, it is a claim that many people hold on the strength of experience, and I can’t hand you the experience in an essay.

Karma, or the fingers

If we are one, there is nobody else. Everyone I could harm is part of the whole I belong to. Hold up your hand. If the index finger and the middle finger start fighting, pressing and scratching, they hurt the hand, and the hand is the only thing either of them can be a part of. Nobody had to appoint a judge. The harm lands on the one doing it, because there was only ever one of us.

That is what I think karma means once you take the courtroom out. The word is Sanskrit for action, and in the classical Hindu and Buddhist teaching it’s a law of consequence, which makes it a different thing from fate. The traditions differ on who, if anyone, administers it. In the Buddhist account nobody does, and the Buddha said that it is intention he calls karma. In the theistic schools of Hinduism God has a role, and in Advaita karma belongs to the world of apparent individuals, to be transcended by knowledge. In the axiom, the idea comes out as a consequence: what I do to another part of the whole, I do to the whole, and the whole is me, so it comes around. In the machine, a program that corrupts shared memory will eventually read the corrupted data back, and it can’t pretend the failure belongs to somebody else, because it was the same memory all along.

Several traditions have this structure, though few spell it out as geometry. Paul writes that whatever a person sows, that will he also reap, which is accountability under God, with no rebirth in it, though the shape is the same. The Quran says that if you do good, you do good for yourselves, and if you do evil, you do it to yourselves (17:7, in the Sahih International rendering), and that whoever does an atom’s weight of good or evil will see it (99:7 and 99:8). The Mishnah says that by the measure a person measures, he is measured (Sotah 1:7), and a saying attributed to Ben Azzai holds that one commandment draws another and one transgression draws another (Avot 4:2).

The image of the fingers is old too. Paul gave the church a body with many members and said that if one member suffers, all suffer together (1 Corinthians 12:26). A hadith in Bukhari and Muslim says that the believers are like one body, so that when one limb suffers the rest respond with sleeplessness and fever. Shantideva, the Indian Buddhist, wrote that the hand and the other limbs are many and distinct, yet all are one, a body to be guarded, and that beings are in the same way all one in wanting happiness (Bodhicaryavatara 8.91). Chabad’s Tanya grounds love for a fellow Jew in the claim that all Jewish souls share one root in God. The Jain verse of forgiveness says “I forgive all beings, may all beings forgive me,” and Jainism builds its ethic of non-harm on the equality of souls. Even its oldest text on conduct, the Acharanga, tells the reader that the person you intend to hurt is as you are, and one modern commentator reads the line as the oneness of the killer and the killed, though Hermann Jacobi’s older translation puts it more carefully, and Jain doctrine holds the souls to be many. Most of these speak first of a community, the church, the believers, Israel, and Shantideva reaches compassion by arguing that suffering has no owner, which is a different route to the same place. The circle widens in the later readings. I’m pointing at the shape of the idea.

Outside the traditions, people with no creed have reasoned their way to the same picture. Marcus Aurelius, an emperor and a Stoic, opens the second book of his Meditations by telling himself that he will meet difficult people today and that none of them can really harm him, because they are his kin, and we are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. Arthur Schopenhauer built his ethics on compassion, which he described as recognizing in another person the same being you are, and he put it in the Sanskrit formula tat tvam asi, that art thou. And in 1527 the Chinese philosopher Wang Yangming wrote that the great person forms one body with heaven, earth and all things, and offered as his evidence the alarm you feel at the sight of a child about to fall into a well, an example he took from Mencius.

Then comes the golden rule, which is the same logic read forwards. If the other is me, treating them as I want to be treated stops being a nicety and becomes consistency. Nearly every tradition has it. Leviticus says to love your neighbor as yourself, and in the Talmud Hillel is reported to have said, asked to teach the whole Torah while standing on one foot, that what is hateful to you, you should not do to your neighbor, and that the rest is commentary. Jesus gives the positive form in the Sermon on the Mount. A saying reported in Bukhari and Muslim has the Prophet say that none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself. Confucius names reciprocity as the one word for conduct in Analects 15.24, numbered 15.23 in older translations, and the Mahabharata and the Buddhist Udanavarga have it as well. Most of these rest on reciprocity or empathy, and a few go further. The Isha Upanishad says that the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, recoils from nothing, and asks what delusion or sorrow can remain for the one who sees oneness. That is the ethic of oneness in its strongest form, and the axiom is a short explanation of why reciprocity works. The rabbis had a version of this argument. Rabbi Akiva said that love your neighbor as yourself is the great principle of the Torah, and Ben Azzai answered that the verse about the generations of Adam is greater, since it shows that everyone descends from one man. Even a lab has touched it. In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how “one” people felt with a person in need predicted their willingness to help better than sympathy did, though other researchers dispute the reading.

Two warnings. The cheap version of karma is blame: she had it coming, he reaped what he sowed. That is spiritual bypassing with a calculator, and the axiom doesn’t support it, because the person you’re blaming is as much a part of the whole as you are. The version that follows from the axiom is responsibility: what is my next move toward the part of myself that I’ve been fighting?

And what undoes it? Not punishment. Repair. The fingers stop fighting. In Part one’s terms that’s inner agency, the sorry, the first step in the conflict, the unsaid sentence. The traditions have words for it: the Hebrew teshuvah means return, the Arabic tawba means turning back, and the Greek metanoia in the New Testament means a change of mind. Return, turn, change of mind. I’d call that the door, described from the inside.

The small square and the big one

Part one drew a chart, and I’d like to read it again through the axiom, because it says something I couldn’t say then.

Every room on the chart is a separate program trying to run well. Default protects itself. Driven goes out and takes, from the outside, what it can’t feel on the inside, which is mostly approval. Drifting steps away from whatever pokes it and calls the stepping away peace. High agency does all of it well: it acts, it is honest and kind, and most of the fear is gone. It’s the best version of a separate program that I know how to describe. That is what the small square is: the area available to a separate self when it gets as good as it can get at being separate.

The big square is something else. It begins when the separation itself stops being assumed, and not merely when the program improves. You can optimize a program for years and it stays a program, in its own memory, behind its own wall. At some point the question stops being how to run better and becomes whether the wall is real. In my old field that move is called a sandbox escape. It pays a bounty and ruins the vendor’s afternoon, and I like the term because it names the right kind of move: a different relationship to the walls, which no amount of polishing the sandbox gives you.

This also puts Part one’s gate in a new light. A reason that is about you can’t carry you out, because the reason is the thing you’re trying to leave. It takes a reason bigger than yourself to get across the ring, and the axiom says why: you’re asking the program to act on behalf of the machine.

The arrow

Our school has a scale for this, which it calls the arrow of consciousness, and it runs from minus four to plus four. It is the same diagonal you’ve been looking at since Part one. The bottom left of the small square is the low end. The door, in the middle, is zero. The top right corner is plus one. Everything past it lives outside the small square, in the zoomed-out map from the end of Part one: plus two, plus three and plus four, and at plus four my teacher Gennady describes a new stage of evolution that he calls birth.

I’ve found one picture that carries the whole scale, and it’s about speed. The measure is how freely you can move through space, and what holds you back is gravity. Under the axiom, moving to a place means being able to experience from another position, so the speed is how many points of view of the whole you can be at home in. Gravity is the pull of the one you started with.

At the low end, in Default, you can run, ride a bike and drive a car. Those are real speeds, and you can get a lot done at them, but you move along the ground, so the ground decides where you go. People who go a bit further, up to zero, learn to fly. An aircraft takes you to nearly any point on the globe within about a day, connections included, and roads stop mattering. You’re still inside the atmosphere, burning fuel the whole way, and you land where there is a runway.

After zero, in the high agency half, you can orbit the Earth. This is a new category. You are technically in space, you can reach any point on Earth very quickly, and you feel weightless. But at the altitude of the International Space Station, about 400 kilometers up, gravity is still about 88 percent as strong as on the ground. The astronauts float because they are falling around the planet, constantly, at about 7.7 kilometers per second, and missing it, once every ninety minutes or so. They are free of the ground and not free of the Earth. That’s how I’d describe the high agency room: light, quick, able to reach anywhere on the planet, and still inside the field of the separate self.

The top right corner of the small square is escape velocity, the speed at which Earth’s pull can never bring you back, about 11.2 kilometers per second from the surface and about 10.8 at the height of the space station. Notice how little extra it takes. At any height, escape speed is about 41 percent higher than orbital speed there, and the difference in outcome is total, since in the ideal case one speed circles forever and the other goes. Part one’s math said that effectiveness is roughly BE times DO, and the corner is the maximum of that product, 100 times 100. Don’t quote me, but I like that the geometry of the small square and the physics of rockets agree on where the threshold is.

Past it, each step is a new order of freedom, and each has its own escape speed: roughly 17 kilometers per second from Earth’s surface to leave the Sun’s pull, and something like 500 to leave the Milky Way from where we are. Plus two is the solar system. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, passed out of the Sun’s bubble of particles in 2012, though not out of the Sun’s gravitational reach. Plus three is the galaxy, about 100,000 light-years across. Plus four is every point in the universe, which is about 93 billion light-years across as far as we can see, and perhaps larger, so the word every is doing some work. In the axiom’s terms, each step is a wider piece of the whole that you can be at home in, and at plus four no point in the universe is somewhere else.

After plus four, my teacher describes a new stage, which he calls birth. I’ll say what I can responsibly say. It’s a stage, so the arrow isn’t the end of the story, and many traditions have an image of a second birth for a decisive change in a person. In John’s gospel Jesus says that one must be born again, or born from above, since the Greek word can mean either. In the Hindu tradition the twice-born are those who have received the initiation that counts as a second birth. Meister Eckhart taught that God is born in the ground of the soul, among the teachings that led to an inquiry for heresy. The traditions use the image for different things, and using it for the stage after the top of a scale is our school’s own extension of a shared image. Beyond that I’m at the edge of what I can say. Some physicists speculate that our universe is one of many, an idea nobody has confirmed, and a hard one to test, and if so even plus four may be a stage and not an ending. In Kerbal Space Program, reaching orbit feels like winning, right up until you notice the Mun.

The old maps were scale maps as well. In Dante’s Paradiso the pilgrim rises through the heavens without noticing, and Beatrice tells him there is nothing to marvel at, since it would be stranger if he stayed low, like a flame sitting still on the ground. Jain teaching has a liberated soul rise by its own nature, like a gourd released from its clay, to the summit of the universe.

Other traditions draw staged maps too, so the scale isn’t unique to our school. Early Buddhism has four stages from stream entry to the arahant. The Jain tradition has fourteen, the gunasthanas, a path said to take many lifetimes, on which a soul can also fall back. Teresa of Avila described seven mansions of the inner castle, the last of which is the spiritual marriage. And Buddhists have their own version of a threshold birth: in Shantideva’s ritual of taking the vow, the practitioner says that today he is born into the family of the Buddhas.

One caution about the numbers. The arrow is our school’s scale, and I use it because it’s the one I know. Treat the labels as labels. The picture is what carries the point.

Why I want this

This is the goal I hinted at in Part one’s zoom out, and it’s the one Anna and I have for our lifetime: the whole map, not the corner of the chart. The reason is simple. I want to be useful, with nothing standing between me and whoever is in front of me. In the axiom’s terms, that’s a program that has stopped managing its walls and can look at the person in front of it as another part of the same machine.

I’d ask you to use on me the two questions Part one gave you for testing a reason. Would I still do it if nobody ever found out? And does it make me gentler with the person in front of me, or does that person turn into material for the cause? The first is hard to check, since I’m writing this somewhere people can read it. The second you can check better than I can.

People ask two things at this point. The first: isn’t this just becoming a vegetable? Part one met that version, the person who watches their whole life from the side and calls it calm. The feeling is one channel, and you can’t dam half a river, so a person who cuts the channel to feel less pain feels less of everything. Take the border away and everything comes through, the hard things included. The second: how do you know it exists? I don’t, not the way I know Paris exists. I know the direction, because each step toward it shows up in things I can check on this chart, and I’ve met people who are further along, and a room settles when they walk in. Beyond that, I’d rather spend my life walking toward something I’m not sure of than sitting in a room I’m sure I hate.

So the honest summary is that I don’t know if I’ll get there, and I know the direction.

What you can check

If the axiom is right, loosening the wall should leave marks you can check, and the first ones are on the chart. How many options do you see in a hard moment? How long does a reaction last? How quickly do you say sorry first, even when it costs you? When did you last do something nobody asked for, for somebody else’s good? The school I belong to has watched this change for a long time, and the order people describe is consistent: first a person starts to listen, as attention leaves the self it was protecting and lands on somebody else; then giving shows up, unasked and without a tally; then perception clears, since someone who no longer feels under siege doesn’t need to find threats everywhere. That’s observation and not a study, and I’d weigh it that way.

The prediction is the interesting part. If the sense of separation is held by tension, then loosening it might make a person more generous without anyone telling them to be. The nearest lab results are mixed. Cialdini’s result above points part of the way: how close people felt to someone in need predicted their willingness to help, and other researchers dispute that reading. Feeling safe raised compassion and helping in some experiments, and a short breathing practice lowered the urge to make amends in others. Nobody has tested letting go of tension as such. It’s also an experiment you can run on yourself, which is the nice thing about this kind of claim.

Where this goes next

That’s the map as far as I can draw it honestly. What I haven’t told you is how to walk it, and that is the next part, which has a lot less philosophy in it and a lot more schedule. The short version is that it uses stillness and work together, every day. The work shows you where you’re still braced, the stillness lets it go, and the energy that comes back goes into the work. That alternation is the shrinking swing from Part one, put on a timetable.

Until then, one question that needs no equipment. Where, right now, does the border of you sit? Don’t quote me, but I’d bet it isn’t where you think.
