Where the room splits

Part one was the polite part. Everything in it holds up whichever way you think the universe works, which is why I could write it without anybody leaving the table. This part is where the room splits, so I’d like to say up front that you’re allowed to disagree with it, and that I’ll try to be clear about which steps I can show you and which I can only report.

One thing before the two views, because it changes how you should read everything after it. I’m not religious. None of the traditions I’m about to quote is mine, and I’m not asking you to adopt any of them. What I will do is quote a lot of them: Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Daoist, Greek, and a few more. My claim is that when you lay them side by side, they speak of the same things. They use different words, different stories and sometimes different gods, and I’ll say where I think they really disagree. But the overlap is the point of this part. I’m using the traditions the way you’d use witnesses, and not as authorities.

Two ways of reading a life sit at opposite ends of the room. Most people stand somewhere in between without noticing.

The first starts from observation. Look around and write down what you see. A world of things, tables and bodies and planets, and underneath them particles, or quantum fields if you want the exact physicist’s answer. Billions of people, each inside a skull with a point of view on the far side of it. I look in a mirror, see one of those people, and feel like one: me in here, everybody else out there. Put those observations together and you get an assumption, which is that matter is the basic stuff, a mind is something a brain does, and consciousness depends on that activity. Followed honestly, the assumption leads to materialism, and, if you also believe the laws leave no gaps, to determinism, the picture Laplace painted in 1814 of an intellect that knew every force and position and could therefore compute the whole future. In its standard form quantum mechanics adds some dice to this, which gives you randomness and not freedom, though some interpretations keep the whole picture deterministic. When the brain stops, the experiencer stops. Free will is a story the brain tells, and meaning is something we make up, which we’re allowed to do, since nobody else is going to.

I’m not being sarcastic. This view built hospitals and phones, I live inside it for most of my day, and in my body it feels like the plain truth. Me, Alex, a separate entity. Hello.

The second view sits at the other extreme, and it starts from a different assumption: that we are all one. Everything is part of a greater whole, and that whole is experiencing separation through the individual experience we call our lives. On this reading I’m not a thing inside the world. I’m the world, looking at itself from one position, with the memory of the rest switched off for a while.

Both of these are assumptions, and I want to be careful about that, because everything else in this part depends on it. Nobody has observed that matter is all there is. We have observed a lot of patterns and built excellent models of them, and every one of those observations was made through the same mind we’re trying to explain, which makes it a poor judge in its own case. The first view is strong at prediction. Most neuroscientists work on the assumption that conscious experience depends on activity in the brain, and the evidence for that dependence is strong. What nobody has is an accepted account of why that activity feels like something from the inside, which the philosopher David Chalmers named the hard problem in 1995. The second view finds experience easy, since it is the basic ingredient, and has trouble with the opposite question: if everything is one, why does separation feel so convincing? Most of this part is about that trouble.

The second view is less exotic than it sounds. Spinoza argued that there is only one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that everything else is a form of it. When a rabbi cabled Einstein in 1929 to ask whether he believed in God, Einstein answered that he believed in Spinoza’s God, though he meant the lawful order of nature and nothing that cares what we do. Erwin Schrödinger, who has a Nobel Prize in physics and an equation named after him, ended What Is Life? in 1944 by arguing that the plurality of minds is only apparent, and called that the teaching of the Upanishads. In his 1956 lectures he wrote that the overall number of minds is just one, and he added that at that point he was doing religion and not science.

Closer to our time, the computer engineer turned philosopher Bernardo Kastrup argues that reality is one mind and that individual lives are like dissociated parts of it, and critics ask why and how a single mind would split. The cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman argues from evolution that perception works like the icons on a desktop, a useful interface and not the machinery underneath. Most scientists accept that perception is tuned for usefulness, and many reject his stronger conclusion. None of this proves anything, and none of these people holds exactly the view I’ll use. I mention them so you know the second view isn’t only something people say after a long weekend in the mountains.

So here is what I’m going to do. I’ll take the second view as an axiom, the way a mathematician takes one, and see what follows from it. The question is whether the main ideas of the world’s religions come out as logical consequences, one after another, something like a proof. That tests coherence and not truth, because plenty of consistent systems are false, and I’ll say at each step what the step doesn’t show. But if one coherent system turns out to be what independent traditions reached separately, in different vocabularies, I’d call it at least a sane place to stand. If you’re in the first camp, treat this as a thought experiment. Part one still works.

The axiom and the machine

The axiom has three clauses.

We are one. There is a single whole, and everything is in it.

The whole experiences separation. It looks at itself through individual lives, each one a position with its own point of view, which each of us experiences as “my life.”

The separation ends. Being separate is a phase, and it returns to knowing itself as one.

None of this is new. The oldest statement of it that I know sits in the Upanishads. In the Chandogya, a father tells his son Svetaketu that the finest essence of which the whole world is made is the self, and that he is that. The Brihadaranyaka says that in the beginning this world was only Brahman, which knew only itself, thought “I am Brahman,” and so became the whole.

I’ll explain it with a computer, because Part one admitted that I spent years in cyber security, so I can’t help it. Picture one machine running thousands of programs. Every program gets its own private memory, and each believes it has the whole memory to itself. The operating system maps all that private memory onto the same physical chips, and the hardware enforces the walls, so by default one program can’t read another’s memory. That’s process isolation, and it’s what keeps a random app from reading your password manager. The walls are real. Inside the system they are as solid as anything gets, though they are an arrangement and not a law: in 2018 the Meltdown and Spectre flaws showed that information could leak across them. And underneath, there is one machine.

Now picture the screen. Two windows sit in opposite corners of the display, and it looks as if there is a distance between them. There is a distance, on the screen. The computation for both windows happens on the same machine, in the same moment, and the machine has never heard of that distance. It’s a property of how things get drawn, and two windows side by side may even belong to programs that have nothing to do with each other.

Under the axiom, you and I are programs. “Me over here, you over there” is the screen. My private memory is the self I feel from inside, the border from Part one is the wall around it, and the whole is the machine that was running both of us all along.

I should say what the word illusion means here, because it gets misused. Something is there. The icons on a desktop exist, and you can click them and get results. What doesn’t exist is the little folder you imagine you’re dragging, because there is no folder inside the machine, only patterns of charge. An illusion is a wrong reading of something real, and the wall between programs is real in exactly that sense: a fact inside the system, read as if it were a fact about the machine.

A computer is a picture, and a picture is all it is. I’m not saying the universe is a simulation, or that anyone is running it. It’s the best picture I have for how one thing can look like many without being many, and I’ll keep coming back to it.

The same things in different words

If we take the axiom seriously, a chain of ideas follows from it, and the chain has a shape you will recognize from religion: what is real, who we are, what happens to us, how we should live. I’m not the first person to notice that traditions that never met describe that shape. A few people made careers of checking.

The best known name for their project is the perennial philosophy. The phrase goes back to the Italian scholar Agostino Steuco, whose book De perenni philosophia appeared in 1540, and Leibniz used it in a letter in 1714. Aldous Huxley made it famous in 1945 with The Perennial Philosophy, an anthology of short passages by Christian mystics such as Eckhart and John of the Cross, from the Upanishads and the Gita, and by Taoist, Buddhist and Sufi writers, laid side by side. Huxley’s definition has three parts. A metaphysic that recognizes a divine Reality underlying the world of things and lives and minds. A psychology that finds in the soul something similar to, or even identical with, that Reality. An ethic that puts the end of human life in knowing it. What is real, who are we, how do we live. That’s the skeleton of this part, and I’ll follow it. The Swiss philosopher Frithjof Schuon argued in The Transcendent Unity of Religions that the traditions differ at the outer level, in rituals and stories, and converge at the inner one. William James had noticed something similar in 1902: mystical reports from Hindu, Neoplatonic, Sufi and Christian sources keep returning to the same notes, a unanimity he called eternal, and one that he thought should make a critic stop and think.

The other group is the Theosophists. The Theosophical Society was founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky, Henry Olcott, William Judge and a few others, and its declared aims came to include the comparative study of religion, philosophy and science. Blavatsky’s books, Isis Unveiled in 1877 and The Secret Doctrine in 1888, were one of the main routes by which English readers met karma and reincarnation, alongside the earlier Spiritism of Allan Kardec. It has a mixed reputation. In 1885 the Society for Psychical Research published a report by Richard Hodgson that called Blavatsky an impostor who had written the Mahatma letters herself. A century later the handwriting expert Vernon Harrison, a long standing member of that society, argued in its own journal that the report was biased and its evidence selective, and found that the letters were written in different hands. That doesn’t show that the Masters existed, only that the case against her was weaker than long assumed. Scholars of religion treat Theosophy as an influential modern esoteric movement that helped make Asian religions a popular interest in the West, and most doubt its picture of one ancient wisdom behind all faiths. I’m borrowing the comparison and not vouching for the Masters.

The idea is much older than either, and so are the doubts about it. A verse in the Rigveda (1.164.46) says that what is real is one and that the poets give it many names, Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni. In the Buddhist Udana the Buddha tells of blind men who feel an elephant and quarrel, as an image of sectarians who each hold one side. Jain thinkers later used the story for many-sidedness, and Rumi retells it with an elephant in a dark room. In the nineteenth century Ramakrishna pictured the faiths as the different landing steps of one lake, where Hindus say jal, Muslims say pani and Christians say water.

Now the objection, and it’s a fair one. In 1978 the philosopher Steven Katz argued that there are no pure, unmediated mystical experiences: a mystic’s tradition shapes what is experienced, so a Christian’s and a Buddhist’s experiences may differ from the start, well before anyone describes them. Wayne Proudfoot and Robert Sharf pressed similar points, and many scholars of religion today reject the strong form of perennialism and are wary of comparison itself. Defenders such as Robert Forman and John Hick offer more modest versions, and Huston Smith’s Forgotten Truth is the popular case for a common core. I’ll stay with the modest version, and I’ll tell you where the traditions say the same thing and where they plainly don’t.

Here is where they don’t. The axiom sits most comfortably in Advaita Vedanta, in Neoplatonism, in the Sufi school of Ibn Arabi and, with some strain, in the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria. It sits badly with Theravada Buddhism, which has no self and no One, with Jainism, which has many eternal souls and no creator, and with mainstream Judaism, Christianity and Islam, where Creator and creature stay distinct and a life is lived once. The disagreement is largest about the nature of the whole: is it a person, a ground or an emptiness? That’s also where I’d expect it, since it’s the one question the axiom leaves open. I’d rather say all this now than have you find it later.

So here is my claim, in its bold form: the traditions are describing the same thing in different words. I’ll argue it as hard as I honestly can, and I’ll mark the places where the bold form breaks. The version I’d defend without a fight is narrower. Start from “we are one, and experiencing separation,” and the main religious ideas about the world, the self and conduct come out in a fixed order, with strands in each tradition that have their own word for each step. I’m not saying the traditions reasoned their way here from an axiom. In history the ideas came first, and the axiom is a way of seeing why they fit together. And the witnesses are not perfectly independent: Neoplatonism fed into Christian and Islamic thought, Buddhism traveled across Asia, and Theosophy mixed the lot. That cuts both ways: agreement between traditions that never met counts for more than agreement between traditions that borrowed from each other, and I’ll try to say which is which.

Why separation feels so real

The first consequence is the obvious one: if we are one, the separation we experience is an illusion, in the sense I gave it. Several traditions have a word for it, and the words are not interchangeable. Advaita Vedanta calls it maya, and scholars of Advaita warn that illusion translates the word badly, since it means something dependent, neither fully real nor simply unreal. It calls the ignorance that keeps it going avidya, a word Buddhism uses too, as the first link in the chain that keeps suffering going. Plato’s prisoners in the cave take shadows for the world. Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, woke up, and couldn’t tell whether he was a man who had dreamed of a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming it was a man, though the text adds that there must be some distinction, which it calls the transformation of things. In Genesis, the couple eat from the tree of knowledge, notice that they are exposed, and hide from the one they’d been walking with in the garden. The Heart Sutra says that form is emptiness and emptiness is form, and here the disagreement is real. Buddhists mean that things have no fixed independent nature, which is a different claim from “all is one,” and Buddhist schools reject the Atman of the Upanishads, so Advaita and Buddhism disagree about what is left when the ignorance ends.

The question I care about is why it feels so solid. In the machine, the wall is real inside the system. A program can’t read another program’s memory, so from inside, “I” and “you” are as different as two things can be. The feeling of separation is an accurate report of a local fact, this is my memory and that is yours, read as if it were a fact about the whole machine. That’s the difference between how we experience our lives and the claim that we are one: the screen against the processor.

It also turns out that the wall is built from soft material. In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen sat people at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand in plain view, and stroked both with a brush at the same moment. Most people soon felt the touch in the rubber hand, and a lot of them said it felt like their own, and later studies timed it at well under a minute for most people. A video version with a person’s own back appeared in 2007 (Lenggenhager and colleagues). The philosopher Thomas Metzinger builds a whole theory on results like these: the felt self is a model the brain builds of the body and then can’t see as a model. Two caveats. What moves in these experiments is the felt ownership of a hand or the location of a body, which is a lot less than the whole sense of “me,” and how strongly people report the illusion goes with how suggestible they are. So I won’t claim that it proves the axiom. What it shows is smaller and I think useful: the border of the self, as you feel it, is made of the kind of stuff that can be edited.

The body keeps it in place. This is Part one’s tension, and it’s the part of the story I can show you in ten seconds. Make a fist, a hard one, and tense your arm and shoulder and jaw while you’re at it, and try to feel glad about something. I’ll wait. You’ve turned into a small hard object in a world of other objects: I end here, and everything past this point is other. My teacher Gennady keeps returning to three words in his lectures, tension, resistance and non-acceptance, and I read all three as the wall being maintained. Tension holds the outline in the muscles. Resistance pushes against what’s here. Non-acceptance refuses what has already happened. Let go of the fist and the sentence it was saying goes soft. That’s BE, in the language of this part: the amount of wall you are currently holding up. It also explains why you can’t argue a person out of the feeling. A body that holds a belief as tension needs evidence, and the evidence is the experience of letting go.

Why would the whole do this at all? Several traditions give an answer. Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 meets the question of why a complete God would create by calling creation play, lila. The Kabbalists of sixteenth century Safed taught that the infinite contracted to make room for a world, that the vessels meant to hold its light shattered, and that gathering the pieces is humanity’s work, a story of fragmentation and repair that keeps the Creator and the world distinct. Plotinus described a procession of the One into multiplicity and a return. Sufis quote a saying, “I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known,” which hadith scholars do not accept as a saying of the Prophet and which Ibn Arabi called sound by mystical unveiling, and not by a chain of transmission. My reading of all four is that a whole that wants to know itself can’t do it from nowhere. It needs a point of view, and a point of view is a separation. Knowing needs a gap. I can’t prove that this is why, and the traditions themselves are modest about it.

Not enough time in one life

The third clause says the separation ends, so the process is a return, from experiencing the world as many to experiencing it as one. Here is the problem. Look around. Most people don’t reach it. By Part one’s guess, 95 percent of us spend our lives in the Default room, and even the few who reach the top right corner are, by the standard of the axiom, only at the start of the next map. The chain only works if you grant that the aim is unity, which is the third clause of the axiom, and I haven’t argued for it. I’m following the chain, and not claiming to have proved the first link.

If the process is real and most people don’t finish it in a lifetime, there are two options. Either the process fails for most participants, or it takes more than one lifetime. The first option makes the whole a bad designer: a whole that wanted to know itself and rigged the exercise so that almost everyone is unable to finish it. The axiom prefers the second option. The run is longer than one life.

And if the run continues, something has to continue with it between lives. In the traditions that use the word, that’s the soul. In the machine it would be the saved state: the program is shut down, the window closes, and what carries over is the account and not the session. Turning it off and on again fixes a surprising number of things, and The IT Crowd made a catchphrase of it. In this picture it’s also the policy on death.

Look at how many traditions arrive here. In the Hindu texts the self that moves from body to body is the atman, and the Bhagavad Gita compares it to a person putting on new clothes and discarding worn ones. In Plato’s Phaedo, Socrates gives several arguments for the survival of the soul on his last day, and in the Myth of Er that closes the Republic, souls choose their next lives after drinking from the river of forgetfulness, which is one old answer to why you don’t remember the last one. Buddhism has a subtler version. It holds that there is no fixed soul, and yet that something continues, and in the Milindapanha the monk Nagasena compares it to a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same flame nor a different one. Buddhist schools disagreed about whether there is a state between lives, with some accepting one and the Theravada rejecting it, and the Tibetan Book of the Dead, a fourteenth century text, guides the dying through the bardo, the in-between. Its English title and framing come from W. Y. Evans-Wentz’s 1927 edition, shaped by his Theosophical background, so the Theosophists show up here too. Kabbalists taught gilgul, the turning of souls from life to life, though rationalists such as Saadia Gaon rejected it. Jain teaching has each soul bound by karma and reborn until freed, Sikh scripture teaches rebirth with liberation by grace, and the Druze believe that at death the soul passes directly into a new body.

The three big Western religions mostly settled on one life, followed by resurrection and judgment, though not without argument. Origen taught that souls exist before birth but rejected the transmigration of souls, and the sixth century condemnations of Origenism went after pre-existence and the final restoration of all, and not reincarnation as such. Muslim theologians who discussed tanasukh mostly rejected it, though some minority groups accepted it. So the traditions are not unanimous, and I’d rather say that than bury it. The logic of the axiom gets you to rebirth. Some traditions follow it all the way, and some stop earlier. There’s also a fair question that the axiom has to face: if there is only one mind, who is it that carries over? The Advaita answer is that individuality belongs to the level of appearance, which in the machine is the process and not the processor. It’s an answer, and I’d call it an open question.

I should say plainly that mainstream science has no accepted evidence for survival after death. There is a research literature. Ian Stevenson at the University of Virginia published Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation in 1966 and went on to collect more than two thousand cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives, and Jim Tucker continued with Life Before Life in 2005. Critics point to cultural expectation, parental suggestion, selective reporting and testimony gathered after the fact, and most of the cases come from cultures that already believe in rebirth. Carl Sagan listed it among the few claims in that territory that deserved serious study, and said he doubted it would hold up. I’m not leaning on it. This part is about what follows from an axiom, and a conclusion shouldn’t borrow credibility from a disputed experiment.

This is the step where a rational reader gets to say “unfalsifiable,” and it’s a fair thing to say. Inside the axiom it follows. Outside, it is a claim that many people hold on the strength of experience, and I can’t hand you the experience in an essay.

Karma, or the fingers

If we are one, there is nobody else. Everyone I could harm is part of the whole I belong to. Hold up your hand. If the index finger and the middle finger start fighting, pressing and scratching, they hurt the hand, and the hand is the only thing either of them can be a part of. Nobody had to appoint a judge. The harm lands on the one doing it, because there was only ever one of us.

That is what I think karma means once you take the courtroom out. The word is Sanskrit for action, and in the classical Hindu and Buddhist teaching it’s a law of consequence, which makes it a different thing from fate. The traditions differ on who, if anyone, administers it. In the Buddhist account nobody does, and the Buddha said that it is intention he calls karma. In the theistic schools of Hinduism God has a role, and in Advaita karma belongs to the world of apparent individuals, to be transcended by knowledge. In the axiom, the idea comes out as a consequence: what I do to another part of the whole, I do to the whole, and the whole is me, so it comes around. In the machine, a program that corrupts shared memory will eventually read the corrupted data back, and it can’t pretend the failure belongs to somebody else, because it was the same memory all along.

Several traditions have this structure, though few spell it out as geometry. Paul writes that whatever a person sows, that will he also reap, which is accountability under God, with no rebirth in it, though the shape is the same. In Matthew’s gospel the king says that whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of mine, you did for me. The Quran says that if you do good, you do good to yourselves, and if you do evil, it is to yourselves (17:7), and that whoever does an atom’s weight of good or evil will see it (99:7 and 99:8). Rabbinic Judaism has the phrase measure for measure.

Then comes the golden rule, which is the same logic read forwards. If the other is me, treating them as I want to be treated stops being a nicety and becomes consistency. Nearly every tradition has it. Leviticus says to love your neighbor as yourself, and in the Talmud Hillel is reported to have said, asked to teach the whole Torah while standing on one foot, that what is hateful to you, you should not do to your neighbor, and that the rest is commentary. Jesus gives the positive form in the Sermon on the Mount. A saying reported in Bukhari and Muslim has the Prophet say that none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself. Confucius names reciprocity as the one word for conduct in Analects 15.24, numbered 15.23 in older translations, and the Mahabharata and the Buddhist Udanavarga have it as well. Most of these rest on reciprocity or empathy, and a few go further. The Isha Upanishad says that the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, recoils from nothing, and asks what delusion or sorrow can remain for the one who sees oneness. That is the third part of Huxley’s definition, the ethic, and the axiom is a short explanation of why reciprocity works. Even a lab has touched it. In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how “one” people felt with a person in need predicted their willingness to help better than sympathy did, though other researchers dispute the reading.

Two warnings. The cheap version of karma is blame: she had it coming, he reaped what he sowed. That is spiritual bypassing with a calculator, and the axiom doesn’t support it, because the person you’re blaming is as much a part of the whole as you are. The version that follows from the axiom is responsibility: what is my next move toward the part of myself that I’ve been fighting?

And what undoes it? Not punishment. Repair. The fingers stop fighting. In Part one’s terms that’s inner agency, the sorry, the first step in the conflict, the unsaid sentence. The traditions have words for it: the Hebrew teshuvah means return, the Arabic tawba means turning back, and the Greek metanoia in the New Testament means a change of mind. Return, turn, change of mind. I’d call that the door, described from the inside.

The small square and the big one

Part one drew a chart, and I’d like to read it again through the axiom, because it says something I couldn’t say then.

Every room on the chart is a separate program trying to run well. Default protects itself. Driven goes out and takes, from the outside, what it can’t feel on the inside, which is mostly approval. Drifting steps away from whatever pokes it and calls the stepping away peace. High agency does all of it well: it acts, it is honest and kind, and most of the fear is gone. It’s the best version of a separate program that I know how to describe. That is what the small square is: the area available to a separate self when it gets as good as it can get at being separate.

The big square is something else. It begins when the separation itself stops being assumed, and not merely when the program improves. You can optimize a program for years and it stays a program, in its own memory, behind its own wall. At some point the question stops being how to run better and becomes whether the wall is real. In my old field that move is called a sandbox escape. It pays a bounty and ruins the vendor’s afternoon, and I like the term because it names the right kind of move: a different relationship to the walls, which no amount of polishing the sandbox gives you.

This also puts Part one’s gate in a new light. A reason that is about you can’t carry you out, because the reason is the thing you’re trying to leave. It takes a reason bigger than yourself to get across the ring, and the axiom says why: you’re asking the program to act on behalf of the machine.

The arrow

Our school has a scale for this, which it calls the arrow of consciousness, and it runs from minus four to plus four. It is the same diagonal you’ve been looking at since Part one. The bottom left of the small square is the low end. The door, in the middle, is zero. The top right corner is plus one. Everything past it lives outside the small square, in the zoomed-out map from the end of Part one: plus two, plus three and plus four, and at plus four my teacher Gennady describes a new stage of evolution that he calls birth.

I’ve found one picture that carries the whole scale, and it’s about speed. The measure is how freely you can move through space, and what holds you back is gravity. Under the axiom, moving to a place means being able to experience from another position, so the speed is how many points of view of the whole you can be at home in. Gravity is the pull of the one you started with.

At the low end, in Default, you can run, ride a bike and drive a car. Those are real speeds, and you can get a lot done at them, but you move along the ground, so the ground decides where you go. People who go a bit further, up to zero, learn to fly. An aircraft takes you to nearly any point on the globe within about a day, connections included, and roads stop mattering. You’re still inside the atmosphere, burning fuel the whole way, and you land where there is a runway.

After zero, in the high agency half, you can orbit the Earth. This is a new category. You are technically in space, you can reach any point on Earth very quickly, and you feel weightless. But at the altitude of the International Space Station, about 400 kilometers up, gravity is still about 88 percent as strong as on the ground. The astronauts float because they are falling around the planet, constantly, at about 7.7 kilometers per second, and missing it, once every ninety minutes or so. They are free of the ground and not free of the Earth. That’s how I’d describe the high agency room: light, quick, able to reach anywhere on the planet, and still inside the field of the separate self.

The top right corner of the small square is escape velocity, the speed at which Earth’s pull can never bring you back, about 11.2 kilometers per second from the surface and about 10.8 at the height of the space station. Notice how little extra it takes. At any height, escape speed is about 41 percent higher than orbital speed there, and the difference in outcome is total, since one speed circles forever and the other goes. Part one’s math said that effectiveness is roughly BE times DO, and the corner is the maximum of that product, 100 times 100. Don’t quote me, but I like that the geometry of the small square and the physics of rockets agree on where the threshold is.

Past it, each step is a new order of freedom, and each has its own escape speed: roughly 17 kilometers per second from Earth’s surface to leave the Sun’s pull, and something like 500 to leave the Milky Way from where we are. Plus two is the solar system. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, passed out of the Sun’s bubble of particles in 2012, though not out of the Sun’s gravitational reach. Plus three is the galaxy, about 100,000 light-years across. Plus four is every point in the universe, which is about 90 billion light-years across as far as we can see, and perhaps larger, so the word every is doing some work. In the axiom’s terms, each step is a wider piece of the whole that you can be at home in, and at plus four no point in the universe is somewhere else.

After plus four, my teacher describes a new stage, which he calls birth. I’ll say what I can responsibly say. It’s a stage, so the arrow isn’t the end of the story, and many traditions have an image of a second birth for a decisive change in a person. In John’s gospel Jesus says that one must be born again, or born from above, since the Greek word can mean either. In the Hindu tradition the twice-born are those who have received the initiation that counts as a second birth. Meister Eckhart taught that God is born in the ground of the soul, which got him investigated for heresy. The traditions use the image for different things: in John it is a beginning, in Eckhart a lasting state, and using it for the stage after the top of a scale is our school’s own extension of a shared image. Beyond that I’m at the edge of what I can say. Some physicists speculate that our universe is one of many, an idea nobody has tested, and if so even plus four may be a stage and not an ending. In Kerbal Space Program, reaching orbit feels like winning, right up until you notice the Mun.

One caution about the numbers. The arrow is our school’s scale, and I use it because it’s the one I know. Treat the labels as labels. The picture is what carries the point.

Why I want this

This is the goal I mentioned in Part one, the one Anna and I have for our lifetime: the whole map, not the corner of the chart. The reason is simple. I want to be useful, with nothing standing between me and whoever is in front of me. In the axiom’s terms, that’s a program that has stopped managing its walls and can look at the person in front of it as another part of the same machine.

I’d ask you to use Part one’s two questions on me. Would I still do it if nobody ever found out? And does it make me gentler with the person in front of me, or does that person turn into material for the cause? The first is hard to check, since I’m writing this somewhere people can read it. The second you can check better than I can.

People ask two things at this point. The first: isn’t this just becoming a vegetable? Part one met that version, the cold observer, who looks at the wall from outside and calls it peace. The feeling is one channel, and you can’t dam half a river, so a person who cuts the channel to feel less pain feels less of everything. Take the border away and everything comes through, the hard things included. The second: how do you know it exists? I don’t, not the way I know Paris exists. I know the direction, because each step toward it is measurable on this chart, and I’ve met people who are further along, and a room settles when they walk in. Beyond that, I’d rather spend my life walking toward something I’m not sure of than sitting in a room I’m sure I hate.

So the honest summary is that I don’t know if I’ll get there, and I know the direction.

What you can check

If the axiom is right, loosening the wall should leave marks you can check, and the first ones are on the chart. How many options do you see in a hard moment? How long does a reaction last? How quickly do you say sorry first? When did you last do something nobody asked for, for somebody else’s good? The school I belong to has watched this change for a long time, and the order people describe is consistent: first a person starts to listen, as attention leaves the self it was protecting and lands on somebody else; then giving shows up, unasked and without a tally; then perception clears, since someone who no longer feels under siege doesn’t need to find threats everywhere. That’s observation and not a study, and I’d weigh it that way.

The prediction is the interesting part. If the sense of separation is held by tension, then letting go of the tension should make a person more generous without anyone telling them to be. Cialdini’s result above points that way, and it is small. It’s also an experiment you can run on yourself, which is the nice thing about this kind of claim.

Where this goes next

That’s the map as far as I can draw it honestly. What I haven’t told you is how to walk it, and that is the next part, which has a lot less philosophy in it and a lot more schedule. The short version is that it uses stillness and work together, every day. The work shows you where you’re still braced, the stillness lets it go, and the energy that comes back goes into the work. That alternation is the shrinking swing from Part one, put on a timetable.

Until then, one question that needs no equipment. Where, right now, does the border of you sit? Don’t quote me, but I’d bet it isn’t where you think.
