## David Chalmers, 'Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness' (the hard problem) (Chalmers born 1966. Paper given as a talk in 1994 (my memory, unchecked) and printed in 1995.)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: David Chalmers is an Australian philosopher of mind, now at New York University. In this paper he split the puzzles of consciousness into the 'easy' problems, which are about explaining what the brain does (discriminating, reporting, focusing attention), and the 'hard' problem, which is why any of that activity is accompanied by experience at all. 'The hard problem' became the standard name for the second.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits', in the paragraph saying both views are assumptions: the first view is strong at prediction, but nobody has an accepted account of why brain activity feels like something from the inside, which Chalmers named the hard problem in 1995. Also in the Sources list.

Where it sits: Journal of Consciousness Studies 2(3), 1995, pp. 200 to 219. The easy and hard distinction is made in the opening sections (a university course page describes sections 1 to 3 as drawing it).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: the easy problems are the ones where we explain an ability or function of the brain, and we know what kind of explanation would work, a mechanism. The hard problem is why the performance of those functions comes with experience, the sense that there is something it is like to be the system. Chalmers says that no explanation in terms of function alone seems able to answer it. I did not see the exact sentence in this session, so I give no quotation. Open consc.net and search the page for 'hard problem'.

Read it free:
- Chalmers, Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness, full text on consc.net (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://consc.net/papers/facing.html (not checked)
- CogPrints record for the paper, with abstract and bibliographic details: https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/316/ (seen in a search result)
- Chalmers, Moving Forward on the Problem of Consciousness (1997 reply to 26 commentaries): https://consc.net/papers/moving.html (seen in a search result)
- LSE course page on the paper (sections 1 to 3 on the easy and hard problems): https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ROBERT49/teaching/ph103/dq/3.html (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: David J. Chalmers, The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory (Oxford University Press, 1996) is the full argument. For a recent take, his Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy (W. W. Norton, 2022) deals with simulation and ties in with the computer picture. His 2018 paper 'The Meta-Problem of Consciousness' (Journal of Consciousness Studies) asks why we think there is a hard problem.

Background: The paper grew out of a talk at the Tucson consciousness conference in 1994 (my memory, unchecked), so 1995 is the print date and the essay is right to use it. Chalmers is careful here. He does not say the easy problems are simple to solve. He says we know what a solution would look like, a mechanism that performs the function. The hard problem is different because even a complete account of functions leaves open why performing them comes with experience. Earlier writers pressed the same point, notably Thomas Nagel in 'What is it like to be a bat?' (1974) and Joseph Levine, who called it the explanatory gap (1983). Chalmers goes on to argue that experience may need to be treated as a basic feature of the world, like mass or charge, and he takes panpsychism and some forms of idealism seriously. That is why he fits the set-up of Part two so well. Opponents include Daniel Dennett, who holds that the problem dissolves once the easy ones are solved, and the illusionists, such as Keith Frankish, who say the sense that experience is something extra is itself a mistake the brain makes. An earlier research note put the 2020 PhilPapers survey at about 62 percent of philosophers calling the hard problem genuine and about 30 percent saying it does not exist. I could not reopen it, so check before quoting. The essay claims only that no accepted account exists, and both camps agree with that.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't the hard problem just an easy problem we haven't solved yet? Some serious philosophers say so. The essay claims only that there is no accepted account, which both sides accept as the present state. They disagree on whether the gap is real.
- Does Chalmers back 'we are all one'? No. He treats panpsychism and idealism as live options among several. Cite him for the problem and not for the axiom.

## The working assumption that consciousness depends on brain activity (Modern form from the 1990s (Crick, The Astonishing Hypothesis, 1994); the idea is much older.)

Kind: concept. Status: unchecked.

Who: Not one text but the default position of mainstream neuroscience: changes in the brain (anaesthesia, lesions, stimulation, injury) reliably change experience, so experience is assumed to depend on brain activity. The essay treats this as the evidence base of the first view.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': 'Most neuroscientists work on the assumption that conscious experience depends on activity in the brain, and the evidence for that dependence is strong.' It also underlies the first view's chain: matter is basic, a mind is what a brain does, and when the brain stops the experiencer stops.

Where it sits: Crick, The Astonishing Hypothesis (1994), opening pages. Koch, Massimini, Boly and Tononi, 'Neural correlates of consciousness: progress and problems', Nature Reviews Neuroscience (2016, from memory). William James, Human Immortality (1898) for the alternative reading.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase from memory: Crick's book opens by saying that your joys, sorrows, memories, ambitions, sense of identity and free will are no more than the behaviour of a vast assembly of nerve cells and their molecules. I did not see the exact words in this session, so do not quote them without checking. The checklist advises that no poll of neuroscientists was found and suggests the wording 'the working assumption of neuroscience'.

Read it free:
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Consciousness (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness/ (not checked)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Physicalism (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/physicalism/ (not checked)

Read it in a book: Francis Crick, The Astonishing Hypothesis (Scribner, 1994). Christof Koch, The Feeling of Life Itself (MIT Press, 2019) for a neuroscientist who stays inside the assumption yet takes experience seriously. William James, Human Immortality: Two Supposed Objections to the Doctrine (1898), which is out of copyright and easy to find online, for the 'transmission' alternative.

Background: There is no single text behind this sentence and no poll of neuroscientists, so the checklist suggests the safer wording 'the working assumption of neuroscience'. The evidence for dependence is of an ordinary kind. General anaesthesia switches experience off and back on. Strokes, lesions and injuries remove or distort particular kinds of experience. Electrical stimulation, famously by Wilder Penfield on awake patients, can evoke sights, sounds and memories. Researchers look for the neural correlates of consciousness, the minimal brain events sufficient for a given experience. Progress is real and agreement is thin: an earlier research note records a large adversarial collaboration called COGITATE, reported in Nature in 2025, which tested two leading theories against each other and challenged key claims of both. I did not recheck it. The important logical point is that dependence is not production. William James argued in 1898 that the brain could be a transmitter or filter of mind, and that this fits the same evidence. Most neuroscientists do not hold that view, but it is why the essay says 'depends on' and avoids 'produced by'. For philosophers, an earlier note recorded physicalism as the leading but not dominant view, about half, with different surveys giving different figures. Do not print a percentage without opening the survey table.

If someone pushes back:
- Is it true that most neuroscientists think this? I have no count. Say 'the working assumption of neuroscience', which nobody disputes as a description of how the field operates.
- Doesn't dependence prove the brain produces the mind? No. A radio's output depends on the radio, and the programme still comes from somewhere else. The essay uses 'depends on' for that reason, and takes no side.

## Pierre-Simon Laplace, Essai philosophique sur les probabilités (1814) (Laplace 1749 to 1827. Essai 1814. English translation by Truscott and Emory, 1902.)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Laplace was a French mathematician and astronomer (Mécanique céleste). The Essai philosophique began as the introduction to his technical work on probability and appeared separately in 1814 as a plain-language account for general readers. It contains the famous passage about an all-knowing intelligence.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': the first view, followed honestly, leads to materialism and, if the laws leave no gaps, to determinism, 'the picture Laplace painted in 1814 of an intellect that knew every force and position and could therefore compute the whole future.'

Where it sits: Near the start of the essay, about page 4 of the 1902 English edition, in the opening chapter on probability. The French begins 'Une intelligence qui, pour un instant donné, connaîtrait toutes les forces dont la nature est animée...' (French wording from memory). The essay's Sources line calls this 'the introduction'. The passage sits in the opening pages, whatever your edition names them.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: the present state of the universe is the effect of its past and the cause of its future. An intelligence that knew, at one instant, all the forces acting in nature and the position of every being in it, and was vast enough to analyse that data, would hold the motions of the largest bodies and the lightest atom in one formula. For it nothing would be uncertain, and the future, like the past, would be present to its eyes. Only the opening words 'Given for one instant an intelligence which could comprehend all the forces by which nature is animated' are exact: they are the Truscott and Emory translation (1902) as shown in a search snippet of the Gutenberg text. The rest is my paraphrase.

Read it free:
- Project Gutenberg, A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (Truscott and Emory, 1902), ebook page: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/58881 (seen in a search result)
- Project Gutenberg, the same text as one HTML page: https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/58881/pg58881-images.html (seen in a search result)
- Internet Archive, Cornell scan of the 1902 edition (no known US copyright restrictions): https://archive.org/download/cu31924001150733/cu31924001150733.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia on the book, with links to the French original and the English translation on Wikisource: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Philosophical_Essay_on_Probabilities (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Laplace, A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities, translated by F. W. Truscott and F. L. Emory (John Wiley, 1902; Dover reprint 1951). A modern translation is Andrew I. Dale's Pierre-Simon Laplace, Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (Springer, 1995), from the fifth French edition (from memory).

Background: Laplace wrote 'une intelligence', not 'demon'. The label 'Laplace's demon' was added later by commentators, and the essay is right to say 'intellect'. He was not arguing about free will or God. He was explaining why we need probability: it measures our ignorance, and a being with perfect knowledge would need none. The passage is the clearest classical statement of determinism: given the laws and the state at one time, the state at every other time follows. Challenges came from several directions. Classical chaos (Poincare, later Lorenz) means that tiny errors grow so fast that long-range prediction fails in practice even when the laws are deterministic. That limits prediction and leaves determinism standing. Quantum mechanics in its standard textbook form gives only probabilities for measurement results, though other interpretations stay deterministic (see the quantum entry). And no one has ever shown that the real universe is deterministic; the claim is a thought experiment plus a hope about physics. For your own talks, the useful distinction is between determinism as a property of equations and determinism as a fact about the world, which is much harder to establish. The essay uses Laplace only as the standard picture of where the first view ends.

If someone pushes back:
- Did Laplace say 'demon'? No. He wrote 'an intelligence'. The demon came later, and the essay does not use it.
- Hasn't quantum mechanics killed determinism? Not entirely. Some interpretations stay fully deterministic, as the essay says, and physics has not settled which is right.

## Quantum mechanics, chance, and quantum fields (Schrödinger equation 1926. Bohm's theory 1952. Everett's many-worlds 1957. Standard Model 1970s.)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: Background physics for two sentences in the essay: that the exact physicist's answer to 'what is the world made of' is quantum fields, with particles as their excitations, and that standard quantum mechanics adds chance to the Laplace picture while some interpretations keep the whole picture deterministic.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': 'underneath them particles, or quantum fields if you want the exact physicist's answer', and 'In its standard form quantum mechanics adds some dice to this, which gives you randomness and not freedom, though some interpretations keep the whole picture deterministic.'

Where it sits: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on Bohmian mechanics, the many-worlds interpretation, collapse theories, quantum field theory and causal determinism (the section on quantum mechanics). Bohm, Physical Review 85 (1952). Everett, Reviews of Modern Physics 29 (1957).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The Schrödinger equation, which says how the wave function changes, is deterministic. Randomness enters at measurement, through the Born rule, in textbook (Copenhagen style) quantum mechanics and in collapse theories such as GRW. Bohmian mechanics adds definite particle positions guided by the wave function and is deterministic, with the apparent randomness coming from ignorance of the starting positions. The many-worlds interpretation keeps only the Schrödinger equation: every outcome happens in some branch, so the whole is deterministic and chance is about which branch you find yourself in, though how to derive the Born rule there is still argued. In quantum field theory an electron is an excitation of the electron field and a photon of the electromagnetic field. Gravity is not part of that description.

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Causal Determinism (2006 archived version, includes a section on quantum mechanics): https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2006/entries/determinism-causal/ (seen in a search result)
- Vaidman, Quantum Theory and Determinism (preprint; he favours many-worlds, so treat as advocacy): https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/10684 (seen in a search result)
- Loewer, review of Copenhagen versus Bohmian interpretations: https://sites.rutgers.edu/barry-loewer/wp-content/uploads/sites/195/2019/06/Loewer-Review-Copenhagen-Versus-Bohmian-Interpretations-of-Quantum-Theory.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Why determinism in physics has no implications for free will (arXiv paper that surveys the three versions of quantum mechanics): https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03558 (seen in a search result)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Bohmian Mechanics (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-bohm/ (not checked)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Many-Worlds Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/ (not checked)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Quantum Field Theory (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/quantum-field-theory/ (not checked)

Read it in a book: David Albert, Quantum Mechanics and Experience (Harvard University Press, 1992) is the clearest careful account of why interpretation is a problem. Sean Carroll, Something Deeply Hidden (Dutton, 2019) makes a readable case for many-worlds. Tim Maudlin, Philosophy of Physics: Quantum Theory (Princeton University Press, 2019) is the harder, more rigorous option.

Background: Two separate claims sit in these sentences. The first is that the best physical description uses fields and not little balls. In the Standard Model an electron is a ripple of the electron field, and a photon of the electromagnetic field. Gravity is not in that description, and physicists do not agree on the final ontology, so 'particles, or quantum fields if you want the exact physicist's answer' is a fair hedge. The second is about chance. The Schrödinger equation is deterministic: the wave function now fixes the wave function later. The dice come in at measurement. In textbook quantum mechanics and in collapse theories, the outcome is genuinely random with probabilities given by the Born rule. Bohmian mechanics restores determinism by adding definite particle positions, and it is nonlocal, which Bell's theorem (1964) allows because that theorem rules out local hidden variables only. Many-worlds keeps just the Schrödinger equation, so all outcomes occur and nothing is random from the outside. There is no consensus among physicists on which is right. Randomness does not give freedom either: a coin flip in the neurons is not a choice. That is the essay's point, and it holds under every interpretation. Informal polls of physicists show no majority view (from memory, so I give no figures).

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't quantum randomness make room for free will? Chance alone is not choice. Libertarians about free will need more than randomness, and compatibilists say they don't need either. The essay is neutral beyond that.
- Does consciousness collapse the wave function? The essay does not say so. That idea (von Neumann, Wigner) is a minority view and nothing in this part depends on it.

## Free will as 'a story the brain tells', and the compatibilist reply (Wegner 2002. Dennett 2003. Harris 2012. Sapolsky 2023. All from memory, unchecked.)

Kind: concept. Status: unchecked.

Who: The essay sums up the first view's ending with 'Free will is a story the brain tells, and meaning is something we make up.' That is one side of a live debate. Its best known recent voices are Daniel Wegner, Sam Harris and Robert Sapolsky. The main reply comes from compatibilists such as Daniel Dennett.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': the last step of the first view, after determinism. A checker advised softening it to 'on one reading', because compatibilists would feel misdescribed.

Where it sits: Wegner, The Illusion of Conscious Will (MIT Press, 2002). Dennett, Freedom Evolves (Viking, 2003). Harris, Free Will (Free Press, 2012). Sapolsky, Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will (Penguin Press, 2023).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Wegner argues from psychology that the feeling of willing an act is something the mind constructs, and may not be the cause of the act. Harris and Sapolsky argue that if every choice follows from earlier causes, then the feeling of authorship is real and the authorship is not. Dennett argues that the free will worth wanting is the capacity to respond to reasons, and that determinism leaves it intact.

Read it free:
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Free Will (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freewill/ (not checked)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Compatibilism (standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/ (not checked)
- Why determinism in physics has no implications for free will (arXiv paper): https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03558 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Daniel Dennett, Freedom Evolves (Viking, 2003) for the compatibilist case, and Robert Sapolsky, Determined (Penguin Press, 2023) for the strongest recent skeptical case. Sam Harris's Free Will (2012) is short and quick to read.

Background: The essay's sentence describes where the first view ends up. It is not what Alex believes, and it compresses a debate. Free-will skeptics argue that if every choice follows from earlier causes, genes, upbringing and the state of the brain, then the feeling of authorship is real and the authorship is not. Daniel Wegner argued from psychology that the feeling of willing is constructed by the mind, often after the fact. Benjamin Libet's experiments in 1983 on the readiness potential, a brain signal that appears before people report deciding, were long taken as support. Later work, such as Aaron Schurger and colleagues in 2012, offered a different reading of that signal as background neural noise (from memory, check). The main reply comes from compatibilists such as Daniel Dennett, who say free will worth wanting is the ability to respond to reasons and that determinism leaves it untouched. An earlier research note recorded about 59 percent of philosophers in the 2020 PhilPapers survey as compatibilists. I could not recheck that. This is why the checker recommended softening: write 'on one reading'. Part two later treats karma as responsibility, which sits comfortably with a compatibilist view, so the softening costs the essay nothing.

If someone pushes back:
- So you deny free will? The essay only says what the first view concludes. Many philosophers who accept determinism still think free will survives. Say 'on one reading'.
- Doesn't Libet prove we decide before we know it? The experiments are contested. Later analyses read the early signal as noise and not as a decision.

## Baruch Spinoza, Ethics (one substance, God or Nature) (Spinoza 1632 to 1677. Ethics published after his death, in 1677.)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Spinoza was a Dutch Jewish philosopher born in Amsterdam, expelled from the Jewish community there in 1656 and a lens grinder by trade. The Ethics is written in the style of geometry, with definitions, axioms and proofs. Part I argues that there is only one substance, which is God, and that everything else is a way that substance is, a 'mode'.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': 'Spinoza argued that there is only one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that everything else is a form of it.' It is used to show that the second view is less exotic than it sounds, and it is the link to Einstein's cable.

Where it sits: Ethics, Part I, propositions 14 and 15 (one substance; everything is in God). The phrase 'God or Nature' (Deus sive Natura) is in the Preface to Part IV, and is also reported in the proof of IV.4 (unchecked).

The passage: In the Stanford Encyclopedia's rendering, Part I proposition 14 reads 'Except God, no substance can be or be conceived', and proposition 15 'nothing can be or be conceived without God' (the SEP archive of 2006, shown in the check). In R. H. M. Elwes's translation, the Preface to Part IV speaks of 'the eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature' (as seen in the check of the Gutenberg text). Wording differs between translations.

Read it free:
- Project Gutenberg, The Ethics, Elwes translation (1883), whole book in one HTML page: https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800-images.html (seen in a search result)
- Project Gutenberg, Ethics Part 4, Elwes (for the Preface that has 'God or Nature'): https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/971/pg971.html (seen in a search result)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Spinoza (2006 archived version, gives I.14 and I.15): https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2006/entries/spinoza/ (seen in a search result)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Spinoza (current version, standard address, not seen in a search result): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spinoza/ (not checked)

Read it in a book: Spinoza, Ethics, translated by Edwin Curley (Penguin Classics) or in The Collected Works of Spinoza, vol. 1 (Princeton University Press, 1985). Steven Nadler, Spinoza: A Life (Cambridge University Press, 1999) for the man and his world. Elwes (free) is dated in style but fine for locating a passage.

Background: The Ethics is hard going because it is a chain of proofs, but the plan is simple. Substance is what exists in itself and is conceived through itself. Part I argues there can be only one, God, with infinitely many attributes of which we know two, thought and extension, which is to say mind and matter. Everything else, your body, a stone, an idea, is a mode, a way that one substance is. That is the essay's 'a form of it'. Mind and body are the same thing under two attributes, which makes Spinoza an ancestor of 'dual aspect' views in philosophy of mind. Two cautions. His God has no purposes, will or personality and cannot be prayed to, which his contemporaries called atheism and later readers called pantheism; scholars still argue whether 'panentheism' fits better. And for the axiom: Spinoza gives the first clause (one whole) very well but not the second, a whole that experiences separation, because finite things are real modes and not a veil. Something near the third clause appears at the end, in Part V, where the mind's highest good is the 'intellectual love of God' reached by understanding that all things follow from God's nature. The checker also noted that the Sources line is right to cite Part IV for the phrase 'God or Nature', since Part I supports only the one-substance claim.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't Spinoza just a materialist? He is hard to place. His one substance has thought as well as extension, so it is not matter alone. That is why idealists and materialists both claim him.
- Does Spinoza say the whole experiences separation? No. The essay takes only his one-substance claim. The rest of the axiom is Alex's.

## Albert Einstein, the 1929 telegram: 'I believe in Spinoza's God' (Einstein 1879 to 1955. Telegram April 1929, reported in the New York Times on 25 April 1929.)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein of the Institutional Synagogue in New York cabled Einstein, who was then in Berlin, to ask whether he believed in God. Einstein replied in a short telegram. It is the best known one-line statement of his religious views.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': 'When a rabbi cabled Einstein in 1929 to ask whether he believed in God, Einstein answered that he believed in Spinoza's God, though he meant the lawful order of nature and nothing that cares what we do.'

Where it sits: Telegram to Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein, April 1929; New York Times, 25 April 1929 (page not seen). The essay's Sources line gives this form. The reply was in German, so every English wording is a translation.

The passage: English as commonly reported, from the Skeptic article seen in the check: 'I believe in Spinoza's God, Who reveals Himself in the lawful harmony of the world, not in a God Who concerns Himself with the fate and the doings of mankind.' This is a translation of a German original, and wordings vary ('lawful harmony of all that exists', 'orderly harmony of what exists'). Safest in print: quote only 'I believe in Spinoza's God' and paraphrase the rest.

Read it free:
- Skeptic, What Did Einstein Believe About God? (gives the reply as reported): https://www.skeptic.com/article/what-did-einstein-believe-about-god/ (seen in a search result)
- Snopes, Did Einstein Say He Believed in the Pantheistic God of Baruch Spinoza?: https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/einstein-pantheism-baruch-spinoza/ (seen in a search result)
- Moment magazine, Einstein and His God: https://momentmag.com/einstein-and-his-god/ (seen in a search result)
- Scientific American blog, Einstein's famous God letter is up for auction (context on his views): https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/einsteins-famous-god-letter-is-up-for-auction/ (seen in a search result)
- wist.info, the telegram with the German text and an English version: https://wist.info/einstein-albert/7933/ (seen in a search result)
- arXiv paper on how the phrase became shorthand for his commitment to natural law: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.19695 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Max Jammer, Einstein and Religion (Princeton University Press, 1999). Walter Isaacson, Einstein: His Life and Universe (Simon and Schuster, 2007), chapter on religion. Alice Calaprice, The New Quotable Einstein (Princeton University Press, 2005) for checked wordings of his sayings.

Background: Goldstein wrote after Cardinal O'Connell of Boston attacked relativity as 'befogged speculation producing universal doubt about God and His Creation', and he wanted a public answer. He paid for up to fifty words and Einstein used about twenty-five. The point of the reply was to deny a personal God, one who looks after individuals, while affirming awe at the lawful order of nature. Einstein said the same thing in different ways for the rest of his life, including the 'cosmic religious feeling' of his essay 'Religion and Science' (New York Times, 9 November 1930, from memory). Spinoza's God is not a mind, so this telegram does not support 'all is one consciousness', and the essay does not claim it does. A closer witness for the axiom is a letter of 1950, usually said to be to a bereaved father, in which Einstein calls the sense of being separate 'a kind of optical delusion of consciousness' and says compassion is the way out of that prison. The English wording of that letter circulates in several versions, and I could not confirm the recipient. Check Calaprice or the Einstein Archive before quoting it. It is not in the essay.

If someone pushes back:
- Was Einstein religious? In his own sense, yes, he spoke of cosmic religious feeling. He rejected a personal God and also said he was no atheist. The essay claims only what the telegram says.
- Is the wording exact? It is a translation of a German telegram, so no. That is why the safe form is to quote 'Spinoza's God' only.

## Erwin Schrödinger, What Is Life? (1944) and Mind and Matter (1956 lectures) (Schrödinger 1887 to 1961. Nobel Prize in Physics 1933. What Is Life? 1944. Mind and Matter: Tarner Lectures, Trinity College, Cambridge, October 1956, published 1958.)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Austrian physicist who gave his name to the wave equation of quantum mechanics. In his later years he wrote two short books for general readers. What Is Life? asks how physics might explain living things, and ends with an epilogue on determinism and free will. Mind and Matter turns to consciousness. In both he returns to the idea that the plurality of minds is only apparent, and he links it to Indian philosophy, which he had read since youth, partly through Schopenhauer.

In the essay: 'Where the room splits': Schrödinger argued in 1944, and again in his 1956 lectures, that the plurality of minds is only apparent, and tied the idea to the Upanishads. In those lectures he wrote that the overall number of minds is just one, and added that at that point he was taking religion and not science, 'a religion he said was not opposed to science.'

Where it sits: What Is Life?, the closing Epilogue 'On Determinism and Free Will' (the Cambridge Core page confirms this chapter). Mind and Matter, chapter 4, 'The Arithmetical Paradox: The Oneness of Mind' (the Cambridge Core page confirms the chapter). Both are in the combined CUP volume What Is Life? with Mind and Matter and Autobiographical Sketches. One article cites p. 129 of that volume for the 'only one mind' sentences, which would be inside Mind and Matter.

The passage: Exact wording, in English as printed by the Marginalian and a 1981 Christian Science Monitor page (check the hyphenation in the CUP text): 'the over-all number of minds is just one'. A second passage reads 'Their multiplicity is only apparent, in truth there is only one mind', followed by a sentence calling this the doctrine of the Upanishads (Havel's article, which puts it on p. 129 of the combined volume). The same Monitor page prints Schrödinger saying he is now taking religion, not science, 'but religion not opposed to science'. Paraphrase of the 1944 Epilogue: consciousness is never experienced in the plural; the apparent plurality is a deception he names maya, as in a gallery of mirrors; and the identity of the personal self with the all-embracing self is the heart of Indian thought.

## The machine picture: process isolation and virtual memory (Virtual memory from the 1960s. Standard on desktop and server systems for decades (from memory).)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: The standard operating-system design in which each program (process) gets its own private virtual address space. The kernel sets it up and the memory management unit (hardware) enforces it, so by default a program cannot read another program's memory. This is the picture of 'one machine, many programs, each believing it has the whole memory'.

In the essay: 'The axiom and the machine': process isolation, two windows on a screen, 'my private memory is the self I feel from inside'. Later uses: the saved state in 'Not enough time in one life', 'a program that corrupts shared memory will eventually read the corrupted data back' in 'Karma, or the fingers', and sandbox escape in 'The small square and the big one'.

Where it sits: Arpaci-Dusseau and Arpaci-Dusseau, Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces, chapter 13 'The Abstraction: Address Spaces', and the chapters on address translation that follow.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of the chapter. The operating system builds an easy-to-use abstraction of physical memory called the address space, the running program's own view of memory (its code, stack and heap). When many programs share the same physical RAM, the system must stop one from reading or corrupting another, which is the protection problem. Each process therefore sees only its own private space.

Background: You know this field better than I do, so here is only what a skeptical reader would press on. 'By default' matters. The kernel and root can read any process. A debugger or a same-user process with the right permission can read another process via system calls. Shared memory is a deliberate exception: two processes map the same object into their address spaces, and then one can corrupt what the other reads. The essay is right to say it is using the computer as a picture and not endorsing the simulation hypothesis. The saved state is checkpointing or serialisation.

- Isn't this just the simulation hypothesis? No, and the essay says so. It is a picture of how one thing can look like many.
- Programs can share memory, so the walls are not real. The essay says the walls are real inside the system and are an arrangement, not a law. Shared memory and Meltdown show that exactly.

## Meltdown and Spectre (2018) (Disclosed 3 January 2018. Meltdown paper at USENIX Security, August 2018. Spectre paper at IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, 2019.)

Kind: study. Status: checked.

Who: Two families of hardware flaws, found independently by several teams, that exploit speculative execution in modern processors to leak data across the boundaries that operating systems rely on. Meltdown is the one that reads kernel memory from an ordinary program. Spectre tricks a program into speculatively leaking its own secrets, and it works across process and sandbox boundaries.

In the essay: 'The axiom and the machine': 'The walls are real... though they are an arrangement and not a law: in 2018 the Meltdown and Spectre flaws showed that information could leak across them.'

Where it sits: Lipp et al., 'Meltdown: Reading Kernel Memory from User Space', USENIX Security 2018 (arXiv 1801.01207). Kocher et al., 'Spectre Attacks: Exploiting Speculative Execution' (arXiv 1801.01203; IEEE S and P 2019). Note that 1801.01203 is the Spectre paper.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Meltdown exploits side effects of out-of-order execution to read arbitrary kernel memory locations, and the paper says that on affected systems it can also reach memory of other processes and of virtual machines in the cloud without permission. The root cause is hardware, and the attack does not depend on operating-system bugs. Spectre, in the words of one summary seen in the check, bypasses application isolation by forcing an application to disclose data it should protect.

Background: Meltdown and Spectre were made public together on 3 January 2018 after months of private coordination among researchers at Google Project Zero, Graz University of Technology and others, and the chip makers. Both exploit speculative execution: a modern processor guesses ahead and runs instructions before it knows it should, then discards the results but leaves traces in the cache that can be measured. Meltdown (CVE-2017-5754) let an ordinary program read kernel memory, and through it on affected systems the memory of other processes, because the privilege check was effectively delayed behind the speculative read. It hit mainly Intel processors, with some IBM and ARM designs affected. Spectre (CVE-2017-5753 and CVE-2017-5715, from memory) works on a wider range of processors, including AMD and ARM. Fixes include kernel page-table isolation, retpolines and microcode, with a performance cost, and whole families of related attacks followed. The point the essay needs is modest. The wall is a promise made by the architecture, and the hardware did not keep it perfectly. The checklist says 'across the walls between processes' fits Spectre directly and Meltdown via the kernel. It does not show that walls in general are illusions.

If someone pushes back:
- That was a bug, so it proves nothing about walls. Correct. The essay uses it only to show that the wall is an arrangement and not a law.
- Did Meltdown cross process walls? On affected systems, yes, via the kernel, per the paper. Spectre does so directly.

NASA's classroom page says about 90 percent, and inverse-square arithmetic gives 88.5 percent at 400 km and 88.0 percent at 420 km, so the essay's 88 percent is correct at the upper end. Astronauts float because they and the station are in continuous free fall around the Earth, which NASA calls microgravity. 'Weightless' means they do not feel gravity's pull, not that it is absent. The inclination of the orbit is about 51.6 degrees (from memory of NASA's ISS facts), so the station only passes over latitudes up to that value. That is the one place where the essay's 'you can reach any point on Earth very quickly' needs a footnote: the poles are out of reach from this orbit. - Isn't it wrong to say there is gravity on the ISS? It is right. Astronauts feel weightless because of free fall, not because gravity is absent.
- Is 'any point on Earth' true? Not for the polar regions from the ISS orbit. The essay says 'quickly', and a precise reader will want the footnote.

## Orbital speed and escape speed (7.7, 10.8 and 11.2 km/s, 41 percent) (Classical mechanics, Newton onward.)

Kind: concept. Status: checked.

Who: At any distance from a planet's centre, the speed needed to escape its gravity is the square root of two times the speed of a circular orbit there. The essay uses the numbers 7.7 and 10.8 km/s at the height of the ISS, and 11.2 km/s from Earth's surface.

In the essay: 'The arrow': 'The top right corner of the small square is escape velocity... about 11.2 kilometers per second from the surface and about 10.8 at the height of the space station. Notice how little extra it takes. At any height, escape speed is about 41 percent higher than orbital speed there, and the difference in outcome is total.' It is also drawn in the figure 'orbit versus escape'.

Where it sits: Britannica, 'escape velocity'; a Rochester astronomy lecture slide on escape velocity; Hałas and Pacek on the cosmic velocities.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Escape speed equals the square root of 2 (about 1.414) times circular orbital speed at the same distance from the centre. Surface escape speed is about 11.2 km/s (11.186). A like-for-like calculation gives 7.67 km/s for orbit and 10.84 km/s for escape at ISS height, which supports the essay's 10.8 and 41 percent. Note that 11.2 divided by 7.7 is 1.45, because the two figures are at different heights.

- University of Rochester astronomy lecture slide, escape velocity: https://astro.pas.rochester.edu/ast104/lec/lec8/tsld006.htm (seen in a search result)
- Hałas and Pacek, Controversies about the value of the third cosmic velocity (also lists the second as 11.186 km/s): https://lsmll.journals.umcs.pl/aaa/article/view/807 (seen in a search result)
- issinfo.net, ISS speed (7.67 km/s): https://issinfo.net/blog/tracking-the-iss-why-its-orbit-altitude-and-speed-constantly-change (seen in a search result)

Background: The relationship follows from energy. A circular orbit has kinetic energy equal to half the depth of the gravitational well at that distance. Escape needs kinetic energy equal to the whole depth. Doubling the kinetic energy multiplies the speed by the square root of two. That gives 41 percent more speed, and twice the energy, which is why launching to escape takes much more fuel than reaching orbit. At the surface the figures are 7.9 km/s for a hypothetical circular orbit and 11.19 km/s to escape. At ISS height, with standard constants, I get 7.67 km/s and 10.84 km/s, so the essay's 7.7, 10.8 and 41 percent are consistent. Three caveats. Escape means moving fast enough that Earth's pull can never bring you back; gravity never reaches zero. It assumes no air drag and ignores the Sun and Moon. And the checklist says the ISS decays slowly through drag, so 'circles forever' is true only in the drag-free ideal. In the metaphor, orbit is the high agency room and escape speed is the corner of the small square.

If someone pushes back:
- Escape velocity isn't how rockets leave. Correct, a rocket with a continuous engine need not reach escape speed at once. The essay uses escape velocity as a threshold, not as a flight plan.
- 41 percent isn't 'little extra' in energy terms. Right, energy doubles. The essay says 'speed' and is accurate on that.

- Why 17 and not 42? Because 42 is relative to the Sun at Earth's orbit. From Earth's surface, launched forward, Earth's own motion does part of the work.

Voyager 2 crossed in 2018 (from memory).

- Isn't the universe infinite? Maybe. The observable universe is the part we can see. The essay says 'perhaps larger' for that reason.

Background: 'Multiverse' names several different ideas, and 'speculate' is right for all of them. Feeney and colleagues searched the WMAP 7-year map for the marks a collision with another bubble would leave and found no clear detection, so 'nobody has tested it' is too strong. Critics such as George Ellis and Paul Steinhardt say that an untestable idea is not yet science (from memory). Supporters such as Carroll reply that it follows from theories testable in other ways. Strictly, 'universe' already means everything, so 'multiverse' is a name for 'more than we can see'.

If someone pushes back:
- Is the multiverse science? That is debated. The essay says only that some physicists speculate, and that it is unconfirmed.
- Has it been tested? Searches for one kind of signal have run and found nothing clear. That is not a refutation.
