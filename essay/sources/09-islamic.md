## Quran 2:115 (wherever you turn)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verse 115 of Surat al-Baqara (the Cow), the long second chapter of the Quran. It says the east and the west belong to God, that wherever you turn there is the Face of God, and that God is all-encompassing and all-knowing.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': one of the traditions that say something near the first clause ('wherever you turn there is the face of God (2:115)'). Also the table, Islam column, row 'The one whole', and the Sources list.

Where it sits: Quran 2:115, Surat al-Baqara

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: to God belong the east and the west, and wherever you turn there is the Face of God; God is all-encompassing and knowing. A search result this session confirms that the Sahih International text on quran.com uses 'the Face of Allah'; the wording 'face of God' in the essay matches other translators and older renderings. The essay names no translation for this verse.

Read it free:
- Quran 2:115 with Sahih International and other translations (legacy quran.com): https://legacy.quran.com/2/115-211 (seen in a search result)
- Verse with commentary pages (quran.com): https://quran.com/al-baqarah/115/hadith (seen in a search result)
- Parallel translations of 2:115: https://parallelquran.com/parallel/2/115/ (seen in a search result)
- Tafsir for 2:115 (QuranWorld): https://quranworld.net/surah/al-baqarah/115/tafsir (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, The Qur'an, Oxford World's Classics (2004, revised 2008). For the classical commentary beside each verse: The Study Quran, ed. Seyyed Hossein Nasr and others (HarperOne, 2015). Marmaduke Pickthall (1930) and Yusuf Ali (1934) are older and easy to find free online.

Background: The Arabic for face is wajh, a word that can mean face, direction or the self of a thing, and the readings part there. Early commentators, with Ibn Abbas and Mujahid named on the tafsir pages the checklist cites, tie the verse to prayer. Someone who prays in the wrong direction by mistake, in the dark or while travelling has not lost the prayer, because God is not confined to one direction. The verse sits in the same chapter as the passage on changing the direction of prayer (2:142 to 150). Sufi writers read the same words as a present fact, God's presence in every place. Mainstream theology reads it as God's knowledge, power and mercy reaching everywhere, which keeps God from being a thing located in space. The essay uses it to show that Islam also has a text where the divine is everywhere. That is fair as long as the essay says whose reading it gives. The verse does not say we are one with God or with each other. It says no place is outside God's face. A safe sentence for Alex's own talks: Muslims read it as a rule about prayer and as a statement that God is present everywhere, and the mystical tradition leans on the second.

If someone pushes back:
- That verse is about the direction of prayer. Yes, that is the classical occasion. The essay only claims the wording is there and that Sufis read it as presence everywhere.
- Isn't that pantheism? The verse stops at presence and encompassing, and mainstream theology keeps the line between creator and creature firm.

## Quran 57:3-4 (the Outward and the Inward; with you wherever you are)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Verses 3 and 4 of Surat al-Hadid (Iron). Verse 3 names God as the First and the Last, the Outward (al-Zahir) and the Inward (al-Batin). Verse 4 says God created the heavens and the earth in six days, knows what enters the earth and what leaves it, is with you wherever you are, and sees what you do.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': 'God is with you wherever you are (57:4)'. Verse 3 appears only in the Sources list (57:3-4).

Where it sits: Quran 57:3 and 57:4, Surat al-Hadid

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: God is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward, and He is with you wherever you are. The exact English depends on the translator and was not seen in full this session; take the translation from quran.com and name it in the essay.

Read it free:
- Quran 57:4 with Maarif-ul-Quran commentary (quran.com): https://quran.com/57:4/tafsirs/en-tafsir-maarif-ul-quran (seen in a search result)
- Quran 57:4 (quran.is): https://www.quran.is/57/4 (seen in a search result)
- Salafi-leaning commentary on 57:4 (tohed.com): https://en.tohed.com/tafsir/57/4/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004) or The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015), which gives the classical readings of both verses side by side.

Background: Surat al-Hadid opens with all creation glorifying God, and verse 3 stacks up names: the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward. Verse 4 goes on to the six days, the Throne, what enters the earth and what leaves it, and then says God is with you wherever you are and sees what you do. Classical commentators read 'with you' as accompaniment by knowledge, hearing and sight, which fits the closing clause about seeing your deeds. The Salafi-leaning commentary the checklist cites states it plainly: God is above the Throne and with his creatures by knowledge. Sufi writers read the Outward and the Inward as one reality seen from two sides, and the pair of names keeps returning in their metaphysics. The neighbouring verse 50:16, nearer than the jugular vein, gets the same two readings, with classical commentators taking it as nearness of knowledge and power. For the essay's purpose the verse supports 'God is with you wherever you are' and no more. It does not say there is only one thing. If someone presses Alex, he can say these verses belong to all Muslims, and that the oneness reading is the mystics' way of hearing them.

If someone pushes back:
- Mainstream commentary says 'with you' means by knowledge. That is right, and the essay should say it is giving the mystical reading when it uses the verse as a witness for oneness.
- Verse 3 is only in the Sources list. Either quote it in the body (the Outward and the Inward) or drop it from the list.

## Quran 7:172 (Am I not your Lord?)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verse 172 of Surat al-A'raf. God takes from the loins of Adam's children their offspring and makes them testify about themselves, asking 'Am I not your Lord?' They answer yes, and the verse gives the reason: so that on the Day of Resurrection they cannot say they were unaware of this.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'God asks the descendants of Adam, "Am I not your Lord?", and Sufis read that moment as the origin the soul has forgotten and longs to return to.' Table, Islam column, row 'The veil'.

Where it sits: Quran 7:172, Surat al-A'raf (with 7:173 as the second reason)

The passage: Seen this session: Yusuf Ali (1934) renders the question 'Am I not your Lord (Who cherishes and sustains you)?'. Sahih International renders the reply 'Yes, we have testified' and the reason as 'lest you should say on the day of Resurrection, Indeed, we were of this unaware.' (Both wordings came through search-result summaries of the quran.com and sacred-texts pages; check them on the page.)

Read it free:
- Quran 7:172 with translations (legacy quran.com, Sahih International and others): https://legacy.quran.com/7/172-206 (seen in a search result)
- Yusuf Ali translation of this passage (sacred-texts.com): https://sacred-texts.com/isl/quran/00722.htm (seen in a search result)
- The Day of Alast (Wikipedia): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alast (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004); The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015) for the classical debate. For the Sufi theme of the covenant: Carl W. Ernst, The Shambhala Guide to Sufism (1997), and Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (UNC Press, 1975).

Background: The Arabic is a-lastu bi-rabbikum, qalu bala shahidna. The verse gives its own reason, a legal one: nobody can plead on the Day of Resurrection that they did not know. The next verse adds a second reason about ancestors who set up partners with God. Commentators argue about what happened. Some take a literal pre-existence of all souls, others a figure for the natural disposition to recognise God (fitra), and al-Zamakhshari read it figuratively, as the checklist notes. The Sufis made it central. They call it the Day of Alast after its first Arabic word, treat it as the first hearing of God's voice, and see in it the origin the soul half remembers and longs to return to. Carl Ernst describes it as the day creation was bound to God by love, and Junayd and Rumi are the names the checklist gives for that reading. So 'forgotten' is the Sufi gloss, and the essay's sentence already puts it in the Sufis' mouths. The table cell, though, shows 'the forgotten covenant' under Islam with no such label, and it should carry one. The verse offers a covenant and a witness. It does not say the separation is an illusion. It is the nearest Quranic match for forgetting and returning, and Alex can say that without claiming more.

If someone pushes back:
- The verse never says the soul forgot. Correct. That is the Sufi and later reading; the verse's own point is that no one can claim ignorance.
- Is this a real event? Mainstream exegesis is divided between a literal pre-existence and a figure for innate disposition, and the essay need not choose.

## Quran 2:156 (to Him we return)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verse 156 of Surat al-Baqara. It describes the patient, who say when disaster strikes that they belong to God and to Him they return. In Arabic: inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un.

In the essay: Table, Islam column, row 'The return': '"To Him we return" (2:156)'. Also in the Sources list.

Where it sits: Quran 2:155 to 157, centred on 2:156

The passage: Sahih International, as seen this session on a tafsir page: 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.' The essay's short quote 'To Him we return' needs a named translation; Abdel Haleem is reported to read 'to Him we shall return' (second-hand, check).

Read it free:
- Quran 2:156 (quran.is, with several translations): https://quran.is/2/156 (seen in a search result)
- Tafsir of 2:156 (Quranpedia): https://quranpedia.net/tafsir/al-baqara/156/en (seen in a search result)
- Word-by-word analysis of 2:156: https://parallelquran.com/lexicon-verse/2/156/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004), or The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015).

Background: The verse comes in a passage on trials (2:155 to 157). God will test people with fear, hunger and loss of wealth, lives and fruits, and good news goes to the patient, who say, when disaster strikes, that they belong to God and to him they return. Muslims say the formula on hearing of a death or a loss, so it is one of the most spoken lines in the Quran. The mainstream reading of the return is resurrection and judgment; the research round found the standard tafsir on the parallel verse 2:28 reading it that way. The Sufi reading adds a return of the soul to its source within this life, and cites verses such as 89:27 to 30, where the soul at peace is told to return to its Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing. The words are shared, and the meanings are where the readings diverge. For the axiom's third clause the verse supplies the vocabulary of return. It does not say the separation ends within a life, and it covers everyone, not only the saved. Alex can also point out that the verse teaches a kind of letting go: the response to loss is an act of release into God's keeping.

If someone pushes back:
- That return means resurrection. Yes, in the mainstream reading. The essay's table only shows that Islam has the word return in a central place.
- It is recited at funerals. Right, which shows how deeply the idea of returning is part of ordinary Muslim life.

Translation check (2026-10-08): Sahih International reads 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.' The essay now quotes 'indeed to Him we will return (2:156, Sahih International)'.

## Quran 3:83 (everything submits; Islam as surrender)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Verse 83 of Surat Al Imran. It asks whether people seek a religion other than God's, when everyone in the heavens and the earth has submitted to Him, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will be returned.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'The word Islam means submission.' Table, Islam column, row 'Letting go': 'Islam means surrender (3:83).'

Where it sits: Quran 3:83 (for the word itself, 3:19, 2:112 and 3:20 are more usual locators, per the checklist)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: all in the heavens and the earth have submitted (aslama) to God, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will be returned. I did not see the exact English of this verse in a translation this session.

Read it free:
- Quran 3:83 on quran.com (the address the checklist names; not seen as a search result): https://quran.com/3/83 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004), who renders aslama with 'submit'; The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015) discusses the broad and the narrow sense of the word in its notes on 3:19 and 3:83.

Background: Islam is the verbal noun of aslama, to submit or surrender, from the root s-l-m, which also gives salam, peace. In 3:83 the word is used in a wide sense: everything in the heavens and the earth has submitted to God's order, willingly or unwillingly. That is why the checklist says 3:19 (the religion with God is islam), 2:112 and 3:20 are the usual places to cite for the word itself, and 3:83 a good second for the universal sense. The Quran uses the word both for the personal act of surrender and for the community that follows the Prophet. The essay pairs the word with letting go, and the pairing is an interpretation of its own. Quranic submission means trust in God and obedience to what He commands. The Sufi tradition adds that the self's own claims give way, and later writers speak of trust (tawakkul) and contentment (rida) as stations on the way. Part one's letting go is about tension and non-acceptance, which sits nearer those stations than it does to law. The verse also repeats the return found in 2:156, a useful link when Alex talks about it.

If someone pushes back:
- Islam means obeying rules, with no release in it. In law, yes; the essay is using the Sufi and ethical sense, and should say so.
- Why 3:83 and not 3:19? Because 3:83 shows the universal sense. Citing 3:19 as well would make the locator safer.

## Quran 17:7 (you do good for yourselves)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verse 7 of Surat al-Isra (also called Bani Isra'il). It sits inside a passage about the Children of Israel and states a general rule: if you do good you do it for yourselves, and if you do evil, it is to yourselves.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers': 'The Quran says that if you do good, you do good for yourselves, and if you do evil, you do it to yourselves (17:7, in the Sahih International rendering)'. Table, Islam column, row 'What you do to another'.

Where it sits: Quran 17:7, Surat al-Isra

The passage: Sahih International, as shown in the checklist from legacy.quran.com: 'If you do good, you do good for yourselves; and if you do evil, [you do it] to yourselves.'

Read it free:
- Quran 17:7 with Sahih International (legacy quran.com): https://legacy.quran.com/17/7-85 (seen in a search result)
- Tafsir page for 17:7 (QuranWorld): https://quranworld.net/surah/al-isra/7/tafsir (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004); The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015) for commentary on the closing preposition.

Background: Surat al-Isra opens with the night journey and then turns to the Children of Israel. Verses 4 to 8 say they would cause corruption in the land twice and that God would raise others against them, and verse 7 sits in the middle of that with a general rule. The Arabic of the last clause is fa-laha, and translators differ on whether the evil is 'to' or 'against' the doer; the checklist shows the first clause using a preposition meaning 'for'. The plain sense is accountability: deeds return to the one who does them. Classical commentary reads it as justice before God, in this world and the next. The essay's step goes further than the verse. It reads the line as 'what I do to another I do to myself', and the checklist rightly calls that an interpretive step. The Quran does not teach karma as an impersonal law carried across lives, and the verse is addressed to Israel in a story of corruption and punishment. It is a good example of the shape the essay points at, consequences that come back to the doer, in a theistic frame with a judge. Alex should keep the shape and the frame together.

If someone pushes back:
- The verse is about reward and punishment before God, and has no hint of oneness. Right. The essay says the idea comes out as a consequence of the axiom, and the Quran's version is accountability.
- Which translation? Sahih International, and the essay already says so.

Translation check (2026-10-08): quran.com prints Sahih International as '[And said], If you do good, you do good for yourselves; and if you do evil, [you do it] to yourselves.' Another printing of the same translation reads 'to them [i.e. yourselves]'. The Arabic second half is wa-in asa'tum fa-laha, 'then it is for/against it', the pronoun referring back to the souls.

## Quran 99:7-8 (an atom's weight)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The last two verses of Surat al-Zalzala (the Quake), a short chapter on the Day when the earth is shaken and people are shown their deeds. Whoever has done an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever has done an atom's weight of evil will see it.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers': 'whoever does an atom's weight of good or evil will see it (99:7 and 99:8)'.

Where it sits: Quran 99:7 and 99:8

The passage: Sahih International, as quoted in the checklist from quran.is: 'whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it ... and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it.'

Read it free:
- Quran 99:7-8 (quran.is): https://www.quran.is/99/7/8 (seen in a search result)
- Quran 99:7 (hadithunlocked): https://hadithunlocked.com/quran:99:7 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004), who renders it 'an atom's weight' in a similar way (check); The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015).

Background: The chapter is short. It describes the Day when the earth is shaken and brings out its burdens, and people come forward in groups to be shown their deeds. The last two verses say that whoever has done an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever has done an atom's weight of evil will see it. The Arabic word dharra means a tiny particle, or a small ant, and Ibn Abbas is reported to have said the head of a red ant (a research-round note, not checked here). The classical commentators the checklist names, Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi, read the verses as divine justice at the Judgment: nothing is lost, however small. That is a sense of consequence that fits the karma section, but it has a different structure. Here the deeds are recorded and revealed at the end, in front of God. The verses say nothing about the consequences coming back to the same person in a later life. The essay places the verses beside 17:7 as a Quranic form of 'consequences that come back to the one who acts', which is fair. Alex should not say the Quran teaches karma, and the essay does not. Related verses the research round lists: 41:46 and 45:15, which are worth checking.

If someone pushes back:
- This is about the Last Day, so it is not karma. Correct, and the essay presents it as a parallel in shape, with a different frame.
- Is 'atom' the right word? Dharra covers a tiny particle or ant; 'atom' is a translator's choice.

## Quran 5:32 (one soul as all mankind)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verse 32 of Surat al-Ma'ida, following the story of the two sons of Adam. God decreed for the Children of Israel that whoever kills a person, unless for a person or for corruption in the land, it is as if he had killed all mankind, and whoever saves one, as if he had saved all mankind.

In the essay: Sources list only ('Quran 2:115, 2:156, 3:83, 5:32, ...'). The body text does not use it at present.

Where it sits: Quran 5:32, Surat al-Ma'ida (parallel: Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5)

The passage: Sahih International, as seen this session: 'it is as if he had slain mankind entirely. And whoever saves one - it is as if he had saved mankind entirely.'

Read it free:
- Quran 5:32 with Sahih International (legacy quran.com): https://legacy.quran.com/5/32-44 (seen in a search result)
- Tafsir page for 5:32 with a summary of Ibn Kathir (QuranWorld): https://quranworld.net/surah/al-maidah/32/tafsir (seen in a search result)
- Aaron Koller, 'Three Polemical Qur'anic Citations of the Mishnah' (JSQ 2025), publisher page from the research round: https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/article/three-polemical-quranic-citations-of-the-mishnah-and-their-historical-significance-101628jsq-2025-0007 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004); The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015).

Background: The verse is in Surat al-Ma'ida, right after the story of the two sons of Adam, one of whom kills the other (unnamed in the Quran, Cain and Abel in the Bible). It says that because of that, God decreed for the Children of Israel that whoever kills a person, unless for a person or for corruption in the land, it is as if he had killed all mankind, and whoever saves one, as if he had saved all mankind. The wording closely parallels Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, 'whoever destroys one life destroys a world', and a research-round note reports that Aaron Koller (Journal of Semitic Studies, 2025) argues the Quran is quoting the Mishnah polemically. The research round also notes that many editions of the Mishnah add 'of Israel' after 'one life', and the Jerusalem Talmud omits it. Classical commentary, as the QuranWorld page summarises Ibn Kathir, reads the comparison as a deliberate exaggeration about the gravity of unjust killing and the value of a single life. So the verse speaks of the unity of the human race in value, and makes no claim about unity of being. If Alex wants a Quranic text for 'what you do to one you do to all', this is the one, with the caveat that it is a legal and moral comparison framed as a decree to Israel.

If someone pushes back:
- The verse is not about oneness. Agreed. It is about the worth of a single life, and the essay should not lean on it for the axiom.
- Why is it listed but not used? It was left in the Sources list after the body text changed; either cite it in the fingers section or remove it.

## Quran 23:99-100 (the dying ask to be sent back)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Verses 99 and 100 of Surat al-Mu'minun (the Believers). When death comes to one of the people described in the passage, he says 'my Lord, send me back' so that he might act rightly. The reply is 'No!', and behind them is a barrier (barzakh) until the day they are resurrected.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'the Quran (23:99 to 100) has the dying ask to be sent back and be refused.' The word barzakh appears in the table, Islam column, row 'What continues'.

Where it sits: Quran 23:99 and 23:100

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: a dying person pleads to be sent back to do good; the answer is 'No!', it is only a word he says, and a barrier lies behind them until the Day they are raised. The wording 'My Lord, send me back' and 'No!' was seen in a search summary; the full English text was not reproduced this session.

Read it free:
- Quran 23:94-100 (legacy quran.com): https://legacy.quran.com/23/94-100 (seen in a search result)
- The Realm of Barzakh (WikiShia), with the tafsir of 23:100: https://en.wikishia.net/view/Realm_of_Barzakh (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Abdel Haleem (Oxford World's Classics, 2004); The Study Quran (HarperOne, 2015) has the classical readings of the barzakh clause.

Background: The passage describes people who rejected the message, and says that when death comes to one of them he asks to be sent back so that he might do right in what he left behind. The answer is 'No!', it is only a word he says, and behind them is a barrier until the day they are resurrected. Classical commentators read the plea as the regret of the heedless at the moment of death, and the refusal as final: there is no second try in this world. The barrier is the barzakh, the state between death and resurrection, and this verse is the Quranic source for the word in that sense. Later writers use the verses against transmigration, since they show a return to this world refused. The research round could not find a source that ties this verse specifically to the rejection of tanasukh, so the safe statement is the one the essay makes: the verse has the dying ask to be sent back and be refused. Note that the verse is about one kind of person, the heedless, and about a request for a chance to make amends. It supports 'something continues after death' and also 'there is no return to this life', and the essay says so when it admits the traditions are not unanimous.

If someone pushes back:
- The verse is about disbelievers, so it is no general rule. True; the essay says only what the verse shows, a refused return.
- Does it rule out other lives? In the mainstream reading it does, and the essay lists Islam on that side.

## Barzakh (the barrier, the in-between state)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: An Arabic word for a barrier or partition. In the Quran it is used for the barrier between the two seas and, once, for the barrier behind the dead until the resurrection (23:100). In later Islamic theology it names the state between death and resurrection.

In the essay: Table, Islam column, row 'What continues': 'The barzakh.' Also behind the sentence about Quran 23:99 to 100.

Where it sits: Quran 23:100 (the barrier clause); 25:53 and 55:20 for the barrier between the seas (from memory, check); Encyclopaedia of the Quran or Encyclopaedia of Islam, 'Barzakh' (the checklist's pointer)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: the dead are in a waiting state, a barrier, until the day they are raised. Mainstream teaching adds some form of awareness there (hadith about the questioning in the grave). No exact passage seen.

Read it free:
- The Realm of Barzakh (WikiShia): https://en.wikishia.net/view/Realm_of_Barzakh (seen in a search result)
- Quran 23:94-100 (legacy quran.com): https://legacy.quran.com/23/94-100 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Encyclopaedia of the Quran (Brill, 2001 to 2006) or Encyclopaedia of Islam, THREE (Brill), entries on Barzakh. For the Sufi and philosophical meanings: William Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge (SUNY, 1989), on imagination as a barzakh (check the index).

Background: In the Quran the word means a barrier. It separates the two seas so that they do not mix (25:53 and 55:20, from memory, check), and it stands behind the dead until the resurrection (23:100). Later theology made it the name of the whole state between death and resurrection. In mainstream Sunni and Shia teaching the dead are in barzakh with some form of awareness: hadith literature describes questioning in the grave by two angels and ease or distress until the Last Day, and scholars argue over the details, including whether it is the soul alone that experiences this. Philosophers and Sufis give the term a richer meaning. Ibn Arabi treats the barzakh as an isthmus between two things, the imaginal realm between spirit and body, and Chittick presents imagination itself as a barzakh, neither this nor that, which is how he connects it to 'He/not He' (from memory, check the chapters). Mulla Sadra uses the term for the world in which deeds take form. For the essay, the word does two jobs. It supports 'development continues after death' without a return to this life. It also shows that Islam's answer to 'what continues' is a single earthly life and a waiting state, which is the side of the traditions that do not teach rebirth.

If someone pushes back:
- Is barzakh only a Shia idea? No. It is Quranic, and both Sunni and Shia teach it.
- Does it support lifetimes? No. It is a waiting state between one life and the resurrection.

## Hadith: none of you believes until he loves for his brother (Bukhari 13, Muslim 45)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A saying of the Prophet Muhammad reported by Anas ibn Malik, a Companion who entered the Prophet's service as a boy in Medina and served him for about ten years. It stands in the Book of Faith of both Bukhari and Muslim, and is number 13 in al-Nawawi's Forty Hadith.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers', the golden rule paragraph: 'A saying reported in Bukhari and Muslim has the Prophet say that none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.'

## Ibn Arabi (Muhyi al-Din Ibn al-'Arabi), 'He/not He' (1165 to 1240)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Born in Murcia in Muslim Spain, he lived in Seville, Mecca and Damascus and died in Damascus. A mystic and theorist of Sufism, he wrote the Futuhat al-Makkiyya (The Meccan Openings) and the Fusus al-Hikam (The Bezels of Wisdom). His followers call him al-Shaykh al-Akbar, the greatest master.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine' (the traditions that say something near the first clause: 'the great Sufi Ibn Arabi called all things "He/not He," in William Chittick's rendering, a self-disclosure of God that is also not God'); the paragraph on the hidden treasure saying; table, Islam column, row 'The one whole'.

Where it sits: The phrase huwa la huwa, which Chittick renders 'He/not He'. Discussed in Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge (1989), The Self-Disclosure of God (1998) and Ibn 'Arabi: Heir to the Prophets (Oneworld, 2005). Exact page not located.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: everything other than God is 'He/not He', a self-disclosure (tajalli) of God that is still not God. The two-word formula 'He/not He' is Chittick's English for the Arabic, seen in a Goodreads quotation from his Heir to the Prophets and in a thesis summary on Ibn Arabi and tawhid.

Read it free:
- Summary of Dhanidina's thesis on Ibn Arabi, 'He/not He' and tajalli: https://ismailimail.blog/2016/03/05/adil-s-dhanidina-experiencing-tawhid-ibn-arabi-and-the-power-of-imagination/ (seen in a search result)
- Goodreads quotations from Ibn Arabi, including the Chittick passage on 'He/not He': https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/187247 (seen in a search result)
- Nicholson's 1911 translation of Ibn Arabi's Tarjuman al-Ashwaq (sacred-texts.com), public domain: https://sacred-texts.com/isl/taa/index.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: William C. Chittick, Ibn 'Arabi: Heir to the Prophets (Oneworld, 2005), the shortest way in. For the Fusus: R. W. J. Austin, The Bezels of Wisdom (Paulist Press, 1980), or Caner Dagli, Ringstones of Wisdom (Kazi, 2004) (both from memory, check). For the poems: Michael Sells, The Translator of Desires (Princeton University Press, 2021).

Background: Ibn Arabi's problem is how God can be beyond everything and present in everything. His answer is a double statement. Tanzih says God is incomparable: only God has real being (wujud). Tashbih says the world is God's self-disclosure (tajalli), so to see anything is to see a face of God. Put together, a thing is 'He/not He'. Chittick, the main English interpreter, stresses that Ibn Arabi will not let either half cancel the other. That puts him close to the first clause of the axiom, one whole with everything inside it, and to the second in a sense, since the 'not He' side is real for those who live in it. He is further from the third clause: he keeps creature and Lord distinct to the end, and the fana that dissolves the self's claims is followed by baqa, servanthood continuing. The label wahdat al-wujud, unity of being, was attached to his teaching by later followers and by critics, and specialists such as Chittick note he did not use it himself (research-round note). Ibn Taymiyya, al-Taftazani and al-Biqa'i attacked him, and al-Suyuti and al-Sha'rani defended him. So 'great' in the essay is a followers' judgment, and a contested one. He stayed inside Islam, and his poems speak of love more than of a metaphysical One.

If someone pushes back:
- Is he a pantheist? Specialists mostly say no, because he holds tanzih (God beyond the world) as firmly as tashbih (God in the world).
- Isn't he rejected by many Muslims? Yes. Ibn Taymiyya and the Salafi line reject his ontology; others have defended and revered him. The essay calls him 'the great Sufi', which is the followers' view, and could say 'the influential Sufi' for safety.

Read it in a book: R. A. Nicholson, The Mathnawi of Jalalu'ddin Rumi, volume IV, the translation of Books III and IV (Luzac, 1930 or 1934, sources differ; check the title page). Jawid Mojaddedi's Oxford World's Classics Masnavi, Book Three (from memory) is easier to read.

Background: The elephant parable is much older than Rumi. The Buddhist Udana has the blind men and the elephant, and the Jains use it for many-sidedness. In Persian it appears in Sana'i's Hadiqat al-Haqiqa (he died in 1131), which Rumi follows with changes. Rumi's version puts the elephant in a dark house, so the failure is of sight, and his moral is that the sense-eye is like the palm of the hand, which cannot reach the whole animal. With a candle the differences would vanish. Rumi does not conclude that every view is as good as another. His point is that partial perception produces partial claims, and someone with light sees the whole. That sits easily with the axiom (a whole seen from one position) and with the essay's care that the story was not written to say all religions are one. The line numbers in the essay come from the Nicholson text, and a different translation will number differently. If Alex wants to quote lines in a talk, he should open Nicholson's volume IV and copy the lines with the translator named, since this session found no free Nicholson text of the passage.

If someone pushes back:
- The Jains and Buddhists told it first. True, and the essay says so; it lists Rumi as one retelling.
- Rumi's moral is about the superiority of the Sufi's light. Fair; it is a partial view story that hands the whole to one who sees with light, which is an inside-Islam point.

## Fana and baqa (passing away and subsistence in Sufism)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: A pair of Sufi technical terms. Fana' is passing away or effacement of the self; baqa' is abiding or subsistence, the life that continues afterward with God. They name two moments in the Sufi path rather than a doctrine of identity.

In the essay: Part two: 'the Sufis speak of the passing away of the self in God' (letting go paragraph) and 'the Sufis with the passing away of the self' (the list of vocabularies); table, Islam column, row 'The return': 'Sufis: fana, the passing away of the self, then baqa, subsistence in God and living on in the world.'

Where it sits: Standard terms from the ninth to eleventh centuries. Classic definitions: Hujwiri, Kashf al-Mahjub (eleventh century; Nicholson's 1911 translation on Gutenberg); al-Qushayri, Risala (1045, from memory); al-Junayd's letters.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Britannica says that after fana the seeker is revived and reaches baqa, the 'second sobriety'. The Iranica article is titled 'baqa wa fana: Sufi term signifying subsistence and passing away'. The checklist's fix: 'baqa, subsistence in God, living on in the world', not simply 'the return to life'.

Read it free:
- Encyclopaedia Iranica, 'baqa wa fana': https://iranicaonline.org/articles/baqa-wa-fana-sufi-term-signifying-subsistence-and-passing-away (seen in a search result)
- Britannica, 'fana': https://www.britannica.com/topic/fana-Sufism (seen in a search result)
- Hujwiri, Kashf al-Mahjub, Nicholson's 1911 translation (Project Gutenberg #64786): https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/64786/pg64786-images.html (seen in a search result)
- Kashf al-Mahjub (Internet Archive, University of Toronto scan): https://archive.org/details/kashfalmahjub00usmauoft (seen in a search result)
- Kashf al-Mahjub (Encyclopaedia Iranica): https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kasf_al_mahjub_hojviri/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Annemarie Schimmel, Mystical Dimensions of Islam (UNC Press, 1975); Carl W. Ernst, The Shambhala Guide to Sufism (1997); Hujwiri in Nicholson's 1911 translation (free), or Leslie Kean's Revelation of the Mystery (1999). Search the text for 'annihilation' and 'subsistence', since Nicholson's wording may differ from fana and baqa.

Background: Fana and baqa are the pair Sufi writers use for the central moment of the path. Fana is the passing away of the self's claims, its attributes or its awareness of itself; baqa is what remains and goes on, the person living with God-consciousness in ordinary life. Junayd of Baghdad (died 910), the model of the 'sober' school, stressed that the passing away is followed by a return to sobriety, and Britannica calls that the second sobriety. Bistami (died around 874) is the model of the 'ecstatic' school. The research round notes that the scholar R. C. Zaehner argued Bistami's fana was monistic and influenced by India, which Carl Ernst contests (hints, not checked). The word needs care, because many scholars condemn readings of fana as merging with God, and Sufi theorists themselves distinguish fana from union (ittihad) or indwelling (hulul). So it is closer to the end of the self's claim than to the end of the creature. A saying often tied to it, 'die before you die', is not accepted as a hadith (Ibn Hajar via al-Sakhawi, research-round note), though Sufis use it. For the axiom, fana fits the first part of the third clause (the separation ends), and baqa shows how the tradition holds on to the creature after that.

If someone pushes back:
- Fana is union with God. Many Sufis and most scholars do not say that. It is the end of the self's claim; the Sufi path keeps servanthood through baqa.
- Some Muslims reject fana. Yes, in forms that sound like merging; Sufi authors also guard against that.

## Mulla Sadra (Sadr al-Din Shirazi) (about 1572 to 1640)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: A Persian philosopher of the Isfahan school and a Twelver Shi'i thinker, who combined Avicenna, Suhrawardi, Ibn Arabi and theology in one system. His major work is al-Hikma al-muta'aliya fi l-asfar al-'aqliyya al-arba'a (The Transcendent Wisdom of the Four Journeys).

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'The Persian philosopher Mulla Sadra taught that the soul is bodily in its origin and spiritual in its later life and keeps developing after death, while rejecting rebirth into this world.' Table, Islam column, row 'What continues'.

Where it sits: Sajjad H. Rizvi, 'Mulla Sadra', Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (first published 2009, revised 2019); the Asfar, sections on the soul and on the rejection of transmigration

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The Arabic formula quoted in a source the checklist saw is jismaniyyat al-huduth wa ruhaniyyat al-baqa', 'corporeal in origination, spiritual in subsistence'. A Kheradnameh Sadra paper warns it should not be read as a claim about the origin of the soul's essence.

Read it free:
- Rizvi, 'Mulla Sadra' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mulla-sadra/ (seen in a search result)
- Sadra's five arguments against reincarnation (Kanz Philosophia, via DOAJ): https://doaj.org/article/797aeb9e330a4f89ae66b0393f7edd8e (seen in a search result)
- Mulla Sadra and Descartes on the soul (iranianstudies.org): https://iranianstudies.org/?p=253 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Sajjad H. Rizvi, Mulla Sadra and Metaphysics: Modulation of Being (Routledge, 2009, from memory); Fazlul Rahman, The Philosophy of Mulla Sadra (SUNY Press, 1975), which the checklist names for substantial motion.

Background: Sadra's two best-known principles are substantial motion (al-haraka al-jawhariyya) and the gradation of being (tashkik al-wujud). Substantial motion says substances, and not only qualities, are in constant change, so the soul is a process of becoming. It starts from the body and can rise to intellect, and its development continues after death. The Kanz Philosophia article the checklist saw gives his five arguments against transmigration, the heart of which is that each soul comes into being with its own body and cannot be moved into another. The research round adds that he allowed a kind of 'spiritual transmigration', the embodiment of deeds in the afterlife realm, and that interpreters disagree about whether his later view is a graded unity of being or something closer to personal individuality. So the essay's summary is right: growth continues, rebirth into this world is rejected. For the axiom he shows the third clause in a Muslim philosopher's terms, a return and a rise, minus the return to this life. Mulla Sadra's formula 'corporeal in origination, spiritual in subsistence' should not be read as a claim about where the soul comes from, and the 'bodily in its origin' wording in the essay is a short form of it. Read Rizvi's SEP article first.

If someone pushes back:
- Sadra supports lifetimes. He does not; he rejects transmigration and keeps the growth, as the essay says.
- Is he mainstream Islam? He is a major Shi'i philosopher, with debated standing among Sunni and traditional scholars; the essay presents him as a philosopher.

## Tanasukh (transmigration) in Islamic thought

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Arabic term for the passing of a soul from one body to another, usually translated transmigration or metempsychosis. Mainstream Sunni and Twelver Shi'i theology and the philosophers reject it. Some groups at the edges of Islam, or counted outside it, accepted it.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'Muslim theologians who discussed tanasukh mostly rejected it, though some minority groups accepted it, and the Quran (23:99 to 100) has the dying ask to be sent back and be refused.' The Druze are named in the paragraph of traditions that teach rebirth.

Where it sits: Kamada Shigeru's study of Shaykh al-Mufid on tanasukh, Orient 44 (2009); Ibn Sina and Mulla Sadra's arguments; Hitti on the Druze; Wikipedia 'Ghulat'

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase only. The research round found tanasukh generally denied in Sunni Islam and in Twelver Shi'ism, and accepted by the Druze (called taqammus), by Nusayri (Alawite) belief and by early extremist groups, with the claim that some Sufis accepted it resting on one Theosophical site only.

Read it free:
- Kamada, study of Shaykh al-Mufid on tanasukh, Orient 44 (2009): https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/orient/44/0/44_105/_article (seen in a search result)
- Ghulat (Wikipedia): https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulat (seen in a search result)
- Hitti, Origins of the Druze People and Religion, chapter 10 (sacred-texts.com): https://sacred-texts.com/asia/odpr/odpr10.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sadra's five arguments against reincarnation (Kanz Philosophia, via DOAJ): https://doaj.org/article/797aeb9e330a4f89ae66b0393f7edd8e (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Philip Hitti, Origins of the Druze People and Religion (Columbia University Press, 1928, public domain); Sajjad Rizvi, Mulla Sadra and Metaphysics (Routledge, 2009, from memory) for Sadra's arguments; Brill's Encyclopaedia of Islam, THREE, entry 'Tanasukh' (from memory, check).

Background: The mainstream argument is that there is one earthly life, a waiting state (barzakh), a bodily resurrection and a judgment, and the Quran refuses a return to this world (23:99 to 100). The philosophers add their own reasons. Ibn Sina (died 1037) held that the soul comes into being with its body and refuted transmigration, and Mulla Sadra gave five arguments against it. The Twelver theologian Shaykh al-Mufid (died 1022) denied it too, according to Kamada. The groups that accepted it include some early extremist Shi'i groups (the ghulat), the Nusayri (Alawite), who hold that humans and sometimes animals are reborn, and the Druze, who believe that at death the soul passes at once into a new human body (taqammus) and who reject rebirth in animals. The Druze emerged from Ismaili Islam in the eleventh century and are usually counted as a separate religious community. The mineral, plant, animal lines of Rumi are often cited for rebirth, and the research round reads them as an ascent through kinds of being. The essay's wording, 'mostly rejected it, though some minority groups accepted it', is fair. Alex can say plainly that Islam in its main forms teaches a single life, and that this is one of the places where the traditions are not unanimous.

If someone pushes back:
- Some Sufis believed in reincarnation. The research round found that claim only on a Theosophical site and does not rely on it.
- Does the Quran rule it out? Verse 23:99 to 100 is read as refusing a return to this world, though no source found ties it to tanasukh by name.

## Tawba (turning back, repentance)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Arabic word for repentance, from the root t-w-b, whose basic sense is to return. In the Quran it is used both for people turning back to God and for God turning toward them; one of God's names is al-Tawwab, the One Who Accepts Repentance.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers', last paragraph: 'the Hebrew teshuvah means return, the Arabic tawba means turning back, and the Greek metanoia in the New Testament means a change of mind. Return, turn, change of mind. I'd call that the door, described from the inside.'

Where it sits: Arabic root t-w-b; Quran 66:8 (tawba nasuha, sincere repentance); Atif Khalil, Repentance and the Return to God: Tawba in Early Sufism (SUNY, 2018, from memory)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase from a search summary of Arabic lexicographers: the root carries the sense of return (ruju') and reversion. A scholarly summary distinguishes taba ila, a person turning toward God, from taba 'ala, God turning toward a person. Another study describes it as an act, a turning, more than a feeling.

- Quran 66:8 with Maarif-ul-Quran on tawba nasuha (quran.com): https://quran.com/66:8/tafsirs/en-tafsir-maarif-ul-quran (seen in a search result)

- Publisher page, Khalil, Repentance and the Return to God (SUNY Press): https://www.sunypress.edu/Books/R/Repentance-and-the-Return-to-God2 (seen in a search result)

Background: The search summary of Arabic dictionaries says they agree that the idea under tawba and its verb taba is return. The preposition changes the meaning: a person turns to God with ila, and God turns toward the person with 'ala, so the same root can mean repentance in the human and forgiveness in God. Khalil argues that 'repentance' loses part of the sense, the larger idea of returning to God. In the Quran, 66:8 asks believers to turn to God in sincere repentance (tawba nasuha). The Maarif-ul-Quran commentary gives two readings of nasuha, pure and sincere, or the repairing of a torn garment, and reports Hasan al-Basri's condition (regret and a firm resolve not to repeat) and Ali's list, which includes restoring what was taken and seeking pardon from the person wronged. That matters for the essay: the interpersonal step Alex describes, saying sorry first, is part of classical accounts. In many Sufi manuals tawba is among the first stations of the path (check Qushayri's Risala). The comparison with teshuvah and metanoia is fair at the level of the words, with one difference in emphasis: the Arabic stresses turning and the Greek stresses a change of mind.

If someone pushes back:
- Islamic repentance is directed to God, not to the person wronged. The classical conditions include restoring rights to people, so both are there.
- Is 'turning back' the whole meaning? The dictionary sense is return; Khalil adds the broader idea of returning to God.
