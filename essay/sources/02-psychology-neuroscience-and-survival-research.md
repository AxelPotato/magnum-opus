## Botvinick and Cohen 1998 (the rubber hand illusion) (1998)

Kind: study. Status: partly checked.

Who: Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen, two cognitive neuroscientists, published a one-page letter in Nature in February 1998 describing what is now called the rubber hand illusion. It is the founding experiment of a large literature on how the brain decides which parts of the world belong to the body.

In the essay: Part two, section 'Why separation feels so real', paragraph beginning 'It also turns out that the wall is built from soft material'. It is the first of three pieces of evidence that the felt border of the self is built from sensory signals and can be shifted. The essay then limits the claim to felt ownership of a hand and adds the suggestibility caveat.

Where it sits: Nature 391(6669):756, 19 February 1998 (one page; one source gives 755-756); doi 10.1038/35784; PubMed ID 9486643 as given in a search summary.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Ten participants sat with the left arm hidden behind a screen and a rubber left hand in view. The experimenter stroked the rubber hand and the hidden hand with brushes. After synchronous stroking, participants tended to agree with statements about feeling the touch where the rubber hand was touched and about the rubber hand seeming to be their own. Asynchronous stroking was the control. The method details come from a search summary in the earlier check, not from the letter, which I could not open.

Read it free:
- IDEAS/RePEc record with the abstract (the letter itself is not free there): https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v391y1998i6669d10.1038_35784.html (seen in a search result)
- ScienceDaily news story from February 1998 on the finding: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/1998/02/980225074435.htm (seen in a search result)
- Lancaster University psychology glossary, plain explanation of the illusion: https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fas/psych/glossary/rubber_hand_illusion/ (seen in a search result)
- JoVE video protocol, 'The Rubber Hand Illusion' (shows exactly how it is run): https://app.jove.com/v/10291 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No free full text of the letter was found. Read it through a library or interlibrary loan (doi 10.1038/35784). For a readable account: Anil Seth, Being You (Faber, 2021; Dutton, 2021) uses the rubber hand to argue that the body is a best guess the brain makes; Thomas Metzinger, The Ego Tunnel (Basic Books, 2009) covers it from the philosophy side; Sandra and Matthew Blakeslee, The Body Has a Mind of Its Own (Random House, 2007). These three are from memory, so check edition details.

Background: The letter is one page and the method is simple, which is why it spread. Botvinick and Cohen asked how the brain combines vision, touch and the felt position of a limb. Their answer was that when what you see being touched and what you feel being touched agree in timing, the brain can adopt a nearby object as part of the body. Their ten participants had the left arm hidden, watched a rubber hand being stroked while the hidden hand was stroked at the same moment (for about ten minutes, per a check-session summary), and then answered a short questionnaire. Only after synchronous stroking did they endorse the statements about touch felt in the rubber hand. Later labs added a pointing test (where do you say your hidden hand is) and threat responses when the rubber hand is attacked; the second is from memory. Three limits matter. The first sample was tiny and the main data are self reports. Several later studies found that roughly 16 to 28 percent of people do not respond. And the illusion concerns a hand. It does not reach the wider sense of being someone, which the essay itself says. The usual explanation is multisensory integration, a weighing of the senses by how reliable they are. Peter Lush and colleagues argue that suggestion explains more than that account allows, and Ehrsson's group disputes that (see the Lush entry). For the axiom, nothing here shows oneness. It shows that a boundary people take as given is computed, and a materialist and an idealist can both accept that.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't it just suggestion? Some of it may be. Lush and colleagues found that people who respond strongly to imaginative suggestion report stronger illusions, and Ehrsson's group says that link disappears under standard controls. The essay already calls the link disputed, and its point survives either way: the felt edge of a body part can be moved.
- A rubber hand is a long way from the self. Agreed, and the essay says so. The claim is small: ownership of a hand is worked out from the timing of sight and touch, so the edge of the felt body is made of editable material.

## Lenggenhager, Tadi, Metzinger and Blanke 2007 (Video ergo sum) (2007)

Kind: study. Status: partly checked.

Who: Bigna Lenggenhager, Tej Tadi, Thomas Metzinger and Olaf Blanke, working with Blanke's lab at EPFL in Lausanne. Metzinger is a philosopher of mind and Blanke a neurologist who had studied out-of-body experiences in patients. The paper took the rubber hand idea from a hand to the whole body.

In the essay: Same paragraph of 'Why separation feels so real': 'A video version with a person's own back appeared in 2007 (Lenggenhager and colleagues).' It supports the sentence that the border of the self is made of editable material.

Where it sits: Science 317(5841):1096-1099, 2007; doi 10.1126/science.1143439.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Volunteers wore a head-mounted display that showed live video of their own back filmed from behind, about two metres away. Their real back was stroked while they saw the stroking on the virtual body in front of them, in time or out of time. With synchronous stroking they reported that the virtual body felt like theirs and that they felt located toward it, and when blindfolded, moved and asked to walk back to where they had stood, they tended to stop nearer the virtual body's position. The abstract seen in this session confirms the main result (identification with a virtual body and self-location outside the physical body); the walking test and the two-metre detail come from the earlier check and from later papers using the same paradigm.

Read it free:
- EPFL Infoscience record, with an open-access PDF of the paper (about 177 KB): https://infoscience.epfl.ch/handle/20.500.14299/57536 (seen in a search result)
- EPFL Infoscience article details page: https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/b7f81eda-0f7c-491e-ac50-10f102c6e62b/articledetails (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Thomas Metzinger, The Ego Tunnel (Basic Books, 2009) has a plain-language chapter on out-of-body experiences and body illusions (chapter number from memory, check). The paper is short; read it first.

Background: The paper started from clinical reports. People with certain lesions or seizures, and some healthy people, report out-of-body experiences in which the self seems to sit outside the body. Blanke's lab had linked such experiences to disturbed integration of body signals in the temporo-parietal region (from memory). Lenggenhager and colleagues tried to create a mild version in healthy volunteers by giving them conflicting video and touch. In the same issue of Science, Henrik Ehrsson published a companion study with a different set-up (from memory). The reported result is that felt ownership and felt location shift toward the virtual body, with a modest drift in where people think they are. Limits worth knowing: the samples were small (a secondary source gives 14 for the main study, not confirmed), the measures were a questionnaire plus a drift test, and the earlier check found no preregistered direct replication. Whether ownership and drift measure the same thing is itself argued; a study seen in this session cautions that drift alone cannot measure ownership. The suggestibility worry that applies to the rubber hand plausibly applies here, but I saw no test of it for this paradigm. For the axiom, the paper shows that where you feel you are can shift by some distance in a few minutes. It does not show a self leaving a body, and Metzinger, a co-author, does not read it that way.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't this prove the self is an illusion? No. It shows that felt ownership and felt location can be moved. The essay says exactly this and no more.
- The sample was tiny. True, and no preregistered replication turned up in the check. Treat it as a suggestive demonstration, and lean on the rubber hand and later full-body work for the pattern.

## Kalckert and Ehrsson 2017 (onset time of the moving rubber hand illusion) (2017)

Kind: study. Status: checked.

Who: Andreas Kalckert and H. Henrik Ehrsson. Ehrsson, at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm, leads one of the main rubber hand labs. The moving-hand version, in which the participant's own finger movement drives the rubber hand, was introduced by Kalckert and Ehrsson around 2012 (from memory).

In the essay: Same paragraph of 'Why separation feels so real': 'a later study of the moving-hand version timed it at about half a minute on average.' It backs the word 'soon' in 'Most people soon felt the touch in the rubber hand.'

Where it sits: Frontiers in Psychology 8:344, 10 March 2017; doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00344; open access.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. In a study of 117 people using the moving-hand version, the mean time before the sense of ownership began was about 23 seconds (22.8 seconds with active movement, 23.2 with passive movement), and the 90th percentile was about 50 seconds. These figures are from the Frontiers text as recorded in the earlier check.

Read it free:
- Frontiers in Psychology, full text (open access): https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00344/text (seen in a search result)
- PubMed Central copy: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5345084 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No book needed. The paper is open access and short.

Background: Most rubber hand studies ask whether the illusion happens. This one asks how fast. In the moving-hand version the participant moves a finger that is linked to a rubber finger, either on their own (active) or with the experimenter moving it (passive), and signals when the rubber hand starts to feel like their own. Across 117 people the mean onset was about 23 seconds in both conditions, and nine in ten got there within about 50 seconds. The earlier check also noted an older study from Ehrsson's lab (Ehrsson, Spence and Passingham, Science 305:875, 2004) with about 11 seconds for the stroking version, from one weak source. Two points for Alex. First, the essay's 'about half a minute on average' rounds 23 seconds up. 'Under half a minute on average' is exact, and 'within about a minute for nearly everyone who felt it' fits the 90th percentile. Second, onset can only be timed for people who felt the illusion, so non-responders (16 to 28 percent in several studies, per an earlier check summary) sit outside the average, and the timing is a self-reported moment, so it inherits the suggestion debate. The number's job in the essay is small: it shows the effect is quick. A quick effect looks like a basic perceptual mechanism and less like a long hypnotic build-up.

If someone pushes back:
- Half a minute is not 23 seconds. Correct, and worth fixing in the text; the safe phrasing is 'under half a minute on average'.
- Does everyone get it? No. Roughly one in five to one in four people do not, in several studies. The essay says 'most people', which is right.

## Lush et al. 2020 (trait phenomenological control and the rubber hand illusion), and the dispute (2020; reply and counter-reply 2022)

Kind: study. Status: checked.

Who: Peter Lush at the University of Sussex (Sackler Centre for Consciousness Science) with Vanessa Botan, Ryan Scott, Anil Seth, Jamie Ward and Zoltan Dienes. Seth is a leading consciousness scientist and Dienes a Sussex psychologist known for Bayesian statistics. The opposing side is Henrik Ehrsson's group (with Fotopoulou, Radziun, Longo and Tsakiris).

In the essay: Same paragraph of 'Why separation feels so real': 'some studies find that how strongly people report the illusion goes with how suggestible they are, a link other researchers dispute.' The essay says its own conclusion does not depend on this.

Where it sits: Lush, Botan, Scott, Seth, Ward and Dienes, Nature Communications 11, article 4853, 25 September 2020; doi 10.1038/s41467-020-18591-6. Dispute: Ehrsson et al., Nature Communications 13, article 564 (28 January 2022), doi 10.1038/s41467-022-28177-z; Lush and Seth reply, same journal, doi 10.1038/s41467-022-28178-y.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. In three samples (156, 404 and 353 people), trait phenomenological control, the tendency to produce the experience a suggestion implies and closely tied to hypnotic suggestibility, predicted how strongly people reported the rubber hand illusion and mirror-touch synaesthesia. The authors suggested that part of the reported illusion may reflect suggestion carried by the procedure. Ehrsson and colleagues reanalysed the data and found no significant relationship when the illusion was scored against a control condition in line with standard practice and the preregistered hypothesis.

Read it free:
- Lush et al. 2020, full text (PubMed Central, open access): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7519080 (seen in a search result)
- Ehrsson et al. 2022, 'No specific relationship between hypnotic suggestibility and the rubber hand illusion' (PubMed Central): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8799653/ (seen in a search result)
- Lush and Seth 2022, 'Reply to: No specific relationship...' (PubMed Central): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8799699/ (seen in a search result)
- Slater and Ehrsson 2022, 'Multisensory integration dominates hypnotisability and expectations in the rubber hand illusion' (PubMed): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35782045/ (seen in a search result)
- Neurobanter blog post 'What's up with the rubber hand illusion?' (informal explainer, not peer reviewed): https://neurobanter.com/2020/11/09/whats-up-with-the-rubber-hand-illusion/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No book. Read the 2020 paper first, then the 2022 letter and reply, which are short and show both sides.

Background: Lush is a psychologist who argues that many experiences in psychology and neuroscience experiments are shaped by what participants take the experimenter to expect, a point called demand characteristics. His 2020 paper measured a trait, phenomenological control (how readily a person generates the experience a suggestion describes), and found it predicted rubber hand reports in three samples. An exploratory analysis noted by the Sussex group found it also correlated with proprioceptive drift. The earlier check also found a separate Lush paper in Collabra: Psychology (2020, n = 353) in which people who only read about the procedure and imagined being participants gave the expected pattern of answers; I did not open that one in this session. Ehrsson's group answered that the link vanishes when the illusion is scored the standard way, against a control condition, and the Sussex group replied that the reanalysis changes the preregistered measure. A further paper by Slater and Ehrsson (2022) used the 353-person data and concluded that multisensory integration dominates over hypnotisability and expectation. So the fight is mainly about how to score the illusion and how much of the report is suggestion. Nobody in it says the experience is fake. For the essay, the point is modest: a top-down account and a bottom-up account both treat the felt border as built.

If someone pushes back:
- So the illusion is fake? Nobody in this exchange says that. The dispute is about how much comes from sensory timing and how much from expectation.
- Why mention it at all? Because it is a live challenge to the strongest-sounding version of the point, and the essay stays honest by naming it and by resting its claim only on the smaller conclusion.

## Blanke and Metzinger 2009 (Full-body illusions and minimal phenomenal selfhood) (2009)

Kind: study. Status: partly checked.

Who: Olaf Blanke, a neurologist at EPFL in Lausanne who studies out-of-body experiences, and Thomas Metzinger, a philosopher of mind. Their paper is a review, not a new experiment.

In the essay: Same paragraph of 'Why separation feels so real': 'in 2009 he and Olaf Blanke tied that idea to full-body illusions like this one.' It links Metzinger's theory of the self-model to the experiments.

Where it sits: Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13(1):7-13, 2009; doi 10.1016/j.tics.2008.10.003.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The abstract asks what the minimally sufficient conditions are for a phenomenal self, argues that earlier research on the body neglected the global, whole-body character of self-consciousness, and proposes that full-body illusion findings give a new entry point for studying it. From memory, and to be confirmed in the PDF, the paper names three ingredients that these experiments can pull apart: self-identification with a body, self-location, and the first-person perspective.

Read it free:
- EPFL Infoscience record, open-access PDF: https://infoscience.epfl.ch/handle/20.500.14299/57475 (seen in a search result)
- EPFL Infoscience article details: https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/32d4c4e1-04df-41c1-95ab-b698cf7b73d4/articledetails (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: The paper is seven pages. For the wider theory, see Metzinger, The Ego Tunnel (Basic Books, 2009).

Background: This is a review article. Blanke's lab had studied out-of-body experiences in patients (from memory, with a focus on the temporo-parietal region), and Metzinger was building a theory of the self from the philosophy side. The abstract seen in this session asks what the minimally sufficient conditions are for a phenomenal self, meaning the bare experience of being someone, and proposes full-body illusions as the way in. The claim is a research programme, with the experiments as the entry point. The paper does not say it has found the self or shown it to be an illusion. Critics of the wider programme include philosophers who hold that a minimal, pre-reflective sense of self cannot be reduced to a model; Dan Zahavi is the usual name, from memory. Where it meets the axiom: the paper treats the self as assembled from separable parts, which sits well with the essay's picture of a wall maintained by tension and built from soft material. It is just as comfortable inside the first worldview, because Metzinger is a naturalist, and nothing in it supports the second worldview as such. If someone asks whether neuroscience backs oneness, the honest answer is no. It backs one step: the felt self is put together from signals and can be taken apart in the lab.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't this the Buddhist no-self, confirmed? It is a parallel the essay does not draw. Metzinger himself has written about the link with meditation, but this paper is a neuroscience and philosophy review about experimental conditions for a minimal self.
- Does it support the axiom? No. It is compatible with both worldviews, and the essay uses it only to show that the border is built.

## Metzinger 2003, Being No One (2003 (paperback 2004))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Thomas Metzinger, a German philosopher of mind (born 1958, from memory) who spent his career at the University of Mainz. Being No One is his main academic work, setting out the self-model theory of subjectivity.

In the essay: Same paragraph of 'Why separation feels so real': 'The philosopher Thomas Metzinger argues that the felt self is a model the brain builds of the body and then can't see as a model.' The essay does not claim the book rests on the 2007 experiments.

Where it sits: Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity, MIT Press, Cambridge MA, 2003; first MIT Press paperback 2004 (ISBN 9780262633086; hardback 0262134179); about 699 pages.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Metzinger argues that no self-things exist in the world. What exists is a process that builds a model of the organism, and the phenomenal self is the content of that model, which he calls the transparent self-model. It is transparent because the system cannot recognise it as a model, so it appears to be a direct fact about oneself. The CARTA record seen in this session confirms the core idea and that the book draws on clinical cases and neuroscience.

Read it free:
- CARTA record summarising the book's argument: https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/1480 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No legal free copy of the book was found. The MIT Press paperback (2004) is the standard edition. It is dense and long. An easier route is Metzinger, The Ego Tunnel: The Science of the Mind and the Myth of the Self (Basic Books, 2009). Metzinger's later book on meditation and pure consciousness is, from memory, The Elephant and the Blind (MIT Press, 2024); check before citing.

Background: Metzinger's thesis is that the self is the way a system represents itself to itself. The system builds a model of the organism, the model is experienced as a self, and the system cannot see through it because the model is transparent, which in his technical sense means the system does not experience it as a representation. The book draws on clinical cases (neglect, agnosia, blindsight, hallucination, out-of-body states) and on philosophy of representation. The date matters: it predates the 2007 full-body experiments, so the essay is right to connect them only through the 2009 paper with Blanke. Disputes: philosophers differ over whether transparency can do the work he asks of it, and whether a minimal first-person sense of self survives his argument; Dan Zahavi is the usual critic, from memory. Alex should know that Metzinger does not hold that all is one. He has written about meditation and states of pure consciousness in later work (check before citing), but his position is naturalist. If someone asks whether Metzinger supports the axiom, the honest answer is no. He supports one step of the essay's chain, that the felt self is built, and a materialist can accept that step without moving an inch toward the second worldview.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't Metzinger saying the self doesn't exist? He says there are no self-things, only a model. He does not deny that people exist, and he does not say the model is useless.
- Is the theory proven? No. It is a philosophical theory informed by neuroscience, and philosophers contest it.

## Cialdini, Brown, Lewis, Luce and Neuberg 1997 (When one into one equals oneness) (1997)

Kind: study. Status: checked.

Who: Robert Cialdini (best known for his books on persuasion) with Stephanie Brown, Brian Lewis, Carol Luce and Steven Neuberg, all at Arizona State University at the time (from memory). The paper is a technical intervention in a long argument about whether helping can ever be selfless.

In the essay: Used twice in Part two. In 'Karma, or the fingers' (end of the golden rule paragraph): 'In 1997 Robert Cialdini and colleagues found that how "one" people felt with a person in need predicted their willingness to help better than sympathy did, though other researchers dispute the reading.' Again in 'What you can check', second paragraph, as the nearest lab result.

Where it sits: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 73(3):481-494, September 1997; doi 10.1037/0022-3514.73.3.481. Title: 'Reinterpreting the empathy-altruism relationship: When one into one equals oneness.'

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Across three studies, the effect of empathic concern on willingness to help disappeared once perceived oneness (self-other overlap) was taken into account. Path analyses suggested empathic concern raised helping only through its tie to perceived oneness, and the authors read empathic concern mainly as a signal of oneness. Oneness was measured with a seven-point item on how far participants would use the word 'we' for the relationship, combined with the Inclusion of Other in the Self scale of Aron, Aron and Smollan (1992).

Read it free:
- Full-text PDF of the paper (copy hosted on a University of Wisconsin course page): https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~jpiliavi/965/Cialdini%201997.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Stony Brook research record with the abstract: https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/reinterpreting-the-empathy-altruism-relationship-when-one-into-on/ (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, 'Empathy-altruism' (overview of the whole debate): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empathy-altruism (seen in a search result)
- University of Michigan dissertation chapter that discusses the study's numbers (secondhand, not opened by me): https://public.websites.umich.edu/~kruger/dis2.html (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: The article itself is the thing to read. For the other side, C. Daniel Batson, Altruism in Humans (Oxford University Press, 2011; from memory). For the oneness scale, Aron, Aron and Smollan (1992), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 63(4):596-612.

Background: Daniel Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis says that feeling empathic concern for someone in need can produce a genuinely other-directed motive to help. Cialdini and colleagues proposed that the conditions which raise empathic concern also raise a sense of oneness with the person, and that oneness does the work. If so, helping under those conditions is partly directed at a self that now includes the other. In their three studies, once oneness was in the model the separate effect of empathic concern vanished. A secondhand source (a Michigan dissertation chapter, not opened) reports that in study 1 the oneness index explained about 58 percent of the variance in a helping measure and empathic concern about 20 percent. Limits: self report, scenario based, probably student samples (not confirmed), correlational and path-analytic. Above all, oneness here means felt closeness to a particular person, and it ran higher for friends and family. It does not mean the metaphysical oneness of the axiom, and Alex should not let a listener slide between them. Batson's group replied in the same issue; see that entry. The dispute is open. For the axiom the useful point is that on Cialdini's reading the line between selfish and selfless giving blurs, because the other has become part of the self.

If someone pushes back:
- That is just closeness, not cosmic oneness. Correct. The essay uses it as the nearest lab cousin of the idea, and says in the next section that nobody has tested letting go of tension.
- Hasn't it been refuted? It has been contested, not refuted. Batson's experiments, and later work by Maner and colleagues, pull in different directions. The essay says 'other researchers dispute the reading', which is accurate.

## Batson et al. 1997 and the reply to Cialdini (the empathy-altruism dispute) (1997 (Maner et al. 2002))

Kind: study. Status: partly checked.

Who: C. Daniel Batson, a social psychologist (University of Kansas, from memory), and colleagues Sager, Garst, Kang, Rubchinsky and Dawson. Their paper was published in the same issue as Cialdini's, and the Cialdini side answered in a joint comment led by Steven Neuberg.

In the essay: Not named in the current text, which says only 'other researchers dispute the reading'. The CHECKLIST (C11-65) proposes naming Batson, and the dispute is the thing Alex needs to speak to if asked who disagrees.

Where it sits: Batson, Sager, Garst, Kang, Rubchinsky and Dawson, 'Is empathy-induced helping due to self-other merging?', Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 73(3):495-509 (1997). Neuberg, Cialdini, Brown, Luce, Sagarin and Lewis, 'Does empathy lead to anything more than superficial helping? Comment on Batson et al. (1997)', same journal and issue, pp. 510-516. Maner et al. 2002, 'The effects of perspective taking on motivations for helping: Still no evidence for altruism', Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (volume and pages not checked).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Batson and colleagues ran two experiments in which empathy was manipulated by instruction (stay objective, or imagine her feelings) and the target, a young woman in need, was described as a student at the participants' own university or at a rival one. Helping rose with empathy whatever the group membership, and the effect could not be accounted for by any of the merging measures. The Neuberg comment replies that a fair test must measure the plausible nonaltruistic alternatives reliably and control for all of them, and its abstract, as summarised in a search result, ends 'At best, empathy per se leads to superficial helping.'

Read it free:
- Index record for Batson et al. 1997 (abstract only): https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/9464785 (seen in a search result)
- Stony Brook research record for the Neuberg et al. comment: https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/does-empathy-lead-to-anything-more-than-superficial-helping-comme/ (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, 'Empathy-altruism' (summarises Batson's hypothesis and the objections): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empathy-altruism (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: C. Daniel Batson, Altruism in Humans (Oxford University Press, 2011), from memory, is his fullest statement of the case. Batson also published a reply to the Neuberg comment in the same journal volume (from memory, around pp. 517-522; confirm).

Background: Batson spent decades arguing that empathic concern produces genuinely altruistic motivation, against the view that all helping is at root self-serving. Cialdini's oneness reading was a direct challenge, because if empathy works through oneness then helping is partly aimed at a self that now includes the other. Batson's reply used experiments that varied empathy by instruction and varied the target's group, and reported an empathy-helping link that no merging measure could account for. The Neuberg comment answered that a valid test has to measure the plausible nonaltruistic alternatives well and control for all of them, and that Batson's did not. A later paper, Maner and colleagues 2002 ('Still no evidence for altruism'), reported that oneness and negative affect, not empathic concern, mediated helping. As I recall, Batson's own view is that if merging fully explained the help it would still count as egoism, so showing that empathy helps without merging protects his claim; confirm that in his book. Sources seen in this session describe the wider literature as contradictory. It is a dispute about why empathy leads to helping. It never tested tension or letting go. For Alex, the interesting point is that the axiom dissolves the line the two camps argue over, since if the other is part of me, helping them is neither purely selfish nor purely selfless.

If someone pushes back:
- So did Batson win? Neither side has settled it. Sources seen in this session call the evidence contradictory, and both camps have published follow-ups.
- Why does the essay not name him? It keeps the sentence short. Adding 'Batson and colleagues replied in the same issue' is a one-clause fix if Alex wants it.

## Mikulincer, Shaver, Gillath and Nitzberg 2005 (attachment security, compassion and helping) (2005)

Kind: study. Status: partly checked.

Who: Mario Mikulincer (then at Bar-Ilan University, Israel, from memory) and Phillip Shaver (UC Davis), the leading names in adult attachment research, with Omri Gillath and Rachel Nitzberg. Their work extends John Bowlby's attachment theory to how adults treat other people.

In the essay: 'What you can check', second paragraph: 'Feeling safe raised compassion and helping in some experiments.' It is the nearest lab support for the idea that less threat frees people to attend to others.

Where it sits: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 89(5):817-839, 2005. Title: 'Attachment, caregiving, and altruism: Boosting attachment security increases compassion and helping.' (A search result showed a different volume and pages for another Mikulincer paper; the publisher-linked records agree on 89(5), 817-839.)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. A series of experiments (a search summary says five, run in Israel and the United States) raised participants' felt security by priming, both implicit (for example, names of people the participant had identified as safe-haven figures) and explicit (reflecting on a supportive relationship). Primed participants reported more compassion for, and more willingness to help, a person in distress, with lower personal distress. A summary also reports that they took on extra work to help someone needy without benefit to themselves. Dispositional attachment anxiety and avoidance pushed in the opposite direction.

Read it free:
- Hebrew University research record with the abstract: https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/attachment-caregiving-and-altruism-boosting-attachment-security-i/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No free full text found. Get the article through a library (APA PsycArticles). A readable overview by the same authors is Mikulincer and Shaver, Attachment in Adulthood: Structure, Dynamics, and Change (Guilford Press, 2007; 2nd ed. 2016), from memory. Per-study sample sizes and effect sizes were not available to me.

Background: Attachment theory says that a child who can count on a caregiver explores and helps more freely. Mikulincer and Shaver argue that the adult version works the same way: felt security, a sense that someone reliable is there, frees attention from one's own threat so that a person can notice and respond to someone else's distress. The experiments raised security by priming rather than by long-term change. Primed participants showed more compassion and willingness to help, and the effects held up against alternative explanations the authors tested. People high in attachment anxiety or avoidance showed the reverse pattern, with more personal distress and less compassion. Limits Alex should know: priming methods have had a harder time in replication since about 2012 (a general point, from memory), most of the work in this area comes from one research network, and what is manipulated is felt security with a person. That is close to Alex's tension model without being the same thing. The essay is right to say 'in some experiments'. As evidence for a mechanism it is the best fit among the lab results: less threat leaves attention free for someone else. It does not test letting go, and it says nothing about oneness.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't priming discredited? Some social priming effects failed to replicate. This paper reports several experiments with two kinds of priming, and the essay says only 'in some experiments'. Treat it as supporting a mechanism, and do not rest weight on a single result.
- Does it show that letting go makes people kinder? No. It shows that felt safety with a person did, in these experiments. The essay marks the gap by saying nobody has tested letting go of tension as such.

## Hafenbrack, LaPalme and Solal 2022 (mindfulness reduces guilt and reparation) (2022)

Kind: study. Status: checked.

Who: Andrew Hafenbrack (an organizational behaviour researcher at the University of Washington Foster School, from the press coverage), Matthew LaPalme and Isabelle Solal (listed at ESSEC). Hafenbrack studies mindfulness in work settings.

In the essay: 'What you can check', second paragraph: 'a short breathing practice lowered the urge to make amends in others.' It is the counterweight to the hope that calm makes people more generous, and it is why the marker reads 'say sorry first, even when it costs you.'

Where it sits: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 123(1):28-54, 2022; doi 10.1037/pspa0000298. Title: 'Mindfulness meditation reduces guilt and prosocial reparation.'

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Across eight experiments with more than 1,400 participants in the United States and Portugal, a short focused-breathing meditation reduced guilt about a remembered or imagined wrong and reduced the urge to make amends. In the first study meditators wrote less sincere apologies, and the reduced guilt fully accounted for the drop in reparation. The check session recorded about 17 percent less given to the wronged person in a money task. In the final experiment a loving-kindness meditation, compared with breath-focused practice, led to more willingness to make amends and less self-focus.

Read it free:
- ESSEC faculty research record with the abstract: https://faculty.essec.edu/research/mindfulness-meditation-reduces-guilt-and-prosocial-reparation (seen in a search result)
- ScienceDaily summary of the study: https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/03/220304101008.htm (seen in a search result)
- University of Washington Foster School news item, 'Got Guilt?': https://foster.uw.edu/news/got-guilt/ (seen in a search result)
- PsyPost write-up with study details: https://www.psypost.org/mindfulness-meditation-reduces-prosocial-reparative-behaviors-by-buffering-people-against-feelings-of-guilt (seen in a search result)
- DOI link to the article (full text is behind the publisher): https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000298 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: No free full text found; ask the authors or a library. For balance, the meta-analysis Kreplin, Farias and Brazil, 'The limited prosocial effects of meditation', Scientific Reports 8:2403 (2018), doi 10.1038/s41598-018-20299-z, is open access (seen in an earlier check, not re-searched here).

Background: Hafenbrack's question is whether mindfulness changes how strongly guilt pushes people to make amends. In the first study people recalled something they felt guilty about (or described their previous day), then heard either a breath-focused meditation or an instruction to let the mind wander. Meditators felt less guilty and wrote less sincere apologies, and the lower guilt fully accounted for the drop in reparation. The effect repeated across eight experiments and more than 1,400 participants. In the last experiment a loving-kindness meditation, compared with breath-focused practice, produced more willingness to make amends and less self-focus. Hafenbrack's reading is that breath-focused practice turns attention to one's own sensations and calms by clearing negative feeling, while loving-kindness turns attention toward other people. He says the findings do not condemn meditation. Limits: brief online inductions, small stakes, no long-term practitioners, no independent replication seen. Related work, from memory or from the earlier check: Poulin and colleagues 2021 found brief mindfulness lowered helping among people with independent self-views, and the Kreplin 2018 meta-analysis found modest prosocial effects that shrank with active controls. For Alex: calm alone does not make people repair things. That is close to his own point that release is not relaxation, and the loving-kindness result fits his claim that the direction of attention matters.

If someone pushes back:
- Does this mean meditation makes people callous? No. It is a short breathing exercise in the lab, and the final experiment found that loving-kindness raised the willingness to make amends.
- Doesn't it undercut 'say sorry first'? The marker is behaviour, not feeling. It asks how fast you apologise 'even when it costs you', which is the thing that guilt-dulling calm would lower.

## Ian Stevenson (1918-2007)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: A Canadian-born American psychiatrist who chaired the psychiatry department at the University of Virginia, then in 1967 left the chair to run a small research unit that became the Division of Perceptual Studies, funded in part by a bequest from Chester Carlson, who invented the Xerox process. He spent four decades collecting and investigating cases of young children who said they remembered earlier lives.

In the essay: 'Not enough time in one life', the paragraph beginning 'I should say plainly that mainstream science has no accepted evidence for survival after death.' The essay names Stevenson, says he collected about 2,500 cases with colleagues, lists the standard criticisms, and says it does not lean on the research.

Where it sits: Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation, Proceedings of the American Society for Psychical Research vol. 26 (1966); University Press of Virginia 2nd ed. 1974. Cases of the Reincarnation Type, 4 vols, University Press of Virginia: 1975 (India), 1977 (Sri Lanka), 1980 (Lebanon and Turkey), 1983 (Thailand and Burma). Reincarnation and Biology: A Contribution to the Etiology of Birthmarks and Birth Defects, 2 vols, 1997 (publisher Praeger, from memory). Children Who Remember Previous Lives (1987; revised ed. 2001).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Stevenson published Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation in 1966 and, with his colleagues at Virginia, built a collection of about 2,500 cases of children who said they remembered earlier lives. Critics point to cultural expectation, parental suggestion, selective reporting and testimony gathered after the fact, and note that most cases come from cultures that already believe in rebirth. The 2,500 figure is the Virginia group's total by about 2007 (UVA and a Tucker review, as seen in the earlier check and this session), not Stevenson's alone.

Read it free:
- Wikipedia, 'Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation' (summary of the book and its reception): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty_Cases_Suggestive_of_Reincarnation (seen in a search result)
- Psi Encyclopedia (Society for Psychical Research), 'Ian Stevenson' (sympathetic biography): https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/ian-stevenson/ (seen in a search result)
- Psi Encyclopedia, 'Criticisms of Reincarnation Case Studies' (lists the main objections, from a sympathetic site): https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/criticisms-reincarnation-case-studies/ (seen in a search result)
- University of Virginia page for 'Children Who Remember Previous Lives: A Question of Reincarnation': https://med.virginia.edu/perceptual-studies/publications/study-of-reincarnation/children-who-remember-previous-lives-a-question-of-reincarnation/ (seen in a search result)
- Steven Novella, NeuroLogica Blog, 2014 (a sceptical account that discusses Stevenson's work): https://theness.com/neurologicablog/?p=6561 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Best starting point: Stevenson, Children Who Remember Previous Lives: A Question of Reincarnation (revised edition, McFarland, 2001). The 1966 Twenty Cases is the classic first report; the University Press of Virginia 1974 edition adds follow-ups. For a later, easier book, see the Jim Tucker entry. The sceptical counterpart is Paul Edwards (see that entry). Internet Archive may lend some of these; search the title there.

Background: Method: Stevenson travelled to villages, mostly in India, Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand, Lebanon and Turkey, interviewed the child, the family and the relatives of the named earlier person, and compared the child's statements, behaviour, fears and birthmarks with the record. He called the cases 'suggestive of' and 'of the reincarnation type', and he did not claim proof; he said reincarnation seemed to him the best fit for the facts. His late work looked at birthmarks said to match wounds on the previous person. A search summary reports that his own summary covered 895 cases, 309 with birthmarks or defects, only 49 of them with medical records, and that he judged the match confirmed in 43 of those 49. The main criticisms: statements recorded after the families had already identified the previous person, cultural expectation, parental and village influence, interpreters, selective reporting, and the fact that most cases come from places where rebirth is already believed. As I recall, defenders answer that a minority of cases were written down before verification. Mainstream science has not accepted the work. Some critics call it pseudoscience, others say it was done with care. For the axiom: even if the cases were as strong as Stevenson hoped, they would be evidence for an unexplained link between a child's memories and a dead person, and would say nothing about one whole experiencing separation. The essay is right to lean on none of it.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't it pseudoscience? Mainstream scientists have rejected reincarnation as the explanation, and some critics use that word. Others say the work was careful. The essay takes no side. It says there is a literature, that it is disputed, and that the argument does not rest on it.
- Why mention it at all? Because a reader will know it exists, and leaving it out would look like hiding it. One honest paragraph, then move on.

## Jim Tucker (living)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: A child psychiatrist at the University of Virginia (listed as Bonner-Lowry Professor Emeritus of Psychiatry and Neurobehavioral Sciences) who took over the collection of children's past-life cases from Ian Stevenson. A 2008 interview summarised in this session says he began as a sceptic, raised Southern Baptist.

In the essay: 'Not enough time in one life', same paragraph as Stevenson: 'Jim Tucker continued with Life Before Life in 2005.' The essay does not rely on his findings.

Where it sits: Life Before Life: A Scientific Investigation of Children's Memories of Previous Lives, St. Martin's Press, New York, 2005 (ISBN 0-312-32137-6), foreword by Ian Stevenson. Return to Life: Extraordinary Cases of Children Who Remember Past Lives, St. Martin's Press, 2013, mostly American cases.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Tucker continued Stevenson's work and wrote Life Before Life (2005), a general-reader account of the research with new cases. The Virginia group had studied more than 2,500 cases by about 2007. Tucker himself says faulty informant memory is the best normal explanation for many cases (as summarised in the earlier check).

Read it free:
- Wikipedia, 'Life Before Life' (book summary, publication data): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_Before_Life (seen in a search result)
- University of Virginia Division of Perceptual Studies event page for a Tucker talk on children's memories: https://med.virginia.edu/perceptual-studies/event/dr-jim-tucker-childrens-memories-of-previous-lives/ (seen in a search result)
- Psi Encyclopedia biography of Jim Tucker (PDF; sympathetic source): https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/sites/default/files/ebook/article/tucker_jim_b-728.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Publisher page for Life Before Life (Macmillan): https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780312376741/lifebeforelife (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Start with Tucker, Life Before Life (St. Martin's Press, 2005; paperback ISBN 9780312376741). It is the easiest entry to the field. Return to Life (2013) covers American cases. A later book, Before: Children's Memories of Previous Lives (2021), exists from memory; check details. Read Edwards (below) for the other side.

Background: Tucker is the main living figure in the Virginia work. Life Before Life gives the history of Stevenson's research, a tour of the typical case (a child of two or three begins to talk about another life, often one that ended violently, with fears and behaviours that match), and Tucker's own new cases. Return to Life moved to American families, where rebirth is not widely believed. Tucker's argument, as I recall, is that US cases lack the cultural expectation that critics point to in Asia; critics reply that the American parents involved are often open to the idea and that the cases rarely contain details that can be tested against a record. The best known American case is James Leininger, a boy who recalled being a Second World War pilot (the case is described in publisher material for Return to Life; the name is from memory). Reviews seen in this session praise the care of his method and doubt the leap from anecdote to a claim about reincarnation. Tucker addresses alternative explanations such as informant memory, normal learning and fraud, and the book does not claim proof. The final chapters speculate about physics and consciousness, from memory. For the essay: Tucker is cited as the person who carried the work on. Nothing in Part two depends on him.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't Tucker have strong cases? He and his supporters think some are strong. Mainstream science has not accepted the research, and the essay says so before it mentions him.
- Isn't he biased? He started as a sceptic by his own account, but he works inside the group that Stevenson founded. The honest position is that the research is disputed and the essay does not lean on it.

## Paul Edwards (the critic of the reincarnation research) (1923-2004)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: An Austrian-born American moral philosopher and a convinced atheist, editor in chief of Macmillan's eight-volume Encyclopedia of Philosophy (1967). In 1996 he published Reincarnation: A Critical Examination, the fullest philosophical attack on belief in reincarnation and on Stevenson's evidence.

In the essay: Sources list only, under 'Not enough time in one life': Edwards, P. (1996), Reincarnation: A Critical Examination, Prometheus Books. He is not named in the running text. His book is the reference behind the sentence listing the usual criticisms of the child cases.

Where it sits: Reincarnation: A Critical Examination, Prometheus Books, Amherst, New York, 1996, 313 pages. The chapters on Stevenson's cases are in the evidence part of the book (chapter numbers not confirmed).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Edwards argues that reincarnation is conceptually incoherent as well as unsupported, citing notions like the astral body and the soul's invasion of the womb, that the moral argument and the Law of Karma are indefensible, and that Stevenson's cases do not show what Stevenson says they show. Objections on the case side include reliance on interpreters. The CHECKLIST lists his book as the source for 'selective reporting' and parental influence; wording there is unchecked.

- Encyclopedia.com entry, 'Edwards, Paul 1923-2004': https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/edwards-paul-1923-2004 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Edwards, Reincarnation: A Critical Examination (Prometheus Books, 1996) is out of print in places, so look for used copies or a library. Pair it with Robert Almeder's reply and with Stevenson's own overview (Children Who Remember Previous Lives). Edwards also edited Immortality (Macmillan, 1992), from memory.

Background: Edwards is the heavyweight sceptic on this topic. A reviewer reports that his Introduction states his aim as showing the claimed evidence is 'worthless'; that is a secondary quotation, so confirm it in the book. His case has three strands, as described in reviews seen in this session. The conceptual strand says reincarnation is incoherent, citing the astral body and the 'womb-invasion' of the mother by the soul. The moral strand says reincarnation is offered as an answer to the problem of evil and that the Law of Karma cannot bear that weight. The evidential strand attacks child prodigies, déjà vu, hypnotic regression (he rejects the Bridey Murphy case emphatically) and Stevenson, noting for example that Stevenson often had to rely on interpreters. He grants that Stevenson has written about the subject more fully and intelligibly than anyone. Reception: Raymond Martin reviewed it positively in Religious Studies (1997); Robert Almeder argued in the Journal of Scientific Exploration that it is more debunking than the critical examination its title promises. One fairness point I can derive: the book (1996) appeared before Stevenson's two-volume birthmark work (1997), though Stevenson had published earlier papers on birthmarks; I did not check how much Edwards covers. The population argument (where do the souls come from as numbers grow) is often credited to Edwards, from memory.

If someone pushes back:
- Edwards had made up his mind in advance. His Introduction is open about aiming to discredit the evidence, and Almeder says the book reads as debunking. That is a fair criticism, and the essay's own use is mild: Edwards stands for the standard objections, and the sentence lists them without relying on his tone.
- Did Stevenson answer him? I did not find a rebuttal by Stevenson in the results. The Society for Psychical Research review with a Stevenson reply that I saw concerns a different book.

## Carl Sagan on children's past-life claims (The Demon-Haunted World) (1995)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Carl Sagan (1934-1996), astronomer, planetary scientist and the best-known science communicator of his day (Cosmos, 1980). The Demon-Haunted World is a book-length defence of sceptical thinking.

In the essay: 'Not enough time in one life', end of the paragraph on Stevenson and Tucker: 'Carl Sagan listed it among the few claims in that territory that deserved serious study, and said he doubted it would hold up.'

Where it sits: The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark (Random House, 1995; Ballantine paperback), chapter 17, 'The Marriage of Skepticism and Wonder', around page 302. The chapter number is inferred from page numbers in search results (the chapter begins on p. 293 and the next on p. 307 in one Ballantine edition) and pagination varies by edition, so open your copy.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Sagan lists three claims in the ESP field that he thinks deserve serious study: that thought alone can slightly affect random number generators, that people under mild sensory deprivation can receive thoughts or images projected at them, and that young children sometimes report details of a previous life that check out and that they could not otherwise have known. He says he picks them not because he thinks them likely to be valid but as examples of contentions that might be true. A secondary summary (NeuroLogica Blog) quotes the phrases 'at least some, although still dubious, experimental support' and 'of course, I could be wrong'; the exact wording in the book was not seen in this session.

Read it free:
- Steven Novella, NeuroLogica Blog, 2014, on the passage and its misuse by Eben Alexander: https://theness.com/neurologicablog/?p=6561 (seen in a search result)
- Princeton 'What do the skeptics say?' slide that cites the passage: https://noosphere.princeton.edu/papers/GCP5_files/slide0258.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: The book is in copyright, and no legal free copy was found. Look for it at the Internet Archive lending library (search the title), or buy the Ballantine paperback. The passage is short; the surrounding chapter on scepticism and wonder is worth reading in full.

Background: Sagan wrote the book to defend scientific thinking against pseudoscience, with a chapter on a 'baloney detection kit'. Near the end he turns around and warns that closed-mindedness is also a failure. In the chapter on the marriage of scepticism and wonder he gives three claims in the ESP field that he thinks merit serious study. The first is the old random-number-generator work, the second is the Ganzfeld studies on thought transfer under mild sensory deprivation (both from memory), and the third is the claim that some young children report details of a previous life. He explains that he chose them not because he thinks they are likely to be valid, but as examples of what might turn out to be true, and the secondary summary has him allowing that he could be wrong. Alex's sentence matches that: he listed it as worthy of study and doubted it would hold up. Two cautions. Eben Alexander cited the passage as if Sagan believed in reincarnation, and Novella argues that this misreads him. And the line 'worth studying' should not be stretched into 'probably true'. Sagan's own rule, 'extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence', comes from Cosmos (1980), from memory.

If someone pushes back:
- Didn't Sagan believe in reincarnation? No. In this passage he says he does not think the claims are likely to be valid, and wants them studied because they might be true.
- Is it fair to use Sagan here? The essay uses him as an example of a famous sceptic who thought the question deserved a look, and says he doubted it. It does not use him as support.
