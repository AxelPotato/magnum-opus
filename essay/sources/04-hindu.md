## The Upanishads as a corpus (atman, Brahman, and how old) (Early prose Upanishads (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) usually placed before the Buddha, very roughly the 7th to 6th century BCE, with wide uncertainty. The verse Upanishads (Katha, Isha, Shvetashvatara and others) are generally put later.)

Kind: tradition. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Upanishads are the late, philosophical layer of the Vedas: texts about knowledge of the self and of the ground of the world, as opposed to ritual. About a dozen early ones carry the tradition's weight, and the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya are the two oldest and longest. They are collections of teachings and dialogues, not one system.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': 'The oldest statement of it that I know sits in the Upanishads', followed by Chandogya 6.8.7 and Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10. In 'The same things in different words': 'The Upanishads say it with atman and Brahman.' Later in the essay: Isha 6 and 7 and Brihadaranyaka 3.7.23.

Where it sits: No single locator. The dating question is item C1-20 in CHECKLIST.md. The passages themselves are in the entries that follow.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Not a passage. The sentence to be ready to defend is 'The oldest statement of it that I know sits in the Upanishads'. The Rigveda (for example the creation hymn 10.129) is older than the Upanishads, so 'one of the oldest' is the safer wording.

- Max Muller, The Upanishads Part 2 (Sacred Books of the East vol. 15, 1884): Katha, Mundaka, Taittiriya, Brihadaranyaka, Shvetashvatara, Prashna, Maitri: https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe15/ (seen in a search result)

- Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Hindu Philosophy (overview): https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/ (seen in a search result)
- Robert Hume, The Thirteen Principal Upanishads (1921, public domain): search archive.org for the title: https://archive.org (not checked)

Read it in a book: Patrick Olivelle, Upanisads, Oxford World's Classics (Oxford University Press, 1996; reissued 2008, ISBN 9780199540259): the best readable modern translation, with notes. For the Sanskrit beside the English and fuller notes: The Early Upanisads: Annotated Text and Translation (Oxford University Press, 1998), which opens with the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya. For the Advaita reading: Swami Gambhirananda, Eight Upanishads with Shankara's commentary (Advaita Ashrama).

Background: Vedanta means 'end of the Veda', and the Upanishads are that end. They were composed in Sanskrit by priestly and sometimes royal teachers, probably in northern India. As I remember it, Olivelle puts the earliest prose texts (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya and a few more) before the Buddha and the verse texts (Katha, Isha, Shvetashvatara and more) after him. A secondary summary I saw says he warns that dating closer than a few centuries is guesswork, so treat every date as a range. The vocabulary the essay uses is atman, the inner self, and Brahman, the ground of everything. Several famous sayings, the mahavakyas, say the two are the same, including tat tvam asi and aham brahmasmi. The texts do not agree on everything: some teach identity, some a personal Lord, some a mix. Later schools read them differently. Shankara's Advaita is non-dual, Ramanuja's is qualified non-dual and Madhva's is dual. That is why it is safer to say 'one reading of the Upanishads' and to name Advaita when you mean it. Schrodinger's tie between his one-mind view and the Upanishads belongs to another reading pack but rests on the same texts.

If someone pushes back:
- 'The Upanishads do not all teach oneness.' Agree. You are quoting the strand that Advaita made central, and other strands of the same texts are theistic or dual.
- 'Older traditions say it too.' Yes. Change to 'one of the oldest'. The Rigveda and other ancient texts come earlier, and the essay only claims the oldest statement that you know.

## Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7 (tat tvam asi) (Early Upanishad, probably 7th to 6th century BCE (broad range, contested))

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A major early Upanishad of the Samaveda, in eight chapters. Chapter 6 is one long lesson from the teacher Uddalaka Aruni to his son Shvetaketu. The sentence tat tvam asi is the best known of the four 'great sayings' (mahavakyas) of Vedanta.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': 'In the Chandogya, a father tells his son Svetaketu that the finest essence of which the whole world is made is the self, and that he is that.' It is offered as 'the oldest statement' of the axiom's first clause.

Where it sits: Chandogya Upanishad, chapter (prapathaka) 6, section (khanda) 8, verse 7. The same refrain closes the next sections too: 6.9.4, 6.10.3, 6.11.3, 6.12.3, 6.13.3, 6.14.3, 6.15.3 and 6.16.3, nine times in all.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: that finest essence, the one thing this whole world has as its self, is the truth, it is the self (atman), and 'tat tvam asi', 'you are that', Shvetaketu. Translators render the last words differently ('That art thou' in Muller, 'you are that' or 'that is how you are' in modern versions). I did not see an exact wording this session. A Columbia course page is said by the check to quote Olivelle at p. 152 of The Early Upanisads.

Read it free:
- Chandogya 6.8.7 with Sanskrit and word glosses (shlokam.org): https://shlokam.org/texts/Chandogya-6-8-7 (seen in a search result)
- Muller's translation, Sacred Books of the East vol. 1: use the index page and open the Khandogya, sixth chapter: https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe01/pageidx.htm (seen in a search result)
- Muller's text of Khandogya chapter 6 on one page (miscellanie.com): https://www.miscellanie.com/allthetexts/hindu/Others/khandogya06.html (seen in a search result)
- Columbia course page on the mahavakyas of the Samaveda, quoting Olivelle: https://columbia.edu/itc/religion/nonduality/classnotes/sep10/samaveda_mahavakya.html (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Mahavakyas (secondary, useful for the other three sayings): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81v%C4%81kyas (seen in a search result)
- Joel Brereton, 'Tat Tvam Asi in Context', Zeitschrift der Deutschen Morgenlandischen Gesellschaft 136 (1986): search the title; the earlier check saw a PDF on ahandfulofleaves.org: https://www.ahandfulofleaves.org (not checked)

Read it in a book: Patrick Olivelle, Upanisads (Oxford World's Classics, 1996) or The Early Upanisads: Annotated Text and Translation (Oxford University Press, 1998), where Chandogya is the second text after Brihadaranyaka. Swami Gambhirananda's Chandogya Upanishad with Shankara's commentary (Advaita Ashrama) gives the Advaita reading line by line.

Background: Chapter 6 is one long lesson. Shvetaketu comes home at twenty four, proud of having studied every Veda. His father asks whether he learned the teaching by which the unheard becomes heard and the unknown known. He did not. Uddalaka answers with images: clay and every clay pot, gold and every ornament, the nectar of many trees merged in honey, rivers running into the sea, salt dissolved in water that you cannot see but can taste, the invisible seed in a banyan fruit. In each, one underlying thing takes many names and shapes. At 6.8 the lesson reaches sleep, in which a person has gone into Being (sat). Then 6.8.7 gives the line the essay uses. Three things to know. First, the verse speaks of Being and the self (atman) and does not contain the word Brahman. 'Atman and Brahman' is the later Vedanta pairing, found elsewhere, for example in Chandogya 3.14 and Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10. Second, the schools read the sentence differently: Shankara as identity, Ramanuja as the self being the body of the Lord, and Madhva as 'atat tvam asi', you are not that. Third, Brereton's 1986 article argues the sentence must be read inside Uddalaka's whole lesson. I could not open it, so check what he concludes before citing him.

If someone pushes back:
- 'It is said to one student about his own self, not to everyone.' True. That all selves are one self is what Advaita draws out of it and what the axiom assumes. The essay does not claim the verse says it outright.
- 'Other schools read it as something else.' Yes, Ramanuja and Madhva do. Say that you are quoting one witness and one reading, and that the reading is the Advaita one.

## Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.10 (Early Upanishad, probably 7th to 6th century BCE (broad range, contested))

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The Brihadaranyaka ('great forest text') belongs to the White Yajurveda and is the longest and probably the oldest of the major Upanishads. Section 1.4 is a creation account, and 1.4.10 is its turning point.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': 'The Brihadaranyaka says that in the beginning this world was only Brahman, which knew only itself, thought "I am Brahman," and so became the whole.'

Where it sits: Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.10 (the creation account runs 1.4.1 to 1.4.10). Numbering follows the Kanva recension that Muller and Olivelle use; Olivelle's book has a concordance with the other recension.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: in the beginning this was Brahman alone. It knew only itself, 'I am Brahman', and therefore became all. Whoever among gods, seers and humans woke to this became that. Then a warning: whoever worships another god thinking 'he is one and I am another' does not know. Sanskrit key words seen in a handout: brahma va idam agra asit ... aham brahmasmiti ... tasmat tat sarvam abhavat. The essay's English wording follows a modern translation, possibly Olivelle's, which I could not confirm. One English rendering seen: 'In the beginning, Brahman was this. It knew only itself: I am Brahman' (advaita-vision.org).

Read it free:
- Muller's translation of Brihadaranyaka I, 4 (the whole creation account), universalfreemasonry.org: https://www.universalfreemasonry.org/en/library/the-upanishads-part-II/brihadâranyaka-upanishad-I-4 (seen in a search result)
- Sacred Texts mirror of Muller, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15, page for Brihadaranyaka I, 4 (sacred-texts.com has the same file as sbe15055.htm): https://neonvagabond.xyz/mirrors/sacred-texts/hin/sbe15/sbe15055.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sacred Texts, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15 (index): https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe15/ (seen in a search result)
- Muller's complete Brihadaranyaka on one page (hinduwebsite.com): https://www.hinduwebsite.com/sacredscripts/hinduism/upanishads/brihad.asp (seen in a search result)
- Columbia course page on the mahavakya of the Yajurveda (aham brahmasmi): https://columbia.edu/itc/religion/nonduality/classnotes/sep10/yajurveda_mahavakya.html (seen in a search result)
- Swami Madhavananda's translation with Shankara's commentary (Internet Archive): https://archive.org/details/Brihadaranyaka.Upanishad.Shankara.Bhashya.by.Swami.Madhavananda (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Patrick Olivelle, Upanisads (Oxford World's Classics, 1996) or The Early Upanisads (Oxford University Press, 1998). Swami Madhavananda, The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad with Shankara's commentary (Advaita Ashrama) for the Advaita reading.

Background: 1.4.1 to 4 tell a short creation story. In the beginning there was only the Self in the shape of a person. He looked around, saw nothing but himself, and said 'I am'. He was afraid, then reasoned that there was nothing to fear since there was nothing else, and the fear left. He took no delight, as a lonely person does not, so he wanted a second and divided himself into husband and wife, from whom creatures came. (This summary follows Muller's translation as seen in the check.) 1.4.10 is the sequel: this was Brahman, it knew only itself as 'I am Brahman', and from that it became all. Whoever woke to this became it. Then comes a warning that fits your theme: someone who worships another god thinking 'he is one and I am another' does not know, and is like an animal serving the gods. Two cautions. First, the becoming all comes through knowing. The text is about what knowledge does for a person, and it is not an account of how a whole falls into separation. The nearest thing to a separation story is the loneliness in 1.4.1 to 4, which is why that opening is the best Upanishadic text for 'knowing needs a gap'. Second, translations differ in small words ('knew itself', 'knew only itself'), so name the translator if you quote it.

If someone pushes back:
- 'This text is about waking up, not about separation.' Correct. The separation side is the loneliness and the splitting in 1.4.1 to 4. The essay uses 1.4.10 for the first clause of the axiom, not the second.
- 'Aham brahmasmi means each person is God.' In the text it is what Brahman said of itself, and a person who knows it becomes the whole. It is a claim about knowledge, and Advaita reads it as identity of the self with the ground.

## Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.23 (the inner controller) (Early Upanishad, probably 7th to 6th century BCE (broad range, contested))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Inner Controller section (antaryamin brahmana) of the Brihadaranyaka. The sage Yajnavalkya is questioned by Uddalaka Aruni, the same Uddalaka who teaches Shvetaketu in the Chandogya.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'In the Brihadaranyaka the Self is the witness in every being, and there is no other witness but him (3.7.23).' It supports the Advaita answer to 'who is it that carries over' with a single witness at the level of the processor.

Where it sits: Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.23, the last verse of the section 3.7 (Kanva numbering).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: he is the unseen seer, the unheard hearer, the unthought thinker, the unknown knower. There is no other seer, hearer, thinker or knower than he. He is your Self, the inner controller, the immortal. Everything other than this is affliction. The word 'witness' is a translator's choice: an Advaita-l post attributes a rendering with 'no other witness' to Swami Madhavananda. Muller and Olivelle, as far as I recall, say 'seer'. I did not see the verse in Muller's text this session.

Read it free:
- Advaita-l post giving the 3.7.23 rendering attributed to Swami Madhavananda: https://www.advaita-vedanta.org/archives/advaita-l/2020-June/054719.html (seen in a search result)
- Muller's complete Brihadaranyaka on one page (hinduwebsite.com): look for the third chapter, seventh section: https://www.hinduwebsite.com/sacredscripts/hinduism/upanishads/brihad.asp (seen in a search result)
- Sacred Texts, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15 (index): the third chapter, seventh section (III, 7): https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe15/ (seen in a search result)
- Madhavananda's translation with Shankara's commentary (Internet Archive): https://archive.org/details/Brihadaranyaka.Upanishad.Shankara.Bhashya.by.Swami.Madhavananda (seen in a search result)
- Advaita Vision's running commentary on the Brihadaranyaka: https://www.advaita-vision.org/brihadaranyaka-upanishad/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Patrick Olivelle, Upanisads (Oxford World's Classics, 1996) or The Early Upanisads (1998) for the plain reading. Swami Madhavananda's Brihadaranyaka with Shankara's commentary (Advaita Ashrama) for the 'witness' wording and the Advaita argument.

Background: In this section Yajnavalkya first names the thread that ties this world, the next and all beings together (it is the wind, 3.7.2). Then he describes the inner controller. A refrain runs through earth, water, fire, sky, wind, sun, moon, mind, understanding and seed: it dwells in that thing, is within it, the thing does not know it, that thing is its body, and it controls it from within. It is your Self, the inner controller, the immortal. The closing verse 3.7.23 says that it is the unseen seer, the unheard hearer, the unthought thinker, the unknown knower, with no other seer, hearer, thinker or knower. The essay's 'witness' is a translator's word, taken from the Advaita tradition's term for the seer role, so if someone checks Muller or Olivelle they will read 'seer'. The check found this verse only through that one post, so open the text before leaning on it. There are two old readings. Shankara takes the controller as the Self that is identical with each person. Theistic Vedantins take it as God ruling the world from inside it, with controller and controlled still two. So the verse is a witness for a single inner knower, not a clean proof of oneness.

If someone pushes back:
- 'The text says controller and controlled, which is two, not one.' Fair. The passage can be read as a ruler within, as Ramanuja did. You are using the Advaita reading and you can say so.
- 'You wrote witness, the text says seer.' Say that witness is how Madhavananda and the Advaita commentators render the seer role, and that the plain translation is seer.

## Isha Upanishad 6 and 7 (Later than the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya; no firm date. Olivelle, as I remember, puts it after the Buddha.)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A short Upanishad of eighteen verses, the last chapter (40) of the Vajasaneyi Samhita of the White Yajurveda, the only Upanishad that sits inside a Samhita. It is named for its first word, isha, 'by the Lord'.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers': 'The Isha Upanishad says that the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, recoils from nothing, and asks what delusion or sorrow can remain for the one who sees oneness. That is the ethic of oneness in its strongest form.'

Where it sits: Isha Upanishad (also called Isavasya or Vajasaneyi Samhita Upanishad), verses 6 and 7. Also Vajasaneyi Samhita 40.6 and 40.7.

The passage: Muller's 1879 translation (Sacred Books of the East vol. 1), as reproduced on a third-party copy and seen in the search summary this session: verse 6, 'And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it.' Verse 7, 'When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?' Check against the sacred-texts page before quoting. Other translators have 'delusion or sorrow' in verse 7.

Read it free:
- Isha Upanishad verse 6 with Sanskrit and translation (shlokam.org): https://shlokam.org/texts/ishavasya-6/ (seen in a search result)
- Muller's Vajasaneyi Samhita Upanishad on one page (hinduwebsite.com): https://hinduwebsite.com/sacredscripts/hinduism/upanishads/vagasaneyi.asp (seen in a search result)
- Sacred Texts, Sacred Books of the East vol. 1, the Vajasaneyi Samhita Upanishad section: https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe01/sbe01021.htm (seen in a search result)
- Muller's Isavasya Upanishad on one page (miscellanie.com): https://www.miscellanie.com/allthetexts/hindu/Others/isavasya01.html (seen in a search result)
- Third-party copy of Muller's Isavasya, the source of the exact verse wording above: https://advocatetanmoy.com/isavasya-upanishad-translated-by-friedrich-max-muller-1879/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Patrick Olivelle, Upanisads (Oxford World's Classics, 1996). Sri Aurobindo's The Upanishads, which contains his translation and commentary on the Isha, for the 'shrinks not' rendering that the essay's wording follows. Swami Gambhirananda, Eight Upanishads (Advaita Ashrama), which starts with the Isha and gives Shankara's commentary.

Background: Verse 1 says all this is to be covered over, or lived in, by the Lord, and to enjoy by renouncing. Verse 2 says to do your actions and wish to live a hundred years. Verses 6 and 7 are the oneness verses. The key word in 6 is vijugupsate, 'shrinks from, feels disgust at, hates'. Muller read it with the Self: 'he never turns away from it'. Most later translators, Aurobindo's 'shrinks not' among them, take it as not recoiling from any being, which is the essay's 'recoils from nothing'. Both are grammatically possible, so do not say the verse 'obviously' means one of them. Verse 7 has moha (delusion or bewilderment) and shoka (sorrow); Muller has 'sorrow' and 'trouble', others 'delusion'. Note also that verses 9 to 14 warn against one-sidedness with the words vidya and avidya. Here they are usually read as knowledge and ritual or worldly action, which is not the Advaita sense of avidya as ignorance of the self. Do not carry the word over to the maya and avidya entry. Shankara, Madhva and others wrote commentaries on the Isha, so each school has its own reading of 6 and 7.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Your wording recoils from nothing is not what Muller says.' Right. Muller reads it as not turning away from the Self, and most later translators read it as not shrinking from any being. Both are possible, and the essay follows the second.
- 'This only says the knower feels no sorrow, not that they treat others well.' The ethical point is an inference, as the essay says: if you see yourself in all beings, there is nothing to recoil from.

## Bhagavad Gita 2.13 and 2.22 (the embodied self and the worn clothes) (Roughly the last centuries BCE to the early centuries CE (broad range, contested))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Bhagavad Gita ('Song of the Lord') is 700 verses inside the Mahabharata, a dialogue on the battlefield of Kurukshetra between the warrior Arjuna, who refuses to fight his kin, and his charioteer Krishna, God in human form. Together with the Upanishads and the Brahma Sutra it is one of the three foundations of Vedanta.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'In the Hindu texts the self that moves from body to body is the atman, and the Bhagavad Gita compares it to a person putting on new clothes and discarding worn ones.' The Sources list adds 2.13.

Where it sits: Bhagavad Gita 2.13 (the embodied one passes through childhood, youth and old age, then to another body) and 2.22 (worn clothes and new bodies). These are in chapter 2, the 'Sankhya' chapter.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): 2.22 in one translation seen this session (Alkondavilli Govindacharya's Gita Bhashya, as quoted on a Sri Vaishnava site): 'As, casting off worn out garments, man puts on other new ones', and so the embodied one casts off worn bodies and takes new ones. The Sanskrit begins vasamsi jirnani yatha vihaya. 2.13 is a paraphrase: as the embodied one passes through childhood, youth and old age in this body, so it passes to another body, and the steady person is not bewildered by it.

Read it in a book: Barbara Stoler Miller, The Bhagavad-Gita: Krishna's Counsel in Time of War (Bantam, 1986): poetic and clear. Laurie Patton, The Bhagavad Gita (Penguin Classics, 2008). Gavin Flood and Charles Martin, The Bhagavad Gita: A New Translation (Norton, 2012). Winthrop Sargeant, The Bhagavad Gita (SUNY Press, 1984) for the Sanskrit word by word. Swami Gambhirananda, Bhagavad Gita with the commentary of Sankaracarya (Advaita Ashrama) for the Advaita reading.

Background: Chapter 2 is Krishna's first answer to Arjuna. He says Arjuna grieves for what should not be grieved, because the embodied one (dehin) is not born and does not die (2.20). In 2.13 the embodied one moves through childhood, youth and age in this body and then to another body, and the steady person is not bewildered. In 2.22 it casts off worn bodies as a man casts off worn clothes. In context the point is consolation, and it also serves as the reason Arjuna should fight. How to read the war, literally or as allegory (Gandhi favoured allegory), is an old argument, and the essay does not need to take a side. In Shankara's commentary on 2.22 to 23 the atman itself neither goes nor comes. What travels is the embodied jiva with its subtle body. So the essay's 'the self that moves from body to body is the atman' is fine as popular speech, and if pushed, say 'the embodied self'. The Gita says more about lives than the essay uses: 6.41 to 45 (the yogi who falls is reborn and 'perfected through many births', 6.45) and 13.28 (seeing the Lord equally everywhere, he does not harm the Self by the Self). All three Vedanta schools wrote commentaries on it and drew different doctrines from it.

If someone pushes back:
- 'In Advaita the atman does not travel at all.' Correct. What travels is the jiva with its subtle body. The essay's sentence is at the popular level, and you can say 'the embodied self' to be exact.
- 'The Gita uses this to tell Arjuna to kill.' The line comes in a debate about duty in war. The essay only uses it for the continuity of a self across bodies, and how the war should be read is a separate argument you do not have to settle.

## Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 (lila, creation as play) (Attributed to Badarayana; dating contested, from the last centuries BCE to about the 5th century CE. Shankara's commentary is from the 8th century CE.)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The Brahma Sutras (also Vedanta Sutras) are about 555 very short aphorisms in four chapters that arrange the teaching of the Upanishads into an argument and defend it against rival schools. They are close to unreadable without a commentary, so what is said about them usually comes from Shankara, Ramanuja or Madhva.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 meets the question of why a complete God would create by calling creation play, lila.' The essay's own reading follows: a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view, and a point of view is a separation.

Where it sits: Brahma Sutra 2.1.33 in Shankara's numbering: lokavat tu lila-kaivalyam. The objection it answers is 2.1.32.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. 2.1.32 objects that Brahman cannot be the maker of the world, because every maker has a purpose and a being with a purpose lacks something. 2.1.33 answers: but it is mere sport, as in the world. In Shankara's commentary (Thibaut's translation, as the earlier check saw) the example is a king or minister whose wishes are all fulfilled and who plays with no further aim, or breathing, which goes on without a motive. I did not open the page this session.

Read it free:

## Advaita Vedanta: maya and avidya (Shankara) (Shankara in the 8th century CE (Stanford Encyclopedia; the IEP's Madhva entry gives the 9th). Gaudapada about 500 CE.)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: Shankara (traditionally 8th century CE) is the most authoritative thinker of Advaita ('non-dual') Vedanta. He wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutras. Maya and avidya are the two words the essay borrows from his school to say why one whole looks like many.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'Advaita Vedanta calls it maya, and scholars of Advaita warn that illusion translates the word badly, since it means something dependent, neither fully real nor simply unreal. It calls the ignorance that keeps it going avidya, a word Buddhism uses too.'

Where it sits: Concepts, not a verse. For primary reading: the opening 'superimposition' (adhyasa) section of Shankara's commentary on the Brahma Sutras (Thibaut, Sacred Books of the East vol. 34, near the start) and his Upadeshasahasri (Mayeda's translation).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of the doctrine: avidya, beginningless ignorance, covers what Brahman is and superimposes (adhyasa) the world of names and forms on it, as a snake is seen on a rope. The world that results is mithya: it appears and works, but it depends on Brahman. Later Advaitins called its status anirvacaniya, 'not definable as either real or unreal'. The Stanford Encyclopedia compares maya to a magic trick, an objective appearance that falls short of full reality.

Karl Potter (ed.), Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies vol. 3: Advaita Vedanta up to Samkara and his Pupils (Princeton, 1981) for summaries of the primary works.

Background: Maya in the Rigveda means a god's power or craft, even a trick. In the Upanishads (for example Shvetashvatara 4.9 to 10) it becomes the power through which the Lord makes the world. In Shankara it works with avidya, ignorance, and adhyasa, superimposition, the mistake of seeing silver in mother of pearl. Avidya is beginningless but ends with knowledge. The world is mithya: it appears and works but depends on Brahman. It is not real as Brahman is, and not unreal like a hare's horn. So the essay's definition, 'a wrong reading of something real', is close to the Advaita idea and is not idiosyncratic. The popular claim that maya means the world is an illusion is a simplification. Some specialists argue that Shankara himself leans on avidya and adhyasa more than on maya, and that the stress on maya grew later. Paul Hacker's 1950 article is the one to look for (from memory, I did not check it). Buddhism's avidya (Pali avijja) is the first link in dependent arising. It means ignorance of the four truths and of how things arise, which is not the same content, though the essay only claims it is the same word.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Maya just means illusion.' Popularly yes. The technical Advaita position is that the world depends on Brahman and is neither fully real nor nothing, which is why the essay says an illusion is a wrong reading of something real.
- 'Buddhist avidya is not the same as Advaita avidya.' Agreed. The essay says only that the word is shared. What the ignorance is about differs: not seeing impermanence and no-self in Buddhism, not seeing the self as Brahman in Advaita.

Background: The doctrine of karma and rebirth is not in the oldest Vedic hymns. In the Upanishads it first appears in the teaching of the five fires and the two paths after death (Chandogya 5.3 to 10, Brihadaranyaka 6.2). Notably, Shvetaketu cannot answer the king's questions there and the king Pravahana teaches the Brahmin, which scholars read as a sign that the idea entered priestly teaching from outside the priestly circle (from memory). Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5 gives the moral version in one line: as one acts, so one becomes. The Gita adds the embodied self taking new bodies (2.22) and the yogi perfected over many births (6.45). Who administers the result is where the schools differ. Badarayana gives the Lord that role in Brahma Sutra 3.2.38, Ramanuja and Madhva follow, and the Mimamsa school works without a God, with an unseen potency (apurva). In Advaita karma, merit and rebirth belong to the empirical standpoint, and Shankara says only knowledge brings liberation. Advaita still accepts rebirth until liberation, carried by the subtle body and the jiva, while the atman does not travel. This answers the 'who carries over' question at the level of appearance.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Hindu schools do not agree on karma.' True, and the essay says the traditions differ on who administers it. The axiom needs only that consequence comes back to the one who acts.
- 'Advaita says the atman does not move.' Right. What moves is the jiva with its subtle body, so the essay's sentence is popular speech. The essay already says the Advaita answer is at the level of appearance.
