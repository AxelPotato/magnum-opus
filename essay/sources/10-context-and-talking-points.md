## Theosophy (Helena Blavatsky and the Theosophical Society) (Society founded 17 November 1875 in New York. Blavatsky 1831 to 1891. Isis Unveiled 1877; The Secret Doctrine 1888; The Key to Theosophy 1889.)

Kind: tradition. Status: partly checked.

Who: A modern esoteric movement founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky, Henry Olcott, William Judge and others, which moved to India in 1879 and later split into several bodies. Blavatsky's main books are Isis Unveiled, The Secret Doctrine (subtitled The Synthesis of Science, Religion and Philosophy) and The Key to Theosophy, a question and answer primer. It presents itself as a restatement of an old wisdom behind all religions, taught to her by hidden Masters.

In the essay: One paragraph in 'The same things in different words' (the paragraph that begins 'A word on where I stand'): Alex says Theosophy is the nearest organized system to his views (one source, a long return, many lives, consequences that come back to the one who acts), that he leaves out the ascended masters and the parts that drift further off, and that it carries more mysticism than he likes, so he will not quote it. Since version 2.3.0 the essay cites no Theosophical text. Theosophy also appears in one other sentence, in 'Not enough time in one life': the English framing of the Tibetan Book of the Dead is 'shaped by his own Theosophical background' (Evans-Wentz joined the Theosophical Society in 1902, per the Wikipedia and Theosophy Wiki pages in the CHECKLIST).

Where it sits: Essay v2.3.0: 'The same things in different words', third paragraph (line 57 of part-2-spiritual.md); 'Not enough time in one life', Tibetan Book of the Dead sentence. Primary texts: The Secret Doctrine vol. 1, Proem, the three fundamental propositions (pp. 14 to 17 in the 1888 pagination, per one Theosophical page; not checked against a scan); vol. 1 near p. 643 for karma as harmony (reprints quote it at pp. 643 to 644; the arrangement in the 1888 original was not confirmed); The Key to Theosophy, see the table of contents on Gutenberg for the sections on the fundamental teachings, reincarnation and karma (section numbers not checked).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of the Proem. (1) One omnipresent, eternal, boundless, immutable Principle, beyond thought. (2) The universe appears and vanishes in regular cycles, like an ebb and flow. (3) All souls are fundamentally identical with the Universal Over-Soul, itself an aspect of the Unknown Root, and each soul, as a spark of it, must make a compulsory pilgrimage through the Cycle of Incarnation (also called Necessity) under cyclic and karmic law. On karma, Theosophical reprints seen in a search summary give: 'the only decree of Karma ... is absolute Harmony in the world of matter as it is in the world of Spirit', so 'it is we, who reward or punish ourselves'. Those two fragments are quoted from reprints whose wording varies; the 1888 text was not opened, so treat them as a guide to what to look for.

Background: Blavatsky was a Russian-born writer and medium who said hidden Masters had taught her an old wisdom behind all religions. The Secret Doctrine opens with three propositions: one boundless Principle beyond thought; universes that appear and vanish in cycles; and the identity of every soul with a universal Over-Soul, with a compulsory pilgrimage of incarnation under karmic law. Her karma has no judge: we reward or punish ourselves. Rebirth came late: Isis Unveiled (1877) argued against Allan Kardec's Spiritist reincarnation, and repeated earthly lives are taught from the 1880s. Her reason for the descent is that a spark becomes self-conscious only by passing through experience, close to Alex's 'knowing needs a gap'. Close to the axiom: propositions one and three, the return, karma without a judge. Different: she gives it as received teaching, her Absolute lies beyond consciousness, and the proof is said to sit in an archive no outsider has seen. The Masters were Mahatmas, living men she placed in the Himalayas, who wrote to Sinnett and Hume in 1880 to 1885. 'Ascended Masters' is a later label that spread through Guy Ballard's I AM movement in the 1930s, and Theosophists say it distorts the older idea. Scholars see the Society as a major channel of Asian ideas to the West, with shaky Eastern scholarship (Coleman traced much of her material to nineteenth century books), a root-race scheme with racial hierarchies, and an 1885 Society for Psychical Research report that called her phenomena fraud.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't Theosophy discredited? The Society for Psychical Research judged her phenomena fraudulent in 1885, and a 1986 paper by Vernon Harrison in the SPR's own journal challenged the handwriting evidence. Scholars now mostly study what the movement did, not whether the Masters existed. The essay uses Theosophy for the shape of an idea only and quotes no Theosophical text.
- Isn't your axiom just the Secret Doctrine? It shares the shape, but she presents it as received teaching with hidden sources, and Alex presents it as an assumption he tests for coherence. Also the Traditionalist school (Guenon) wrote against her movement and rejected its reincarnation, so the two movements cannot be cited as one team; see the perennial philosophy entry.

## The perennial philosophy (the Traditionalists, Huxley and the wider movement) (Phrase: Steuco 1540; Leibniz, letter of 26 August 1714. Twentieth century movement: Guenon from 1921; Huxley 1945; Schuon 1948 (French) and 1953 (English); Perry 1971; Smith 1976.)

Kind: tradition. Status: partly checked.

Who: The idea that the world's religions share one inner core under different outer forms, and the family of authors who defended it. The strict core is the Traditionalist school: Rene Guenon, Ananda Coomaraswamy and Frithjof Schuon, with Titus Burckhardt, Martin Lings, Marco Pallis, Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Huston Smith around them. Aldous Huxley's 1945 anthology is the best known door for English readers and is one voice among these, not the school. Others who compared traditions in related ways include William James, Evelyn Underhill, Rudolf Otto, W. T. Stace, Alan Watts and Ken Wilber.

In the essay: Not in the essay since version 2.3.0 (Alex's decision of 2026-10-08, see CLAUDE.md). Earlier drafts used Huxley's three-part definition as a skeleton and named Steuco, Schuon, James and Smith. Private context only: Alex's own position, that the traditions speak of the same few ideas in different words and that their inner teaching often carries what the popular version does not, has the same shape as the perennial claim in its modest form, so he may be asked whether this is just perennialism.

Where it sits: Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (1945), the opening Introduction (the three-part definition and the 'Highest Common Factor'); Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions, the Introduction and chapter 1, 'The Limitations of Exoterism' (chapter title recalled, not checked); Huston Smith, Forgotten Truth (1976); Katz's chapter in Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis (1978), the 'no pure experiences' sentence at about p. 26 (page recalled, not checked); Sedgwick, Against the Modern World (2004). SEP Mysticism has sections on perennialism and constructivism (seen in the outline only).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Huxley's Introduction defines the perennial philosophy in three parts: the metaphysic that 'recognizes a divine Reality substantial to the world of things and lives and minds'; a psychology that finds in the soul something similar to, or even identical with, that Reality; and an ethic that places man's final end in knowing the immanent and transcendent Ground of all being. He calls what is shared a 'Highest Common Factor in all preceding and subsequent theologies'. The quoted fragments are exact as printed in secondary pages that quote the Introduction (Boston University review page, Wikipedia, a search summary of a book review); the edition and page were not seen. The rest is paraphrase.

Background: The phrase is old. Agostino Steuco, a papal librarian, put it on a book in 1540 to mean a revealed deposit that Christianity completes; Leibniz used it in 1714 in a looser sense, gold to be panned out of every school. In the twentieth century it became a family of authors. The Traditionalists (Guenon from 1921, Coomaraswamy, Schuon from 1948) hold that each religion has an outer layer of doctrine, law and rite and an inner layer that agrees, and they insist on one real tradition with a lineage. Huston Smith's Forgotten Truth (1976) is the readable map. These authors disagree among themselves: Guenon wrote against Theosophy and rejected its reincarnation, and Coomaraswamy argued that only the one Self transmigrates. Critics (from the research files, not rechecked) come from several directions. Katz argued in 1978 that no experience is unmediated; Zaehner denied that mystics report one thing; Griffiths, Plantinga and D'Costa say pluralism is itself a doctrine the traditions would reject; Richard King calls the 'mystic East' a Western construct; Sedgwick and Hanegraaff study the movements as history. The standard complaint about the outer and inner rule is that it cannot lose, since any text that disagrees can be filed as outer. Alex differs in three ways: he derives the ideas from one axiom, he uses witnesses without a lineage, and he says openly where the traditions disagree (rebirth). Sedgwick also covers controversies around Schuon's later community; I did not check them.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't that just perennialism? In its modest form, yes: a family of ideas that recur in different vocabularies. Not in the strong forms. He claims no single mystical experience, no primordial source, no lineage and no rule that disagreeing texts are 'outer', and he says the traditions are not unanimous on lives. The strict school would not accept his method of many witnesses.
- Haven't scholars rejected perennialism? The strong form (one shared experience) has been a minority view since Katz in 1978. Doctrinal and family-resemblance forms are still argued (Smith, Hick, Schmidt-Leukel) and still attacked (Griffiths, Plantinga, D'Costa). There is no consensus, and the essay claims examples that fit, not proof.

The essay's record of holding back helps. A chain of true quotes can still mislead if the reader is not told what was left out. Alex's best defence is the table and the sources list, where every line can be looked up. The sharpest form of the criticism is aimed at the rule that a tradition's inner layer is the real one, because a text that disagrees can always be filed as outer. The essay uses that idea only in the modest form of 'I'll say which layer each quote comes from'.

Background: It would be false if that were the claim, and the essay takes care not to make it. Its claim is smaller: a few ideas recur in different vocabularies. It has been corrected where a believer or specialist would object, for example on Chabad, the Zohar, Eph 4:18 and the Hymn of the Pearl. Some believers will still hear 'the same' as flattening. Every historical comparer had a home team (Ficino and Steuco put Christianity on top, Dara Shikoh read the Upanishads in Sufi words, Vivekananda ranked Advaita highest), and Alex says his is the axiom. The useful answer to an objecting believer is to ask which line was misread and fix it.

If someone pushes back:
- Believers don't think their religion is one of several. True, and the essay says the traditions use different gods. It asks to be read as a thought experiment about a few ideas and does not ask any tradition to agree with it.
- Isn't that what Hick says? Related, not the same. Hick holds that the traditions are different responses to one ineffable Real and is attacked from both sides (Plantinga: pluralism is itself an exclusive claim; Buddhists: nirvana is not a manifestation of a ground). Alex claims only that certain ideas recur.

## Talking point: 'Don't Buddhists deny a self, so how can it be one whole?'

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: The strongest objection from Buddhist studies. Early Buddhism teaches not-self, Theravada has no creator, ground or whole, and Mahayana emptiness is not a substance.

In the essay: The essay does not say Buddhism teaches one whole. The table puts dependent arising and the Huayan net of jewels under 'The one whole'. 'Why separation feels so real' reads the Heart Sutra as saying things have 'no fixed independent nature', calls it 'the Buddhist way of saying that the walls between us are not made of anything solid', and says the self is treated as assembled from parts. The same section notes that Buddhism 'uses other words than the Upanishads' for what is left when ignorance ends.

Where it sits: Essay v2.3.0: 'Why separation feels so real', first two paragraphs; the table rows 'The one whole' and 'The veil'; 'Not enough time in one life', the Nagasena and Samyutta Nikaya sentences. The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The world is 'empty of a self or of anything pertaining to a self' (SN 35.85). Not exact for any named translation.

Background: Correct, and Theravada gives the oneness clause no support. Early Buddhism teaches not-self: each of the five aggregates is to be seen as not mine, not I, not my self (SN 22.59). The earlier research round recalls the Buddha listing 'all is a unity' among extremes in SN 12.48 (not retrieved this session; open Thanissaro's translation before using it). Mahayana emptiness means no intrinsic nature and not a substance or ground, which is why the essay warns against reading emptiness as the One. What overlaps with the axiom is the shape of the problem: a constructed sense of a separate self, ignorance as its root, a staged path (stream entry to arahant), letting go as the method, and something that continues without a fixed soul. The closest Buddhist ideas to a whole are the Huayan jewel net, buddha-nature and some Dzogchen, and other Buddhists dispute all three. Scholars also differ on how far 'not-self' reaches. The SEP notes that the reading which limits the denial to aggregate-based selves is considered tenuous, while Thanissaro argues that the Buddha never concluded 'there is no self' (from the research file, not rechecked). An earlier research round said the honest wording is 'same structure of the problem and the way out, differing on whether a single whole lies behind it'. Alex corrected that round's tone on 2026-10-07, because it judged by public teaching, but the specific points are what a Buddhist specialist could raise.

If someone pushes back:
- So Buddhism contradicts the axiom. On clause one, Theravada gives no support and the essay does not claim it does. On the other clauses (a constructed self, a veil of ignorance, an end to it, letting go) the match is strong. The table cites dependent arising and Huayan, not a Buddhist One.
- Isn't emptiness just the One? No. Emptiness is the absence of intrinsic nature, and 'not two' does not mean 'one' (Loy). The essay says Buddhists read 'form is emptiness' as meaning things have no fixed independent nature.
