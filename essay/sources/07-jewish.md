## Shneur Zalman of Liadi and Chabad (Habad) Hasidism (1745 to 1812 (dates from memory, not checked this session))

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, called the Alter Rebbe, founded Chabad, the intellectual branch of Hasidism, and wrote the Tanya and a law code, the Shulchan Aruch HaRav. Chabad is a Hebrew acronym for wisdom, understanding, knowledge. Today's Chabad-Lubavitch movement runs Chabad.org and Kabbalah Online, which supplied most of the pages the check found.

In the essay: In 'The axiom and the machine' the essay says Chabad Hasidism, founded by Shneur Zalman, teaches that from God's own point of view the world is as nothing before him and has no independent existence, while it is real from ours, and calls that close to the processor's view of the two windows. In 'Karma, or the fingers' it adds that the Tanya grounds love for a fellow Jew in one shared root of souls in God. Both points are in the Judaism column of the table.

Where it sits: Essay: 'The axiom and the machine' (paragraph beginning 'Several traditions say something near it in their own words'); 'Karma, or the fingers' (the paragraph on the image of the fingers); table rows 'The one whole' and 'What you do to another'; Sources, 'The traditions' inner layers'. Text: Tanya Part II (Gate of Unity and Faith) chapters 1 to 7 and 20; Tanya Part I chapter 32.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. God alone truly exists. Creation is sustained moment by moment by the divine word, so in relation to God it is as nothing, yet creatures experience a real world. And Jewish souls are branches of one root in God, divided only by their bodies.

Read it free:
- Encyclopaedia Judaica, Chabad (on Jewish Virtual Library): https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/jsource/judaica/ejud_0002_0004_0_04125.html (seen in a search result)
- My Jewish Learning, The Theology of Chabad: https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/the-theology-of-chabad/ (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, Writings of the Alter Rebbe (an index of his works): https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/2076435/jewish/Writings-of-the-Alter-Rebbe.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Naftali Loewenthal, Communicating the Infinite: The Emergence of the Habad School (University of Chicago Press, 1990), for the ideas and history. Immanuel Etkes, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liady: The Origins of Chabad Hasidism (Brandeis University Press, 2015), for the life. Both from memory, not checked this session.

Background: Hasidism began in the mid eighteenth century in Podolia and Volhynia around the Baal Shem Tov, as a movement of inner devotion, joy and closeness to God, in a culture that prized scholarship over feeling. Shneur Zalman studied under the Baal Shem Tov's successor, the Maggid Dov Ber of Mezritch, and gave Hasidism a systematic, intellectual form, which the acronym Chabad points at. His Tanya (first printed 1796) is built around the beinoni, the 'intermediate' person who never acts on his impulses but still feels them, and around two souls in each Jew, a divine one and an animal one. Opponents, the mitnagdim, feared the movement, and I recall that he was denounced to the Russian authorities and held in 1798 and again in 1800. Chabad's goal is to make this lowest world a home for God, so the self-nullification it teaches is meant to fuel action in the world, not to retreat from it. That corrects any reading of Chabad as simply 'letting go'. Chabad.org and Kabbalah Online are run by the movement, so they teach it from the inside. For outside views use Elior, Loewenthal and Etkes. On the axiom: Chabad has the three clauses in its own terms (one reality, a concealment that makes the world look separate, an end to the concealment), but with a Creator who stays Creator. The essay says 'close to', and the processor picture is the essay's own.

If someone pushes back:
- Is Chabad saying the world is an illusion? Not simply. Chabad holds that from God's side only God truly exists and from ours the world is real. Scholars disagree how far the Tanya goes (Elior reads it as acosmism, Jacobson and Rubin argue Shneur Zalman believed the world is real). The essay uses only the 'from God's side' half and says the computer picture is its own.
- You say you are not religious, so why quote a Hasidic movement? As a witness to a pattern, never as an authority, and the essay says so near the start.

## Tanya, Gate of Unity and Faith (Sha'ar ha-Yichud ve-ha-Emunah) (Tanya Part I first printed 1796; check the print date of Part II)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The second part of the Tanya, the founding book of Chabad. It is a meditation on how God can be one when a world seems to exist. It was written by Shneur Zalman of Liadi and is studied daily by Chabad Hasidim.

In the essay: Source for the sentence that Chabad teaches that from God's own point of view the world is as nothing before him and has no independent existence, while it is real from ours. The sentence is in 'The axiom and the machine' and echoed in the table row 'The one whole'. The CHECKLIST (C1-25) required the softened wording now in the essay.

Where it sits: Tanya Part II, chapters 1 to 3 (creation by the divine word, 'as nothing'), chapter 7 (higher and lower unity) and chapter 20, as recorded in the CHECKLIST and in the search summaries.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Before creation God was alone and after creation he is alone, because everything is as nothing in relation to his being. The Encyclopaedia Judaica reports it that way (with the Tanya's page 219 as its source, in the CHECKLIST). Chapter 7 calls the Shema 'higher unity' (yichuda ila'ah), where only God exists, and 'Blessed be the name of his glorious kingdom' 'lower unity' (yichuda tata'ah), where the world appears to exist and is nullified in God.

Read it free:
- Steinsaltz Center, Tanya, Gate of Unity and Faith, chapter 7: https://steinsaltz-center.org/portal/library/Tanya/Shaar%20Hayichud%20Vehaemuna/chapter/7 (seen in a search result)
- Steinsaltz Center, Tanya, Gate of Unity and Faith, chapter 1: https://steinsaltz-center.org/portal/library/Tanya/Shaar%20Hayichud%20Vehaemuna/chapter/1 (seen in a search result)
- Chabad Research, Gate of Unity and Faith, part 1: https://chabadresearch.net/library/project-lechaim/gate-of-unity-and-faith-part-1 (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, The Divine Oneness: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/84835/jewish/The-Divine-Oneness.htm (seen in a search result)
- Ansche Chesed, Nothing But You (an accessible explanation): https://anschechesed.org/nothing-but-you/ (seen in a search result)
- Naftali Loewenthal, Lower Unity, part 1 (Chabad Research): https://chabadresearch.net/library/nl-articles/lower-unity-part-1 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: The Kehot Publication Society bilingual Likkutei Amarim Tanya (all five parts in one volume), from memory. Adin Steinsaltz's guides to the Tanya (Jossey-Bass, 2003 to 2007) for a modern commentary; check which one covers Part II.

Background: The core argument is that the world is created from nothing by the divine word, and that word must keep flowing into creation or creation would stop. So in relation to God the world has no standing of its own. Chapter 7 reads a Zohar saying as two unities. In the higher unity only God exists. In the lower unity the world appears to exist but is nullified in God. The Chabad answer is that both are held at once: from God's side nothing but God, from the creature's side a real world in which we have work to do. In Chabad usage ayin (nothing) means the incomprehensible, not plain non-being. Scholars disagree about how much reality the world keeps. Rachel Elior reads Chabad as acosmism, meaning the world has no independent existence. Yoram Jacobson, writing in Hebrew, and Eli Rubin, a Chabad scholar with a UCL doctorate, argue that Shneur Zalman held the world to be real though wholly dependent on God. Compare the essay's machine: each process sees its walls, the processor sees one machine. The match is partial. The Tanya has a God who creates and commands, and its unity is lived inside the commandments. The essay is careful to call the analogy its own, and Alex should say so aloud too.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't a Creator God contradict 'we are one'? Yes, at the level of theology. The essay claims only a family resemblance in how one reality can appear as many, and it says the traditions differ about who or what you let go into.
- Isn't this acosmism? Some scholars say so and others say the Tanya affirms the world as real. Say 'a leading academic reading' and name the disagreement.

## Tanya, Likkutei Amarim, chapter 32 (love of a fellow Jew) (1796 (first printing of Part I; the CHECKLIST left the exact print date open))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The chapter of the Tanya's first part that treats ahavat Yisrael, love of a fellow Jew. Chabad commentary treats it as the emotional centre of the book.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers' the essay says Chabad's Tanya grounds love for a fellow Jew in the claim that all Jewish souls share one root in God. The table row 'What you do to another' repeats it ('Tanya: Jewish souls share one root').

Where it sits: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim (Part I), chapter 32.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The body is what separates people and the soul is what joins them. All Jews are called brothers because they are brothers at the root of their souls in God's oneness, and only their body-selves differ. Wording seen in a Chabad.org translation as summarised by the search tool: all Jews are brothers 'at the root of their souls in G-d's oneness', with only the body-selves differentiating them. I did not see the full page.

Read it free:
- Chabad.org, Tanya Chapter 32: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/42573/jewish/Tanya-Chapter-32.htm (seen in a search result)
- Steinsaltz Center, Tanya, Likkutei Amarim, chapter 32 (with commentary): https://steinsaltz-center.org/portal/library/Tanya/Likutei%20Amarim/chapter/32 (seen in a search result)
- Shulchan Aruch HaRav study, Tanya chapter 32: Ahavas Yisrael: https://shulchanaruchharav.com/?p=34997 (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, Love Your Fellow as Yourself (why souls are one at the source): https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/2312296/jewish/Chapter-1-The-Mitzvah.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: The Kehot bilingual Tanya, or Steinsaltz's Tanya guides (Jossey-Bass), from memory.

Background: Chapter 32 gives a direct route to the commandment of loving your fellow as yourself. If you hold your own body-self lightly, it stops being the deciding factor in how you see another Jew, and what is left is soul meeting soul. Every Jewish soul is a portion of God, so at the root they are one, and the chapter extends the love to the sinner and the non-observant (the title of one Chabad study of it says so). A Chabad.org commentary notes that 32 is the numerical value of lev, heart, a nice detail for a talk. A Steinsaltz Center summary ties the chapter to Hillel's 'the rest is commentary'. Two cautions matter. First, the scope is Jewish. I recall that in Part I, chapters 1 and 2, the Tanya says non-Jewish souls come from a lower source, and critics quote that; modern Chabad teaching, especially under Menachem Mendel Schneerson, stresses concern for all people, but this chapter is about Jews. The essay already says 'fellow Jew' and admits that most of these sources 'speak first of a community'. Second, this is love meant to be acted on, inside Jewish law. It is the closest Jewish text to the fingers picture, because it says what divides is the body-self. I only saw summaries of the chapter, not the whole of it.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't the Tanya say non-Jews have a lesser soul? In Part I chapters 1 and 2 as I recall it does, and I have not verified the wording. The essay does not claim universal reach for this text. It says 'fellow Jew' and names the limit.
- Is 'one root' in the chapter's own words? A Chabad magazine excerpt gives the idea as souls' source in the One God with only the bodies separated. The exact phrase 'one root' came from a Shulchan Aruch HaRav study, so say 'a shared source' when speaking.

## The Zohar: the Torah, the Holy One and Israel are one (Tradition: second century sage Shimon bar Yochai. Most academics: late thirteenth century Castile)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Zohar is the central book of Kabbalah, a mystical commentary on the Torah in a cryptic Aramaic. Orthodox tradition holds it comes from Shimon bar Yochai and his circle. Most academic scholars since Gershom Scholem place it in late thirteenth century Spain, with Moses de Leon as the main author.

In the essay: In 'The axiom and the machine': 'A saying in the Zohar has the Torah, the Holy One and Israel as one, which its commentators read as a union reached through the Torah and God's will.' Also the table row 'The one whole'.

Where it sits: Cited to Zohar III 73a in the Chabad pages the check saw (portion Acharei Mot, from memory). The Aramaic formula, as transliterated on a JTS page recorded in the CHECKLIST: 'Kudsha Brich Hu, Orayta, VeYisrael - Had Hu'. The CHECKLIST (C9-34) says to give the page number only after checking the Aramaic on Sefaria. A related Zohar line is cited as Zohar II 60a.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of the formula: the Holy One, blessed be He, the Torah and Israel are one. The wording varies between quotations, so I give the Aramaic transliteration only as the JTS page printed it.

Read it free:
- Kabbalah Online, The Tongue of the Learned (explains the line as union through God's will): https://www.kabbalaonline.org/kabbalah/article_cdo/aid/379498/jewish/The-Tongue-of-the-Learned.htm (seen in a search result)
- JTS, Torah: Vision Beyond the Text, part 2 (prints the Aramaic formula): https://www.jtsa.edu/torah/torah-vision-beyond-the-text-part-2/ (seen in a search result)
- Yeshiva.co, Ask the Rabbi (cites Zohar III 73a): https://www.yeshiva.co/ask/63065 (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, Rashbi: Basic Kabbalah Tenets (cites 73a for a different point): https://www.chabad.org/kabbalah/article_cdo/aid/758951/jewish/Rashbi-Basic-Kabbalah-Tenets.htm (seen in a search result)
- Daily Zohar, English Zohar on Acharei Mot (PDF; I did not check that the line is in it): https://www.dailyzohar.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Zohar-Achare-mot.pdf (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Isaiah Tishby, The Wisdom of the Zohar, 3 volumes (Littman Library, 1989, translated by David Goldstein), for passages by theme. Daniel C. Matt, The Zohar: Pritzker Edition (Stanford University Press, from 2004, with commentary), for the text. Both from memory. On sefaria.org, search for the Zohar and its commentaries; a Zohar commentary, Ohr HaChammah, was seen at https://www.sefaria.org/Ohr_HaChammah_on_Zohar.

Background: The line is the best known summary of a Zohar theme. Kabbalah Online, a Chabad site, explains it this way: Israel keeps the Torah, which expresses God's will, and so becomes one with that will and with him. The Nefesh HaChayim of Rabbi Chaim of Volozhin, which I saw only through a search summary, treats the equation as a recurring Zohar theme and says each Jewish soul root clings to a letter of the Torah. Three cautions. The page reference is not settled: Yeshiva.co puts the line at III 73a while Chabad's own Rashbi article cites 73a for a different point and gives the unity statement from another Zohar passage. The dating of the book itself is disputed between tradition and academics, so avoid saying 'the second century Zohar'. And the line is about Israel, the Jewish people, as Tishby reads it, so it does not say all beings are one, and it does not say a whole experiences separation. The essay's own wording, a union reached through the Torah and God's will, is the fair one, and the table places it only under 'The one whole'. Use it as a witness to a three-way unity in Kabbalistic thought and no more.

If someone pushes back:
- Does the Zohar teach that everything is one? Not in this line. It is about God, Torah and Israel. Other Kabbalistic passages say God fills all things, but I have not checked one here.
- Is it really from the second century? Tradition says so. Most academic scholars date it to thirteenth century Spain. The essay does not need the date.

## Zechariah 14:9 and the Zohar's reading (Hebrew Bible prophet; the book is dated by most scholars to the Persian period)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The last verse of the prophet's vision of the day of the LORD, recited at the end of the Aleinu prayer that closes every Jewish service.

In the essay: In the table, row 'The return', Judaism column: 'On that day the LORD will be one and his name one' (Zech 14:9, ESV).

Where it sits: Zechariah 14:9.

The passage: On that day the LORD will be one and his name one (ESV, as recorded in the CHECKLIST from a Biblehub parallel page). The KJV reads 'in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one'.

Read it free:
- Biblehub parallel translations of Zechariah 14:9: https://biblehub.com/parallel/zechariah/14-9.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sefaria, Zechariah 14:9 (standard Sefaria address pattern, not seen in a search result): https://www.sefaria.org/Zechariah.14.9 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Any study Bible with a commentary on Zechariah; the JPS Bible Commentary or the Hebrew Bible volume Nevi'im in the JPS Tanakh.

Background: Zechariah 14 is a vision of a last battle over Jerusalem after which the LORD becomes king over all the earth. Verse 9 ends it. The plain sense is universal recognition: on that day everyone will acknowledge the one God, which echoes the Shema, 'the LORD is one' (Deuteronomy 6:4). The Aleinu prayer ends with the verse, which is why Jews know it by heart. An earlier research round, not checked here, says the Zohar reads it as the unification of two aspects of God (Zeir Anpin and Malkhut), so that God and his name become one, a statement about the inside of the divine and not about all beings merging. Chabad readings speak of the revelation of God's unity in the world, from memory. So the table is right to place the verse under 'The return' in the sense of an end to division, but it needs care. The plain end is recognition and rule. The mystical end is unification within God. Neither one says that individual lives flow back into a whole. Translations differ, so quote a named one: ESV and NRSV say 'will be one and his name one', and the KJV says 'shall there be one LORD, and his name one'. I did not check the JPS rendering.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't that verse about God ruling the world, not unity? Yes, in its plain sense. The table uses it for 'the return' as an end to division, and the Kabbalistic reading adds an inner unification.
- Which translation? ESV, and say so.

## Lurianic Kabbalah and Isaac Luria (tzimtzum, the breaking of the vessels, tikkun) (Isaac Luria, 1534 to 1572; Hayyim Vital, 1542 to 1620 (from memory))

Kind: tradition. Status: partly checked.

Who: Isaac Luria, called the Ari, taught a circle of Kabbalists in Safed in the Galilee in the early 1570s. His student Hayyim Vital wrote down his system. Its three central symbols, in Scholem's phrase, are tzimtzum, the breaking of the vessels and tikkun.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real' the essay says the Kabbalists of sixteenth century Safed taught that the infinite contracted to make room for a world, that the vessels meant to hold its light shattered, and that gathering the pieces is humanity's work, 'a story of fragmentation and repair'. Also 'The same things in different words' ('the Lurianic Kabbalists with the shattered vessels and their repair') and the table (shattered vessels under 'The veil', tikkun under 'The return').

Where it sits: Essay: 'Why separation feels so real' (paragraph beginning 'Why would the whole do this at all?'). Sources: Hayyim Vital, Etz Chaim; Scholem, Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism, lecture 7, 'Isaac Luria and His School'.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. God withdrew into himself to leave an empty space (tzimtzum). Divine light poured into vessels that could not hold it, and they broke, scattering sparks into matter (shevirat ha-kelim). People repair this, mainly by prayer and by mitzvot that raise the sparks (tikkun).

Read it free:
- My Jewish Learning, Tikkun in Lurianic Kabbalah: https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/tikkun-in-lurianic-kabbalah/ (seen in a search result)
- Britannica, Isaac ben Solomon Luria (print version): https://www.britannica.com/print/article/351761 (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, Introduction to the Arizal's innovations (tzimtzum, shevirah, tikkun): https://www.chabad.org/kabbalah/article_cdo/aid/380320/jewish/Introduction.htm (seen in a search result)
- IMHU, Isaac Luria (Ari): https://imhu.org/people/isaac-luria-ari (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Gershom Scholem, Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism (Schocken; lectures given 1938, first published 1941; a 1995 Schocken edition edited by Robert Alter is listed in a library record), lecture 7. Lawrence Fine, Physician of the Soul, Healer of the Cosmos: Isaac Luria and His Kabbalistic Fellowship (Stanford University Press, 2003), from memory.

Background: Luria was born in Jerusalem, raised in Egypt, moved to Safed around 1570 and died of plague in 1572, aged about thirty-eight. Safed had a gathering of Kabbalists, many of them refugees from the Spanish expulsion of 1492, including Moses Cordovero and Joseph Karo. Luria wrote almost nothing himself, as a search result also noted, and Vital preserved his teaching in the Etz Chaim. Scholem read the myth as a story of exile and repair: God's own presence, the Shekhinah, is scattered, and human acts mend it. Three cautions. In Luria the breaking is a real catastrophe or a deliberate descent, depending on the reading, and not an illusion or a forgetting. Tikkun is done through commandments, so it sits inside Jewish law. And tzimtzum is contested: Chabad, following the Tanya, reads it non-literally, so that the world looks separate only from the creatures' side, while others read it literally. The essay's own thought, that a whole that wants to know itself needs a point of view, is the essay's reading and not Luria's stated motive. The checker's note that 'shattered vessels under the veil' is an interpretive step still holds.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't Luria say the separation is real and terrible, not an illusion? Yes. The essay says only 'fragmentation and repair' and offers its own reading of why. Concede the difference and move on.
- Is tikkun the same as awakening? No. It is repair through action, mostly commandments. It is a human task, not a sudden insight.

## Baal Shem Tov on Psalm 16:8 and equanimity (hishtavut) (Israel ben Eliezer, about 1700 to 1760 (sources give 1698 or 1700 for his birth))

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: The Baal Shem Tov ('master of the good name', the Besht) was a healer and preacher in Podolia who started the Hasidic movement. He wrote nothing himself, so all his teaching comes through students.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real': 'the Baal Shem Tov read a verse in Psalm 16 as teaching equanimity, so that praise and insult feel the same.' Table, row 'Letting go': 'Bittul, nullifying the self. Praise and insult feel the same.'

Where it sits: Psalm 16:8, 'Shiviti Adonai l'negdi tamid'. The teaching is recorded in Tzava'at HaRivash (Kehot edition, p. 1 according to the Sichos in English page).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. 'Shiviti' ('I have set') is read as related to shaveh, equal, so setting God before you means equanimity, hishtavut: praise and shame, comfort and trouble all feel the same. The Hebrew words of the verse were seen in a search summary.

Read it free:
- Chabad.org, 'Shivisi' (Tehillim 16:8): Equanimity: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/2606250/jewish/Equanimity.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sichos In English, Chassidic Remedies, chapter 37 (cites Tzava'at HaRivash): https://sichos-in-english.org/books/chassidic-remedies/037.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sefaria, Tzava'at HaRivash (the book): https://www.sefaria.org/Tzava'at_HaRivash (seen in a search result)
- Chabad.org, Tzavaat Harivash Introduction: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/145201/jewish/Tzavaat-Harivash-Introduction.htm (seen in a search result)
- Sefaria, Psalms 16:8 (standard address pattern, not seen in a search result): https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.16.8 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Tzava'at HaRivash: The Testament of Rabbi Israel Baal Shem Tov, Kehot, translated by Jacob Immanuel Schochet (from memory, check the year). Immanuel Etkes, The Besht: Magician, Mystic, and Leader (Brandeis University Press, 2005), for the historical figure, from memory.

Background: The pun works because shiviti comes from a root meaning to set or place, and the Hebrew for 'equal', shaveh, is built on the same letters. The Besht is reported to have taught that if you have really set God before you, honour and humiliation feel the same, because you are not living on the audience's reaction. The record is late and indirect. Tzava'at HaRivash is an anthology of teachings of the Besht and his successor, the Maggid Dov Ber of Mezritch, compiled by disciples. Wikipedia says the Besht did not write it, and Sefaria lists Yeshaya of Yanov as the compiler, dating it to about 1730 to 1770, so say 'is reported to have taught' as the essay does. One modern speaker, Rabbi Nancy Flam, on a Sefaria Voices page, says equanimity is not a mind that fails to notice the difference between praise and blame but one that does not react through craving or aversion. That is a modern gloss and not the Besht, but it fits the essay's 'released, not relaxed'. The equanimity in the text is grounded in God's presence, so it is a consequence of devotion and not a technique. Hasidic sources also expect it to show up in action. A medieval source for the same idea, Bahya ibn Paquda's Duties of the Heart, was suggested in the CHECKLIST for the praise-and-insult line, unchecked.

If someone pushes back:
- Did the Baal Shem Tov actually say this? He is reported to, in Tzava'at HaRivash, a book compiled by disciples. The attribution rests on those records.
- Isn't this just indifference? The teachers I saw say not. It is not failing to notice the difference but not being ruled by it.

## Baal Shem Tov on the hiding of God's face (hester panim) (Israel ben Eliezer, about 1700 to 1760; reported by Yaakov Yosef of Polnoye, died 1783)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: The same Baal Shem Tov. This teaching comes to us through his disciple Rabbi Yaakov Yosef of Polnoye, whose Toldot Yaakov Yosef (first printed 1780, from memory) is the first Hasidic book.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real': 'In the Hasidic tradition the Baal Shem Tov is reported, through a disciple, to have taught that the hiding of God's face is only apparent, a test of how we respond.' Also the table, row 'The veil': 'God's hidden face, said by the Baal Shem Tov to be only apparent.'

Where it sits: Toldot Yaakov Yosef, a teaching on Deuteronomy 31:17 to 18 ('I will hide my face', haster astir panai), as reported on Kabbalah Online and in a Yeshivat Maharat PDF cited in the CHECKLIST.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The hiding is only apparent. The doubled 'haster astir' (verse 18) was read as a hiding within a hiding, a concealment so complete that we no longer notice we are not seeing. Whether the hiding is 'a test of how we respond' is not shown in the pages I saw.

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Read it free:
- Tanya, Gate of Unity and Faith (the home of the idea of nullification), Chabad Research: https://chabadresearch.net/library/project-lechaim/gate-of-unity-and-faith-part-1 (seen in a search result)

Background: The Maggid Dov Ber of Mezritch, the Baal Shem Tov's successor, taught that a person should become ayin, nothing, so that the divine can flow through him. Three things to keep in your pocket. First, bittul is nullification before God's will, and it sits inside the commandments. Hasidism does not ask people to stop acting. It asks them to act without ego, and Chabad uses bittul to fuel work in the world. The CHECKLIST (C3-28) warns that the essay's 'letting go' framing should not imply dropping action. Third, scholars disagree about how quietist it is. As I recall, Schatz-Uffenheimer argued that early Hasidic thought has strong quietist, mystical elements in which the self disappears, while other writers stress its practical, social side. The table cell adds 'praise and insult feel the same' with no attribution; that line comes from the Baal Shem Tov entry above.

If someone pushes back:
- Is bittul the same as letting go of tension? They rhyme, but bittul is before God and inside the law. The essay says the traditions differ about who or what you let go into.
- Isn't self-nullification unhealthy? Hasidic teachers insist it is not self-hatred, and I recall this from reading rather than from a source I checked. Say that you are citing the concept and not recommending the practice.
