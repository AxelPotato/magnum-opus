## Paul, 1 Corinthians 12:12-27 (one body, many members) (1 Corinthians written about AD 53-55)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Paul, the first-century Jewish apostle, writing to the church at Corinth. Scholars accept 1 Corinthians as authentic. In chapter 12 he uses one body with many members to settle a quarrel about spiritual gifts and status.

In the essay: Karma, or the fingers: Paul gave the church a body with many members and said that if one member suffers, all suffer together (12:26). Also the table, Christianity column, rows 'The one whole' (one body in Christ) and 'What you do to another' (12:26).

Where it sits: 1 Corinthians 12:12-27, especially 12:12-13 (one body, baptized by one Spirit) and 12:26 (Greek sympaschei, 'suffers together')

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Paul says the body is one though it has many members, and so it is with Christ. All were baptized into one body in one Spirit. The eye cannot say to the hand 'I don't need you'. If one member suffers, all suffer together, and if one is honored, all rejoice together. In ESV and RSV 12:26 is usually given as 'if one member suffers, all suffer together'; I did not see that wording on a page, so treat it as unconfirmed.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, 1 Corinthians 12:26 (version selectable): https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/1cor+12:26 (seen in a search result)
- Bible Gateway, 1 Corinthians 12:12-27 in the ESV: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+12%3A12-27&version=ESV (not checked)

Read it in a book: Read it in the NRSV with notes (HarperCollins Study Bible or New Oxford Annotated Bible). Commentaries: Anthony Thiselton, The First Epistle to the Corinthians (NIGTC, Eerdmans, 2000); Gordon Fee, The First Epistle to the Corinthians (NICNT, revised ed., Eerdmans, 2014); Richard Hays, First Corinthians (Interpretation, John Knox, 1997). For the body image in its ancient setting: Dale B. Martin, The Corinthian Body (Yale, 1995).

Background: Paul wrote to a church split over status, mainly over who had the more impressive spiritual gifts. The body was a stock image in the ancient world for a city or state whose citizens depend on one another. Paul puts it to work for a congregation and adds a claim of his own: this body is Christ's (12:12, 12:27). The Greek verb in 12:26 means to suffer with, and the sentence both describes and instructs. The plain scope is the church, not humanity. Augustine later turned the idea into the 'whole Christ', head and body together, and Catholic theology developed the 'mystical body' from it. Specialists, with Dale Martin's book as the usual reference (I recall it, I did not check it), point out that Paul also uses the body to defend an order within the community, so the passage has a harder edge than the warm reading suggests. For your discussions: the passage supports 'harm to one is harm to the whole' inside one community, and it keeps real differences between members. It does not say everyone is one. The essay already admits that most of its parallels speak first of a community. It is still a close fit for the fingers picture.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Paul only means the church.' Yes, that is the plain sense, and the essay says most of these parallels speak first of a community. I am borrowing the shape of the idea, and I am not saying Paul taught that all people are one body.
- 'The body image is just Greek political thought.' It was a common image and Paul adapts it. The claim that the body is Christ's is his own.

## Paul, 1 Corinthians 15:28 (God all in all) (1 Corinthians written about AD 53-55)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Paul's argument for the resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15, which ends its sequence of Christ's reign with the Son handed over to the Father. The verse is the key text for the restoration of all things in Origen and Gregory of Nyssa.

In the essay: The axiom and the machine: 'Paul writes that at the end Christ hands the kingdom to the Father so that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28), and Origen and Gregory of Nyssa read that as the restoration of all things.' Also the table: The one whole (Christianity) and The return (Christianity).

Where it sits: 1 Corinthians 15:24-28; Greek hina he ho theos panta en pasin

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Christ must reign until every enemy is put under his feet, death last of all. When everything has been subjected to him, the Son himself will be subjected to the one who subjected all things, so that God may be all in all. The phrase 'that God may be all in all' is attested in the check as the standard English rendering; I did not see a full English verse on a page.

Read it free:
- Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies, essay on Origen, Eusebius and apokatastasis (ties 'all in all' to 1 Cor 15:28): https://chs.harvard.edu/?p=228875 (seen in a search result)
- Catholic Encyclopedia, 'Apocatastasis' (New Advent): https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01599a.htm (seen in a search result)
- Bible Gateway, 1 Corinthians 15:28: https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/1cor+15:28 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Commentaries: Gordon Fee, NICNT (rev. ed., Eerdmans, 2014); Anthony Thiselton, NIGTC (Eerdmans, 2000). On the restoration reading: Ilaria Ramelli, The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis (Brill, 2013). Gregory's treatise on the verse, In illud: Tunc et ipse Filius, is in the Greek edition of his works (Gregorii Nysseni Opera, vol. III.2); I did not find a free English text.

Background: Paul is arguing for the resurrection. Christ, the last Adam, reigns until every enemy is under his feet, death last of all (15:24-26). Then the Son hands the kingdom to the Father and is himself subjected, so that God may be all in all (15:28). The Greek panta en pasin can mean everything in everyone, everything in all things, or all things among all persons, and the sentence is a purpose clause (hina) at the end of a sequence. Many commentators read it as the end of Christ's mediating reign, the point where God's rule is complete. Origen set it beside Acts 3:21, 'the restoration of all things', and read it as the final restoration of all rational creatures (On First Principles 2.3.4-5, per the Harvard CHS essay). Gregory of Nyssa wrote a treatise on the verse, arguing that the Son's subjection is humanity, Christ's body, brought fully into God. Augustine read the subjection as that of Christ's assumed human nature, and the checklist records that other Fathers read it differently. This is the best Christian line for the third clause of the axiom, and one of the most argued over. Some modern theologians use it for panentheism; others reject that.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Paul doesn't say everyone is saved here.' Correct. The verse is about the end of Christ's reign. The essay credits the restoration reading only to Origen and Gregory of Nyssa.
- 'All in all is about God's rule, not about merging.' That is a respectable reading. I use the verse as a Christian line near the third clause, in other words, and not as proof.

## Paul, Galatians 6:7 (whatever a person sows) (Galatians written between the late 40s and mid 50s AD (date debated))

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Paul writing to the churches of Galatia, an authentic letter. The sentence closes a short section on bearing one another's burdens and not growing weary of doing good.

In the essay: Karma, or the fingers: 'Paul writes that whatever a person sows, that will he also reap, which is accountability under God, with no rebirth in it, though the shape is the same.' Sources list: Galatians 6:7.

Where it sits: Galatians 6:7 (context 6:1-10)

The passage: ESV (wording recorded in the check): 'whatever one sows, that will he also reap.' Another form seen on a study site, translation not named: 'whatever a man sows, that he will also reap.' Paul adds in 6:8 that the one who sows to the flesh reaps corruption and the one who sows to the Spirit reaps eternal life, and in 6:9 that we should not grow weary of doing good.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, Galatians 6:7: https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/gal+6:7 (seen in a search result)
- Enduring Word, commentary on sowing and reaping (Galatians 6): https://enduringword.com/sowing-and-reaping/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: J. Louis Martyn, Galatians (Anchor Bible 33A, Doubleday, 1997); Richard Longenecker, Galatians (Word Biblical Commentary 41, Word, 1990); Hans Dieter Betz, Galatians (Hermeneia, Fortress, 1979). Read the whole of chapter 6 in the NRSV.

Background: The sowing and reaping image is old wisdom language. Job 4:8, Proverbs 22:8 and Hosea 10:12-13 use it for the way justice and injustice come back on the doer (from memory, not checked), and Paul reuses it as a rule of accountability under God. The immediate context is practical: 6:6 asks the taught to share good things with the teacher, and 6:9-10 urges doing good to everyone. The larger frame is his contrast between living by the flesh and living by the Spirit. There is no rebirth in it. A specialist will ask how this sits with the central claim of the same letter, that a person is put right with God by faith and not by works (2:16). Interpreters usually say Paul separates how a person comes into right standing (grace) from how a life bears fruit (consequence), and some read 6:7 as aimed at believers tempted to treat grace as licence. The tension is real, and Martyn and others discuss it. For the essay the verse witnesses to the shape 'what you do comes back to you', with God and not an impersonal law as the guarantor. Compare the Buddhist account, where nobody administers karma, and the Hindu theistic schools, where God has a role.

If someone pushes back:
- 'That is judgment, not karma.' Yes. The essay says so: accountability under God, no rebirth, same shape.
- 'Doesn't Paul teach grace, not reaping?' He teaches both, and scholars discuss how they fit. I use the sentence for the shape of the idea, and I am not making a point about Paul's theology of salvation.

## Ephesians 4:18 (alienated from the life of God through ignorance) (Ephesians dated between about AD 60 and 90, authorship debated)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A letter in Paul's name to churches around Ephesus. Many New Testament scholars see it as written by a follower of Paul around AD 80-90; others hold that Paul wrote it from prison around AD 60-62. The question is open.

In the essay: Why separation feels so real: 'The letter to the Ephesians says that people are alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them (4:18).' Also the table, row 'The veil' (Christianity), which cites Eph 4:18, ESV.

Where it sits: Ephesians 4:17-24, especially 4:18 (Greek agnoia, 'ignorance')

The passage: ESV, wording recorded in the check from the Bible Hub and Bible Gateway pages: 'alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them'. KJV: 'alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them'. Verse 4:18 goes on to say their understanding is darkened and that the ignorance is due to hardness of heart.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, Ephesians 4:18: https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/eph+4:18 (seen in a search result)
- Bible Hub, Ephesians 4:18 (KJV and parallel translations): https://biblehub.com/kjv/ephesians/4-18.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Andrew Lincoln, Ephesians (Word Biblical Commentary 42, Word, 1990); Ernest Best, Ephesians (International Critical Commentary, T&T Clark, 1998); Markus Barth, Ephesians (Anchor Bible 34-34A, Doubleday, 1974). Each also discusses authorship.

Background: In 4:17-24 the writer tells Gentile readers to stop living as their former neighbours do. Their thinking is futile, their understanding darkened, and they are 'alienated from the life of God' because of the ignorance (agnoia) in them, which the writer ties to hardness of heart. 'Alienated' is the same word used in 2:12 of Gentiles 'alienated from the commonwealth of Israel', so the original sense is social and religious: separated from the people and the life of God. The remedy follows in 4:20-24: put off the old self and put on the new self, 'created after the likeness of God'. That is the useful part for your purposes, because it treats the old self as something that can be taken off. The cause named is moral as much as intellectual, and the letter does not say that humans have forgotten who they are. Specialists would say the verse is about pagan life as the author saw it, and that using it for a universal veil is the essay's step. Because of the authorship question, the essay is right to say 'the letter to the Ephesians' and not 'Paul'.

If someone pushes back:
- 'That verse is about pagans.' In context, yes. The essay uses it as a Christian statement that ignorance is what separates a person from the life of God, and I am not claiming Paul teaches forgotten identity.
- 'Paul didn't write Ephesians.' Many scholars agree, and the essay's wording ('the letter to the Ephesians') allows for that.

## Philippians 2:5-8 (he emptied himself) (Philippians written about AD 55-62 (place and date debated))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Paul writing to the church at Philippi, probably from prison. Philippians 2:6-11 is often called the Christ hymn, and many scholars think Paul is quoting an earlier hymn.

In the essay: Why separation feels so real: 'Christians have kenosis, the Greek word in Philippians for emptying oneself.' Table, row 'Letting go' (Christianity): 'Kenosis, he emptied himself.' Sources list: Philippians 2:5-8.

Where it sits: Philippians 2:5-8 (the verb ekenosen is in 2:7; the hymn runs 2:6-11)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Paul tells the Philippians to have the same mind among themselves that was in Christ Jesus, who, though in the form of God, did not count equality with God something to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, and humbled himself to the point of death on a cross. Translations of 2:7 differ: 'emptied himself' (ESV, RSV), 'made himself of no reputation' (KJV), 'made himself nothing' (NIV), as I recall; not seen on a page in this check.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, Philippians 2:5-11 in the ESV: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A5-11&version=ESV (not checked)
- Trinitarian Bible Society, note on the translation of 'emptied himself' in Philippians 2:7: https://www.tbsbibles.org/page/Philippians2v7 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Gordon Fee, Paul's Letter to the Philippians (NICNT, Eerdmans, 1995); Michael Gorman, Inhabiting the Cruciform God (Eerdmans, 2009), on self-giving as the shape of Paul's theology. For the early interpretation, the Notre Dame dissertation by Michael Magree on Philippians 2:7 from Origen to Cyril of Alexandria is online (see the kenosis entry).

Background: Paul introduces the hymn with an appeal to unity and humility: have this mind among yourselves (2:5). The point is ethical first: the one with every claim to status gives it up for others. Three debates matter. First, harpagmos (2:6), 'something to be grasped' or 'exploited': did Christ refuse to cling to equality with God, or refuse to use it for his own advantage? Second, what he emptied in 2:7: glory, divine attributes (the nineteenth century kenotic theories), or nothing literal, with the verb as a figure for self-giving (Gordon Fee takes it metaphorically). Third, whether 2:6-8 is a contrast with Adam, who reached for divinity; Calvin read it so, and James Dunn has argued the same (I recall this; not checked). Paul does not turn it into a meditation method. The emptying is Christ's, and the readers are asked to imitate its attitude. The step to a general practice of letting go belongs to later spiritual writers. The essay's phrase 'the Greek word in Philippians for emptying oneself' is looser than the text, which has Christ emptying himself, and the checklist suggests wording that says so. As a witness to letting go the passage is strong in spirit and plain in context: self-emptying for the sake of others, ending in death on a cross, followed by exaltation.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Kenosis is about Christ, not about you.' In the text, yes. Later Christian writers took it as a pattern for the believer, and the essay says Christians 'have kenosis', which is fair if worded as a pattern the tradition draws from the verse.
- 'Does this mean Christ gave up being God?' That question divided the Fathers and the modern kenotic theories, and I would not take a side in an essay about letting go.

## Kenosis (the idea of self-emptying) (From Philippians 2:7 (1st century); kenotic theories 19th century; Bourgeault 2008)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: The noun built from the verb ekenosen in Philippians 2:7. It became a technical term in theology about how the Son became human, and later a popular name for the Christian practice of letting go.

In the essay: Why separation feels so real, on the method of letting go: 'Christians have kenosis.' Table, row 'Letting go' (Christianity): 'Kenosis, he emptied himself. Eckhart's letting go. The stillness of the hesychasts.'

Where it sits: Philippians 2:7; later uses in Cyril of Alexandria, the nineteenth century kenotic theologians, and Cynthia Bourgeault, The Wisdom Jesus (2008)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of Bourgeault as relayed by the Center for Action and Contemplation: the verb kenosein means to let go or to empty oneself, Paul chooses it at the key moment to describe the mind of Christ, and she reads Jesus as having one operational mode, self-emptying. This is her reading, and I did not see it in her book.

Read it free:
- Center for Action and Contemplation, daily meditation on kenosis as letting go (Bourgeault): https://cac.org/kenosis-letting-go-2018-12-21/ (seen in a search result)
- Michael Magree, Notre Dame thesis: 'Shaped to the Measure of the Kenosis', Philippians 2:7 from Origen to Cyril of Alexandria: https://curate.nd.edu/show/s4655d90090 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Cynthia Bourgeault, The Wisdom Jesus (Shambhala, 2008); Michael Gorman, Inhabiting the Cruciform God (Eerdmans, 2009); Evans (ed.), Exploring Kenotic Christology (OUP, 2006) for the theology of the term, which I recall but did not check. Search results also name a recent volume of seventeen scholars on the doctrine of kenosis, edited by Nimmo and Johnson.

Background: Paul does not use the noun, only the verb. The Fathers argued over who the subject of the emptying is: Cyril of Alexandria held that it is the Word, who makes human nature his own (Magree's dissertation). In the nineteenth century 'kenotic' theories (Gottfried Thomasius in Germany, Charles Gore in England, as I recall) asked what the Son gave up to become human, and critics said they made Christ less than God. Using kenosis as a general rule of the spiritual life is a modern development, popular through Cynthia Bourgeault and Richard Rohr's Center for Action and Contemplation. Most New Testament scholars keep the term for the self-giving of Christ and for the community's imitation of it, with Gorman as an example. The older Christian vocabulary for human letting go is different: Matthew 16:25 (lose your life to find it), Gethsemane (Luke 22:42), the monastic apatheia of Evagrius, Eckhart's gelazenheit and John of the Cross's nada. In conversation, kenosis is the safe word for Christ's self-emptying and a modern, contested word for yours. That fits the essay's own caution, and nothing more is claimed.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Kenosis isn't about human letting go.' In Philippians it is not. Bourgeault and others extend it. I am quoting the word as a name for the pattern, not as Paul's method.
- 'Isn't Bourgeault popular theology?' Yes, and contested by some New Testament scholars. That is why the essay pairs her kind of reading with Eckhart and the hesychasts, which are older sources.

## Acts 9:4 (Saul, why do you persecute me?) (Acts written about AD 80-90 by the conventional dating; the conversion about AD 33-36)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Acts of the Apostles, the second volume of Luke's work. It tells the conversion of Saul (Paul) on the road to Damascus three times: Acts 9, 22 and 26.

In the essay: Table, row 'What you do to another' (Christianity): '"Saul, why do you persecute me?"'. Sources list: Acts 9:4.

Where it sits: Acts 9:4 (parallels 22:7 and 26:14)

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: on the road to Damascus the risen Jesus speaks to Saul, who has been persecuting the church, and asks, 'Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?' The usual English wording in ESV, NIV and KJV is 'Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?'; I did not see a page confirming it in this check.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, Acts 9:4: https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/acts+9:4 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Joseph Fitzmyer, The Acts of the Apostles (Anchor Bible 31, Doubleday, 1998); C. K. Barrett, Acts (International Critical Commentary, 2 vols, T&T Clark, 1994-98). For the idea that grew from it: Augustine, Enarrationes in Psalmos, on the 'whole Christ' (search for 'totus Christus').

Background: Luke tells the story three times with differences in detail, such as whether Saul's companions heard the voice or saw the light. In each, the risen Jesus asks why Saul persecutes him. Saul was persecuting the Jerusalem church (8:3), so the question treats the community as bound to Jesus: what is done to them is done to him. In 9:5 Saul asks who is speaking and gets the answer 'I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.' Augustine read it as the head speaking for his members, which became the 'whole Christ' idea. Paul's own letters add to it. In Galatians 1:15-16 he says God was pleased to reveal his Son 'to me' (the Greek preposition can also be read 'in me'), and in 2:20 he writes 'it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me'. Mystics often join these with Acts 9 (from memory; check the footnotes in your Bible). Mainstream scholarship stays with solidarity between Christ and his followers. It does not read the verse as a claim about humanity at large. For the essay it is the shortest New Testament example of 'what you do to another, you do to me'. Matthew 25:40 is the nearest parallel.

If someone pushes back:
- 'It means the church, not everyone.' Yes. The essay says most of these examples speak first of a community.
- 'Christ is a different person from Saul, so there is no oneness.' Correct. The text gives solidarity and identification. The axiom takes the same shape one step further.

## John 3:3 and 3:7 (born again, or born from above) (The Gospel of John, about AD 90-100)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The Fourth Gospel, whose author is traditionally John and is debated by scholars. Jesus speaks at night with Nicodemus, a Pharisee and a member of the Jewish council.

In the essay: The arrow: 'In John's gospel Jesus says that one must be born again, or born from above, since the Greek word can mean either.' Sources list: John 3:3 and 3:7.

Where it sits: John 3:3, 3:7 (Greek anothen); the conversation runs 3:1-21

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Jesus tells Nicodemus that unless someone is born anothen, they cannot see the kingdom of God (3:3), and repeats 'you must be born anothen' (3:7). Anothen means both 'from above' and 'again'. Nicodemus hears 'a second time' and asks how an old man can re-enter his mother's womb (3:4). Jesus answers about being born of water and the Spirit (3:5). The NRSV prints 'born from above' with a footnote giving 'born again', per the check.

Read it free:
- Reformation Study Bible note on John 3:3 (both meanings may be intended): https://www.biblegateway.com/resources/reformation-study-bible/John.3.3 (seen in a search result)
- Bible Hub, Greek anothen (Strong's 509): https://biblehub.com/strongs/new/greek/509.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Raymond Brown, The Gospel According to John I-XII (Anchor Bible 29, Doubleday, 1966); Craig Keener, The Gospel of John: A Commentary (2 vols, Hendrickson, 2003). Read it in the NRSV, which keeps the footnote.

Background: John builds this Gospel on double words, literal misunderstandings and deeper explanations; compare the 'living water' of chapter 4. Anothen is the first of these. Nicodemus hears 'again', Jesus means at least 'from above', and John probably wants both. Raymond Brown argued in the Anchor commentary that the double meaning is deliberate (I recall this, not checked). Jesus then explains the birth as of water and Spirit (3:5). The churches have mostly read that as baptism and new life in Christ; evangelical Protestantism reads it as conversion, which is where 'born again' as a label comes from. So the second birth in John is a beginning, an entry into a new life, and not a late stage reached after a long climb. The essay says what is needed: the traditions use the image for different things, and putting it after the top of a scale is the school's own extension. Eckhart's birth of God in the soul is a different image again (see his entry), and the Hindu twice-born are a different case altogether.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Born again just means conversion.' That is one very common reading. The essay doesn't dispute it; it notes that the Greek word has two senses and that the traditions use the second-birth image for different things.
- 'Your school's birth is not in John.' Agreed, and the essay says the use of the image for the stage after plus four is the school's own extension of a shared image.

## John 17:20-23 (that they may all be one) (The Gospel of John, about AD 90-100)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Jesus' long prayer at the end of the farewell discourse in the Fourth Gospel, on the night before his arrest. Verses 20-23 pray for those who will believe through the disciples' word.

In the essay: Sources list only (the section 'The traditions' inner layers'): John 17:20-23. It stands behind the table's 'One body in Christ' and 'The mystics speak of union with God', and the essay's remark that the Christian version is gentler.

Where it sits: John 17:20-23, especially 17:21 and 17:22 ('that they may all be one'; 'the glory that you have given me I have given to them')

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Jesus prays that all who believe may be one, as the Father is in him and he is in the Father, that they may be in us, so that the world may believe. The checklist records the phrase 'that they all may be one' for 17:21; I did not see a full English verse on a page.

Read it free:
- Bible Gateway, John 17:20-23 in the ESV: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+17%3A20-23&version=ESV (not checked)

Read it in a book: Raymond Brown, The Gospel According to John XIII-XXI (Anchor Bible 29A, Doubleday, 1970); Craig Keener, The Gospel of John (Hendrickson, 2003). Augustine, Tractates on the Gospel of John, the ones on chapter 17 (NPNF series 1, vol. 7, free on New Advent and CCEL; search by title).

Background: The prayer comes at the end of the farewell discourse. In 17:20-23 Jesus prays for later believers, asks that they be one as he and the Father are one, and gives as the purpose that the world may believe. The Greek 'one' (hen) is neuter, which points to oneness of kind or union and not to one person. Scholars split on what is meant. One line, strong since Bultmann, reads it as the unity of believers, in agreement and mission; the verse is the motto of the ecumenical movement and the source of the title of John Paul II's 1995 encyclical Ut unum sint. The other line reads it as a real sharing in the life of Father and Son, which Eastern theology takes up as theosis, and 17:22 supports it by saying the glory given to Jesus is given to them. Applying to creatures the Trinitarian language of mutual indwelling (perichoresis) is itself disputed, since it can blur Creator and creature. So this is the New Testament text closest to 'we are one', and also the one that specialists press hardest with the objection that the oneness is communion and not identity. Augustine's tractates discuss how believers are one 'as' the Father and the Son are one without being the same.

If someone pushes back:
- 'This is about church unity.' It is one major reading. The other, a participation reading, is also old. The essay only lists it, and doesn't lean on it.
- 'That would mean Christians are God.' Christian theology says no: it speaks of participation and communion. The essay should say 'union', not 'identity'.

## Origen of Alexandria (about 185 to about 254)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: A Christian teacher in Alexandria and Caesarea, and the most productive scholar of the early church. His works include the Hexapla, hundreds of homilies, Contra Celsum and On First Principles. He was tortured under the emperor Decius and died not long after.

Do not call him a reincarnation teacher.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Wasn't Origen condemned?' A later Origenism was condemned in the sixth century, and his name appears in the council's canons. The essay says only what the condemnations targeted: pre-existence and the final restoration.
- 'Origen taught reincarnation.' The check found his own statements ruling out transmigration. The charge comes from a long-repeated misreading, and the essay says he rejected it.

## Gregory of Nyssa (apokatastasis, epektasis, the Life of Moses) (about 335 to about 395)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Bishop of Nyssa, younger brother of Basil of Caesarea and with Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus one of the three Cappadocian Fathers. He is venerated as a saint in the East and the West, and was never condemned, even though he taught the restoration of all.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: in the Life of Moses, Gregory reads Moses' climb as a journey that never ends, since God is infinite; the soul that has seen is always drawn further, and the true vision of God is a desire that is satisfied by going on. Background: Gregory's two ideas in the essay are separate. Scholars differ on whether he asserts universal salvation or only hopes for it. Ilaria Ramelli argues for assertion, and Morwenna Ludlow finds more ambiguity (both from memory). The second is epektasis: in the Life of Moses the soul's ascent has no end, because God is infinite, so growth never stops, even after death. The noun is probably a scholarly label, drawn from Paul's verb in Philippians 3:13, and Daniélou is the scholar who made it central. Specialists would stress that for Gregory the distance between the infinite and the finite never closes, so epektasis is a union that keeps a gap. That does not fit a clause saying the separation ends, and a fair talk says so. The essay uses it only for the point that growth continues after death, and that is true to him.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Epektasis means you never arrive, which is the opposite of a return.' Fair. The essay uses it only to show that development continues after death, and says the traditions differ about the rest.
- 'Gregory only hoped for restoration.' Scholars disagree. The essay says he and Origen read the verse as the restoration of all things, and does not claim more.

Background: The sixth century Origenist controversy began among monks in Palestine, who quarrelled over Evagrius and Origen. In modern times Catholic and Orthodox writers (Balthasar, Kallistos Ware) argue that one may hope for the salvation of all, though not assert it. For talk, the essay's line is safe: the condemnations went after pre-existence and the restoration of all, not reincarnation as such.

If someone pushes back:
- 'The Church banned reincarnation in 553.' No text I saw says so. The anathemas concern pre-existence and restoration. The story that Justinian removed it from the Bible is unsupported.
- 'The anathemas are an official council act.' Their status is disputed. The essay says 'condemnations of Origenism', which is accurate whichever way that is settled.

Background: Hebrews 9:27 sits in a comparison: as people die once and then face judgment, so Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many (9:28). It is a statement about Christ's once-for-all sacrifice that Christian tradition took as ruling out repeated earthly lives. The creeds teach the resurrection of the body, and the Catechism (1013) says plainly that there is no reincarnation. Early Christian history is less simple than the later doctrine. Origen rejected transmigration but wrote of souls and ages. Reports in Clement and Origen say Basilides held views some scholars read as rebirth (disputed, and the reports come from opponents). The Manichaeans taught transmigration, but they were a separate religion. The Gospels present John the Baptist as the Elijah who was expected (Matthew 11:14, 17:12-13), yet in John 1:21 he says 'I am not'; most scholars read the first as fulfilment of a role. John 9:2 shows disciples asking if a man's sin or his parents' caused his blindness, which implies sin before birth or in the parents, and Jesus rejects both answers. None of this makes a doctrine. The essay is right to say 'mostly settled' and 'not without argument'.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Early Christians believed in reincarnation until Justinian.' No evidence supports this. See the councils entry.
- 'Isn't the one life a late doctrine?' The creeds and Hebrews 9:27 are early, but the argument over pre-existence and restoration ran into the sixth century. The essay mentions it.
