## Ralph Waldo Emerson, The Over-Soul (1841) (Emerson 1803 to 1882; essay published 1841)

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: American essayist and lecturer, a Unitarian minister who left the pulpit in 1832 and became the central voice of New England Transcendentalism. The Over-Soul is the ninth essay in his first collection, Essays: First Series (Boston, 1841).

In the essay: In 'The axiom and the machine', as the first of two witnesses 'with no creed to defend': Emerson says that we live in succession and in parts while the soul of the whole is within us. It is offered as plain-English wording of clauses one and two of the axiom, not as proof.

Where it sits: Essays: First Series (1841), essay IX, The Over-Soul. The sentence comes early in the essay (I believe the second paragraph; check). The sentence after it says that seer and spectacle, subject and object, are one.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Exact, confirmed in a search this session to appear in the essay (Gutenberg and Standard Ebooks reprints): 'We live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles.' The continuation is from memory and the research notes, with the punctuation before the last phrase unchecked: 'Meantime within man is the soul of the whole; the wise silence; the universal beauty, to which every part and particle is equally related; the eternal ONE.' Public domain, so Alex can quote it in full once he has checked it against the Gutenberg text.

Read it free:
- Project Gutenberg, Essays: First Series (The Over-Soul is the ninth essay): https://www.gutenberg.org/etext/2944 (seen in a search result)
- Standard Ebooks, The Over-Soul (clean single-page text): https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/ralph-waldo-emerson/essays/text/the-over-soul (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, The Over-Soul (summary and links to Wikisource): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Over-Soul (seen in a search result)
- Reprint of the essay on a university page (Harvey, FIU): https://faculty.fiu.edu/~harveyb/EmersonOversoul.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Penguin Classics, Selected Essays, ed. Larzer Ziff (1982), or the Library of America, Essays and Lectures (1983). The scholarly text is The Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, vol. 2, Essays: First Series (Harvard University Press, 1979). For his life and reading: Robert D. Richardson, Emerson: The Mind on Fire (University of California Press, 1995). Editions and years are from memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Emerson left the Unitarian ministry in 1832 over the Lord's Supper, and his Divinity School Address of 1838 shocked Boston by denying that Jesus was a unique incarnation and by telling people to look inside for what churches hand out secondhand. The Over-Soul is his plainest statement of what he put in its place: one spiritual reality that every person is part of and can feel directly, in quiet, more than they can argue about it. The word is his own coinage, built from his reading of Plato and Plotinus (through Thomas Taylor's translations), Swedenborg, the Quaker idea of the inner light and early English versions of the Upanishads and the Gita. Arthur Christy's The Orient in American Transcendentalism (1932) is the classic study of the Indian part; this comes from the research notes and from memory, not from a source seen this session. After the sentence the essay quotes, he moves to the claim that the seer and the thing seen are one. He does not teach lives, return or karma in this essay. So he is a precedent and not a proof: an educated person with no creed to defend who reached clauses one and two in his own words. Theosophy later used the word Over-Soul, but the essay is from 1841 and the Theosophical Society dates from 1875, so Emerson belongs to neither movement.

If someone pushes back:
- He is a poet and a preacher, not an argument. Answer: agreed. He is quoted to show that the sentence can be said in plain English by someone outside any church, and the essay says it proves nothing.
- Isn't the Over-Soul a Theosophy word? Answer: Emerson used it first, 34 years before the Theosophical Society existed, and Alex has decided not to quote Theosophy.

## Carl Sagan, Cosmos (1980), episode 1: 'star-stuff' (Sagan 1934 to 1996; Cosmos first broadcast 1980)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: American astronomer at Cornell and the best-known science communicator of his day. He wrote and presented the PBS series Cosmos: A Personal Voyage (1980) and wrote the companion book. He was a skeptic.

In the essay: In 'The axiom and the machine', as the second of two witnesses 'with no creed to defend': 'we are made of star-stuff and we are a way for the cosmos to know itself'. The essay calls him 'about as far from a mystic as a famous person gets'. Flag: this entry is outside the named G3 list but fits the group, so it is included in case no other group has it.

Where it sits: Cosmos: A Personal Voyage, episode 1, 'The Shores of the Cosmic Ocean' (1980), and chapter 1 of the book Cosmos (1980). A later search (2026-10-08) confirmed the line in episode 1: a community subtitle transcript of the episode gives "Because the cosmos is also within us. We're made of star-stuff. We are a way for the cosmos to know itself." at about six minutes in (the timestamp is approximate, and the transcript is not an official script). The ACMI film archive lists the episode. The page in the book is still unchecked.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Sagan says that the atoms in our bodies were made inside stars, so we are made of star-stuff, and that human beings are a way for the cosmos to know itself. From memory, not seen in this session, the line runs about: 'The cosmos is also within us. We're made of star-stuff. We are a way for the cosmos to know itself.' The broadcast and the book differ a little in wording.

Read it in a book: Carl Sagan, Cosmos (Random House, 1980; reissued many times). The episode is in the 1980 PBS series, available on disc and on streaming services. I did not verify a free legal copy of either.

Background: Sagan's line has a literal half and a poetic half. The literal half is physics: the carbon, oxygen, nitrogen and iron in our bodies were forged inside stars and scattered by exploding ones, so we are recycled stellar material. The poetic half, that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself, steps from there to the idea that conscious beings are where the universe becomes aware of itself. Sagan did not mean that the cosmos is one mind that has forgotten itself. He described himself as a skeptic and an agnostic, with no belief in a personal God, and he said that if 'God' meant the sum of the laws of nature then such a God plainly exists (memory, check before using). In The Demon-Haunted World (1995) he wrote that science is itself a source of spirituality (memory, check). In the same book he named children's reports of previous lives as one of three fringe claims that deserved serious study, while saying he doubted they would hold up; the CHECKLIST saw this only in secondary sources. That last point belongs to the survival-research group. So Sagan fits the mood of clauses one and two and is a good example of a sceptic saying the sentence. He is not a witness to the rest of the axiom, and the essay should keep the claim that small.

If someone pushes back:
- Sagan meant physics and poetry, not a single mind. Answer: yes. The essay uses him to show the sentence can be said by a non-mystic and does not say he held the axiom.
- 'Star-stuff' is just chemistry. Answer: yes, and that is the strength of it. Continuity of matter is uncontroversial. What is contested is whether the continuity reaches mind.

Read it free, added later: ACMI archive listing of episode 1, The Shores of the Cosmic Ocean: https://www.acmi.net.au/works/82873--the-shores-of-the-cosmic-ocean/ (seen in a search result). Subtitle transcript of the episode (community made, not official): https://amara.org/subtitles/owfwOsnOXVfx/en/1/download/Cosmos%20A%20Personal%20Voyage%20-%20Episode%201%20Carl%20Sagan.en.vtt (seen in a search result).

## Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, The Education of the Human Race (1780) (Lessing 1729 to 1781; the full text of 100 numbered sections appeared in 1780 (sections 1 to 53 in 1777, per the research notes))

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: German Enlightenment playwright, critic and theologian by temperament, librarian at Wolfenbuttel and author of the play Nathan the Wise (1779).

In the essay: In 'Not enough time in one life': the essay says the argument for more than one life 'is older than I am', and cites Lessing as 'a thoroughly Enlightenment sort of person' who asks why he should not come back as often as he can acquire fresh knowledge.

Where it sits: Section 98 in the usual numbering of 100 numbered sections. The run on repeated lives starts at section 94 and ends at section 100 (CHECKLIST, from the Robertson translation).

The passage: Exact, in F. W. Robertson's English translation (1858, public domain), as recorded in the CHECKLIST evidence from the wist.info and education-uk.org pages (I did not open the pages myself): 'Why should I not come back as often as I am capable of acquiring fresh knowledge, fresh expertness?' Paraphrase of the context: the sections just before ask why the oldest hypothesis, that souls pass through many lives, should be laughed at only because it is old.

Read it free:
- Robertson's translation, whole text (education-uk.org): https://education-uk.org/documents/lessing/ed-hum-race.html (seen in a search result)
- Quotation page with the section 98 sentence (wist.info): https://wist.info/lessing-gotthold/70364/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: H. B. Nisbet, ed. and trans., Lessing: Philosophical and Theological Writings (Cambridge University Press, 2005), which includes the Education. Also Henry Chadwick, Lessing's Theological Writings (A. and C. Black, 1956). For his life and thought: Hugh Barr Nisbet, Gotthold Ephraim Lessing: His Life, Works, and Thought (Oxford University Press, 2013). All from memory; check a catalogue. For the ring parable, read Nathan the Wise, Act III, scene 7, in any complete English edition.

Background: The Education is a short work of 100 numbered paragraphs. Its thesis is that revelation does for humanity what education does for a child. The Old Testament is a primer for a childlike people, the New Testament a second primer, and a coming third age will have people do good because it is good and not for reward. The last paragraphs float rebirth as a hypothesis. He asks why the oldest hypothesis should be laughed at only for being old, why he should not return as often as he can learn something new, and he adds that forgetting between lives is no real objection. He offers it as a suggestion, and nowhere says he holds it as doctrine. Lessing was a pastor's son who became the sharpest critic of Lutheran orthodoxy in his day, publishing the manuscripts of the deist Reimarus (the Wolfenbuttel Fragments, 1774 to 1778), while still defending religion as moral education. In Nathan the Wise three identical rings stand for Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and the sons must prove their rings by gentleness and good deeds, which is close to Alex's own stance. After Lessing died, Friedrich Jacobi reported that in 1780 Lessing had said 'hen kai pan', one and all, and that he knew nothing else. Whether that makes him a Spinozist was fought over for years in the Pantheism controversy that began in 1785.

If someone pushes back:
- Lessing only says 'why not', he never says he believes it. Answer: correct. The essay cites him as an early case of the one-life-is-too-short argument coming from reasons about learning, not from Eastern scripture.
- Lessing was a Christian, so he is not a neutral witness. Answer: he was raised Lutheran and stayed inside the conversation about religion while attacking orthodoxy. The essay says 'Enlightenment sort of person', which is fair, and does not claim he stood outside religion.

## Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1 (and the Stoics) (Marcus 121 to 180 CE; Roman emperor 161 to 180. Stoicism founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE.)

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: Roman emperor and Stoic. The Meditations is a private notebook written in Greek, mostly on campaign, and not meant for publication. The Greek title is roughly 'To himself'. 'Meditations' is a later title.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers', as the first of three people 'with no creed' who reasoned to the same picture: the difficult people he will meet today cannot harm him because they are his kin, and we are made for cooperation like feet, hands, eyelids and teeth. Part one separately uses Marcus as a high agency example and mentions misused Stoicism as a Drifting trap.

Where it sits: Meditations 2.1, the opening of Book Two (George Long's numbering). The limb image also recurs at 7.13 and 8.34 per the research notes (not checked this session).

The passage: Exact, George Long's translation (1862, public domain), seen on MIT Classics and Lexundria in a search this session: 'For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth.' Paraphrase of the context: Marcus tells himself to expect meddlers, the ungrateful and the arrogant, who act from ignorance of good and evil. None can harm him, since none can involve him in what is base, and he cannot be angry with or hate his kinsman, because the wrongdoer shares the same reason and a portion of the same divinity.

Read it free:
- MIT Classics, Meditations Book Two (Long translation): https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html (seen in a search result)
- MIT Classics, Meditations index (all twelve books): https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.html (seen in a search result)
- Lexundria, Meditations 2.1 in Long's translation: https://lexundria.com/m_aur_med/2.1/lg (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Gregory Hays, Meditations (Modern Library, 2002), the most readable modern version. Or Robin Hard, Meditations (Oxford World's Classics, 2011, introduction by Christopher Gill). The best study is Pierre Hadot, The Inner Citadel (Harvard University Press, 1998, trans. Michael Chase). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Stoicism began with Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE. Its picture is one living, rational world, ordered by a divine reason (logos), in which every person is a part because every person shares in reason. From that comes the Stoic ethic of kinship, drawn outward from family to all humankind, and the image of the world as one city. Marcus returns to the body image more than once. In 8.34, as I remember it, a person who rebels against what happens is like a hand or foot cut off from the body (not checked this session). So the Stoics support clause one in the form of one rational whole, and they support the ethic of the fingers. They do not support the rest. Marcus is unsure whether the world runs on providence or on atoms and writes both possibilities down in several places. He treats death as dispersal, with the soul taken back into the whole, and Stoic cosmology is cyclical, so there is no journey of one soul across lives. On religion: as emperor he did his duty to the state gods, and Stoic theology is a philosophical pantheism with a providential order. Christians were persecuted under his rule, and historians debate how far he was responsible. Part one uses him as a high agency example and warns about Stoicism misused as an excuse for Drifting, so he links the parts.

If someone pushes back:
- Marcus means kinship of reason, not oneness of being. Answer: fair. He is a non-religious route to the ethic of the fingers, and the Stoic world is one whole, but the essay should not say he taught what the axiom says about separation or return.
- Isn't Stoicism the thing Part one warned about? Answer: Part one warns about Stoic acceptance used as a reason to do nothing. Marcus ran an empire and a war, so he is the counterexample.

## Arthur Schopenhauer, On the Basis of Morality (1840), compassion and tat tvam asi (Schopenhauer 1788 to 1860; the essay is dated 1840 (written for a prize question in 1839, published 1841))

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: German philosopher, author of The World as Will and Representation (1818). Known as a pessimist and as one of the first major Western philosophers to read Indian thought.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers', as the second of three people 'with no creed': he built his ethics on compassion, which he described as recognizing in another person the same being you are, and put it in the Sanskrit formula tat tvam asi, 'that art thou'.

Where it sits: On the Basis of Morality, the part on the metaphysical explanation of the primal ethical phenomenon (Part IV in Bullock's 1903 English, per a search this session; section 22 in the usual numbering, which I could not confirm). The same idea is in The World as Will and Representation, vol. 1, book 4 (section 63 on 'eternal justice' per the research notes, not checked).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Schopenhauer holds that the only action with real moral worth is done from compassion, meaning an immediate sharing in another's suffering. He explains how that is possible: separate individuals belong to the world as appearance, shaped by space and time, while underneath, the will is one. The compassionate person sees through the division, and tat tvam asi names what is seen: the other is you. He writes the formula in German as 'Tat twam asi', so search the Gutenberg text for 'twam' as well as 'tvam'. Wording not seen this session.

Read it free:
- Project Gutenberg, The Basis of Morality (Bullock translation, 1903): https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/44929 (seen in a search result)
- Wikisource, On the Basis of Morality, Part IV (the metaphysical explanation): https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Basis_of_Morality/Part_IV (seen in a search result)
- Full text PDF hosted by Stephen Hicks: https://www.stephenhicks.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Schopenhauer-Basis-of-Morality-text.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Welchman (2014), Schopenhauer and compassion (course reading, Warwick): https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/philosophy/intranets/undergraduate/modules/ph334/2015-16/welchman_2014_--_schopenhauer_and_compassion.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Southampton repository record that turned up for 'tat tvam asi' (may be Janaway's chapter; check): https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/451919 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: E. F. J. Payne, trans., On the Basis of Morality (Berghahn Books, 1995; first published 1965). The newer Cambridge version is Christopher Janaway's translation in The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics (Cambridge University Press, 2009; check year). For a guide: Christopher Janaway, Schopenhauer: A Very Short Introduction (Oxford University Press, 1994), and his chapter 'Schopenhauer's Indian Ethics' in Patrick Hassan, ed., Schopenhauer's Moral Philosophy (Routledge, 2021), which came up in a search this session.

Background: Schopenhauer wrote the essay for a prize question set by the Royal Danish Society of Sciences. No prize was given, and he published it in 1841 anyway in Die beiden Grundprobleme der Ethik (research notes; check). His argument is that morality rests on compassion, which he treats as an observable fact, and he then explains how it is possible. Being a separate individual belongs to the world as it appears to us, shaped by space and time. Underneath, the will that drives everything is one. In a moment of compassion we see through the division, and tat tvam asi says what we see. He met the Upanishads about 1813 to 1814 through Anquetil-Duperron's Latin Oupnek'hat, itself made from a Persian version, and later called it the solace of his life and of his death (research notes, memory). Three differences from the axiom matter if someone presses you. His One is a blind will, not a mind. He thought existence is mostly suffering. And his ethics ends in the denial of the will, a kind of withdrawal, which is not a return of the whole to knowing itself. On religion: he was an atheist in the Christian sense, admired Buddhism and the Upanishads, and praised saints for their compassion. Janaway notes that Schopenhauer claimed Indian wisdom had told in myth what only his own philosophy could explain.

If someone pushes back:
- Schopenhauer was a pessimist and his One is a blind will. Answer: yes, and the essay uses him for one step only, compassion as seeing the same being in the other. It does not claim he shared the return, and his ethics does end in denying the will.
- Is that really what tat tvam asi means in the Upanishad? Answer: the formula is Chandogya 6.8.7. Schopenhauer reads it through his own metaphysics, and Indian commentators read it in their own ways. The essay only needs that he saw it as the ground of compassion.

## Wang Yangming, Inquiry on the Great Learning (1527) (Wang 1472 to 1529; the Inquiry was written in 1527, shortly before his death)

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: Wang Shouren, known as Wang Yangming: a Ming dynasty official, general and teacher, and the leading figure of the 'learning of the heart-mind' school of Neo-Confucianism.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers', as the third of three people 'with no creed': in 1527 he wrote that the great person forms one body with heaven, earth and all things, and offered as evidence the alarm one feels at a child about to fall into a well, an example he took from Mencius.

Where it sits: Daxue wen (Inquiry on the Great Learning), the opening section. Chan's translation is in Instructions for Practical Living (1963) and, I believe, in his Source Book in Chinese Philosophy. Selections are in Sources of Chinese Tradition (pp. 571 to 581 in the 1960 edition, per the Oxford page).

The passage: Exact, in the translation shown on the Oxford Learning Link selection from Sources of Chinese Tradition (Columbia University Press) and matching the wording recorded in the CHECKLIST: 'Therefore when he sees a child about to fall into a well, he cannot help a feeling of alarm and commiseration. This shows that his humanity forms one body with the child.' Paraphrase of the rest: the great person regards heaven, earth and the myriad things as one body, not by deliberate choice but from the nature of his humane mind. The small person has the same mind and only makes it small. The same unbearable feeling extends to frightened animals, broken plants and smashed tiles and stones.

Read it free:
- Religion-Online, 'Wang Yang-Ming's Inquiry on the Great Learning' (translator not confirmed on the page): https://www.religion-online.org/article/page/83/ (seen in a search result)
- Religion-Online, same text, alternate address: https://www.religion-online.org/?p=8123 (seen in a search result)
- Oxford Learning Link, selections from Wang Yangming (from Sources of Chinese Tradition): https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/von-sivers-3e-dashboard-resources/document-selections-from-the-writings-of-wang-yangming (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Wing-tsit Chan, trans., Instructions for Practical Living and Other Neo-Confucian Writings by Wang Yang-ming (Columbia University Press, 1963). Philip J. Ivanhoe, Ethics in the Confucian Tradition: The Thought of Mengzi and Wang Yangming (Hackett, 2nd ed., 2002). Julia Ching, To Acquire Wisdom: The Way of Wang Yang-ming (Columbia University Press, 1976). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Wang Yangming was an official, a general and a teacher. Exiled in 1506 to a remote post in Guizhou after offending a powerful eunuch, he had in 1508 the insight that he later put as 'principle is in the heart-mind', against Zhu Xi's habit of seeking it by studying outer things (memory). Two ideas follow: knowing and acting are one, and everyone has an innate moral knowing (liangzhi). The Inquiry is a dialogue on the opening of a short Confucian classic. Wang reads 'manifesting bright virtue' as recovering our original unity with all things, which selfish desire covers over. The well scene is from Mencius, and Wang then widens it to frightened animals, broken plants and smashed tiles. Be careful with 'one body'. Robert Neville argues the metaphor does not deny diversity, and specialists note that Confucian care is graded, with family first and then outward. So it is a claim about relation and responsibility, and not that you and the child are the same person. On religion: Wang is a Neo-Confucian, and whether Confucianism is a religion is itself debated. He borrowed from Chan Buddhism and Daoism while critics accused him of sounding Buddhist. He died in 1529, not long after writing this.

If someone pushes back:
- Confucian love is graded, so this is not oneness. Answer: fair. 'One body' with all things is a wide moral concern and not a claim of numerical identity, and the essay should not say more than that the great person feels the child's danger as his own.
- The well is Mencius, not Wang. Answer: yes, and the essay says so. Wang takes the scene and turns it into evidence of a one-body humanity.

## Mencius (Mengzi) 2A6, the child at the well (Mencius, traditional dates c. 372 to 289 BCE)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: Confucian philosopher who argued that human nature is good. The book Mencius (Mengzi) records his conversations with rulers and disciples.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers', as the source of the well example that Wang Yangming 'took from Mencius'. The essay does not claim Mencius taught oneness. Listed in the Sources as 'Mencius 2A6'.

Where it sits: Mencius 2A6 (book 2, Gongsun Chou I, section 6). Legge numbers it Book II, Part I, chapter 6.

The passage: Exact opening line, D. C. Lau's translation (Penguin), as quoted in a search summary this session: 'No man is devoid of a heart sensitive to the sufferings of others.' Paraphrase of the scene: anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion, and not in order to win favour with the parents, praise from neighbours or friends, or to avoid a bad name. Mencius concludes that the heart of compassion is the sprout of benevolence, alongside shame (righteousness), deference (ritual propriety) and the sense of right and wrong (wisdom).

Read it free:
- Chinese Text Project, Mengzi with English (Legge), book Gongsun Chou I: https://ctext.org/mengzi/ens (seen in a search result)
- Legge's complete Works of Mencius: https://www.hinduwebsite.com/sacredscripts/mencius.asp (seen in a search result)
- Sacred-texts, Mencius (this page came up for 2A6; confirm it is the Gongsun Chou chapter): https://sacred-texts.com/cfu/menc/menc06.htm (seen in a search result)
- Translation by Gier (University of Idaho): https://www.webpages.uidaho.edu/ngier/103/mentrans.htm (seen in a search result)
- Warp, Weft and Way, commentary on Mencius 2A6: https://warpweftandway.com/ability-and-cultivation-in-mencius-2a6/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: D. C. Lau, Mencius (Penguin Classics, 1970, rev. 2003). Bryan W. Van Norden, Mengzi: With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (Hackett, 2008). Irene Bloom, Mencius (Columbia University Press, 2009). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Mencius argues against thinkers who said human nature is neutral or bad. The child at the well is his thought experiment. It is sudden, there is no time to calculate, and the pang you feel comes before any thought of reward or reputation. From this he says everyone has the beginnings, or sprouts (duan), of four virtues, and that morality is the tending of those sprouts, as a farmer tends shoots. Scholars note the stress on suddenness, and Richard King observes that the example illustrates the universal claim and does not prove it. Critics ancient and modern doubt the inference. The best known is Xunzi, who held that human nature is bad and goodness is made by training. Important for Alex: Mencius does not say all people are one. He says humans share a moral heart. The one-body reading is the later Neo-Confucian development of Zhang Zai (the Western Inscription, 'heaven is my father and earth my mother', from memory) and Wang Yangming. Mencius also holds that love is graded, and he attacks Mozi for teaching impartial care. On religion: Confucianism is a moral and ritual tradition in which Tian, Heaven, is a moral order, and Mencius says the heart's goodness comes from Heaven. The essay's use is fair as long as it says Wang took the example from Mencius and does not say Mencius taught oneness.

If someone pushes back:
- Mencius does not teach oneness. Answer: right, and the essay does not say he does. It credits the one-body claim to Wang and uses Mencius only for the scene.
- The pang could be self-interest or disgust at a scream. Answer: Mencius anticipates that and rules out favour, praise and the child's cry as the cause. Modern psychologists still argue about it, so the essay uses it as an illustration and not as a result.

## Plato, Republic VII, the allegory of the cave (Plato c. 428 to 348 BCE; the Republic is a mid-career dialogue, roughly 375 BCE)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Athenian philosopher, student of Socrates and founder of the Academy. The Republic is a dialogue, with Socrates as the speaker, on justice and the ideal city.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real': 'Plato's prisoners in the cave take shadows for the world', one of several old pictures of the veil. Listed in the Sources as 'Plato, Republic VII (the cave)'.

Where it sits: Republic 514a to 521b (Book VII). The shadows and the freed prisoner are 514a to 517c; the return to the cave is 519c to 521b.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Socrates asks us to imagine prisoners chained since childhood in an underground cave, facing a wall and unable to turn their heads. Behind them a fire burns and people carry statues and objects along a walkway, so the prisoners see only shadows on the wall and take them for the real things. One prisoner is freed and dragged up into daylight. He is dazzled and pained at first, then learns to see, and at last looks at the sun, which stands for the Form of the Good. If he goes back down he sees badly in the dark, and the others mock him. Wording not seen this session.

A guide: Julia Annas, An Introduction to Plato's Republic (Oxford University Press, 1981). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: The cave comes right after the images of the sun and the divided line, as a picture of education. The soul has to be turned around, as I recall around 518c and d, from the world of changing appearances toward what is real. The freed prisoner's return (519c to 521b) is the demand that the philosopher go back down and take part in the life of the city, though he would rather stay in the light. That return is a useful link to the bodhisattva who comes back to the marketplace in the essay's table. Scholars read the cave as being about knowledge, politics and the Good. The prisoners are not told that they and the others are one, and Plato's Forms are many. So the cave supports one thing in the essay, the shape of the veil: appearances taken for reality. It says nothing about oneness. Later readers have borrowed the cave for media, ideology and simulation arguments. On religion: Plato was a Greek who believed in gods in his own way, criticised Homer's picture of them, taught that the soul is immortal and pre-exists, and in the Timaeus described a divine craftsman. He is religious in a philosophical key, and Platonism fed Jewish, Christian and Islamic thought.

If someone pushes back:
- The cave is about knowledge, not oneness. Answer: agreed. It is quoted only as an old picture of taking appearances for the real, which is the veil, and the essay says that is all the traditions' pictures share.
- Doesn't Plato mean the body is the cave? Answer: that reading comes more from the Phaedo and the Phaedrus. In the Republic the cave is mostly about education and what the philosopher owes the city.

## Plato, Phaedo (Plato c. 428 to 348 BCE; the dialogue is set on the day Socrates died, 399 BCE, and was written some years later)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A dialogue narrated by Phaedo, set in the prison on the last day of Socrates, with his friends Simmias and Cebes. It ends with Socrates drinking hemlock.

In the essay: In 'Not enough time in one life': 'In Plato's Phaedo, Socrates gives several arguments for the survival of the soul on his last day', as one Greek example in a list of traditions where something continues between lives.

Where it sits: Phaedo 70c to 107b for the arguments. According to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy summary seen this session: cyclical argument 70c to 72e, recollection 72e to 78b, affinity 78b to 84b, final argument from the Forms 102b to 107b. The myth of the afterlife follows, roughly 107c to 115a (memory).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Socrates says death is the separation of soul from body. Cyclical argument: opposites come from opposites, as waking from sleeping, so the living come from the dead and souls exist before birth and after death. Recollection: we know Equality itself though we have never seen perfect equals, so the soul knew the Forms before birth. Affinity: the soul is like the invisible, unchanging and divine, the body like the visible and perishable. Wording not seen this session.

Read it free:
- Project Gutenberg, Phaedo (Jowett): https://www.gutenberg.org/etext/1658 (seen in a search result)
- Project Gutenberg, Phaedo, single-page HTML: https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1658/pg1658-images.html (seen in a search result)
- Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Plato: Phaedo (summary of the arguments): https://iep.utm.edu/phaedo (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: David Gallop, trans., Phaedo (Oxford World's Classics, 1993), or Plato: Complete Works, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett, 1997). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: The scene is 399 BCE. A jury has condemned Socrates, and he spends his last day talking with friends before he drinks the hemlock. The dialogue itself says Plato was ill and not there (59b). Socrates argues that a philosopher should not fear death, because philosophy is a practice of loosening the soul from bodily concerns. Simmias and Cebes raise objections, for example that the soul might be like the harmony of a lyre that ends when the lyre breaks, and Socrates answers with a final argument from the Forms. Scholars disagree about how much the arguments prove. From the search summaries this session: the affinity argument makes survival likely and does not prove it, recollection shows pre-existence and not survival, and John Palmer reads the arguments as dialectical and not as proofs. Near the end Socrates himself says no sensible man would insist that things are just as he described, but that it is a risk worth taking (114d, memory). The dialogue names an 'ancient doctrine' that souls go to Hades and come back (70c), and the research notes trace Plato's soul lore to Orphic and Pythagorean circles (Dodds, Burkert). On religion: Socrates was tried for impiety, yet speaks as a pious man who obeyed the god at Delphi, and his last words order a cock sacrificed to Asclepius. The link to Part one is the idea of practising dying, which is letting go.

If someone pushes back:
- The arguments are weak. Answer: many philosophers agree they do not prove survival. The essay says 'arguments for' and cites the dialogue as an example of the idea, not as evidence.
- Plato has rebirth as animals, which the axiom does not say. Answer: correct. The essay uses only the idea that something continues, and says the traditions disagree on the details.

## Plato, Republic X, the Myth of Er (Plato c. 428 to 348 BCE; the myth closes the Republic, roughly 375 BCE)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A story that Socrates tells at the end of the Republic, given as the report of Er, a soldier who was killed in battle, lay twelve days, and woke on his funeral pyre.

In the essay: In 'Not enough time in one life': 'in the Myth of Er that closes the Republic, souls choose their next lives and then drink from the river of forgetfulness before they are born, which is one old answer to why you don't remember the last one.' This wording already has the order the CHECKLIST required (choose, then drink).

Where it sits: Republic 614b to 621d. The lottery and the choice of lives are about 617d to 620d. The plain of Lethe and the river Ameles are about 620e to 621b.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. After judgment and a thousand years of reward or punishment, souls come to the Spindle of Necessity, draw lots for the order of choice and choose their next lives from a spread of patterns, human and animal, tyrants and ordinary people. The blame is on the chooser and god is blameless (about 617e). Odysseus, last in line, picks a quiet private life, and Orpheus picks to be a swan. Then the souls go to the plain of Lethe and drink from the river Ameles, some more than the measure, forget, and are born. Er was forbidden to drink and woke on the pyre. Details beyond the order of events are from memory and search summaries.

Read it free:
- Jowett's translation of Republic X, 614b to 621d (University of Tennessee course page): https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~bmaclenn/Classes/US310/rep.X.614B-621D.html (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Myth of Er (sequence of events): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_Er (seen in a search result)
- EBSCO Research Starters, The Myth of Er: https://ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/myth-er (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Lethe (plain of Lethe and the river Ameles): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lethe (seen in a search result)
- Project Gutenberg, The Republic, Jowett (number from a search snippet; confirm on the page): https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1497 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Same editions as the Republic: Grube and Reeve (Hackett, 1992), Tom Griffith (Cambridge University Press, 2000), or Allan Bloom (Basic Books, 1968). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: The Republic ends with this myth to show that justice pays in this life and the next. Socrates says that if we believe the story we will believe the soul is immortal and keep to the upward road (621c, memory). Three points help for discussions. First, choice. Souls choose their next lives with the character they brought from the last one, and those who had lived in a well-ordered city by habit, without philosophy, often choose badly (619c to d, memory). Second, forgetting. The souls drink and forget, which is Plato's answer to why we do not remember. Third, what carries over is character and habits of choosing, not memory, which fits the essay's picture of the saved account rather than the session. Scholars read Plato's myths as 'likely stories', and Plato does not claim they are literally true. The idea of rebirth itself reached him from Orphic and Pythagorean circles (Dodds, Burkert, per the research notes). On religion: Plato was religious in a philosophical key. The myth also cuts against easy fatalism, since the responsibility sits with the chooser.

If someone pushes back:
- It is a literary myth, not evidence. Answer: yes. The essay cites it as an old answer to the forgetting question and not as proof of anything.
- Plato's souls can come back as animals. Answer: yes, and the essay claims nothing about that. It uses the idea of choosing and forgetting only.

## Plotinus, Enneads (procession and return) (Plotinus c. 204 to 270 CE; treatises written from about 253 and arranged by Porphyry)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Greek-speaking philosopher born in Egypt, a pupil of Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria who taught in Rome from 244. His 54 treatises were arranged into six groups of nine (the Enneads) by his student Porphyry. Founder of Neoplatonism.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real', in the paragraph on why the whole would do this at all: 'Plotinus described a procession of the One into multiplicity and a return.' It is one of four answers given by the traditions, with lila, tzimtzum and the hidden treasure.

Where it sits: Enneads V.1 and V.2 for the procession from the One to Intellect and Soul. IV.8 for the descent of the soul. VI.9.11 for 'the flight of the alone to the Alone' (the phrase was seen in a search this session, but the section number is from the CHECKLIST and memory).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The One is beyond being and thought. Because it is perfect it overflows, producing Intellect (Nous), which looks back at the One and so becomes all the Forms. Intellect produces Soul, and Soul makes and orders the visible world. Each level turns back toward its source, and that turning is the return. The soul's return ends in a union that Plotinus calls, at VI.9.11, the 'flight of the alone to the Alone'. The English of that phrase varies by translator; the Greek is phyge monou pros monon, as given in a search this session. Everything else is paraphrase.

Armstrong, Plotinus (Loeb Classical Library, 7 vols., Harvard University Press, 1966 to 1988). Guides: Lloyd P. Gerson, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Plotinus (1996); Pierre Hadot, Plotinus, or the Simplicity of Vision (University of Chicago Press, 1993). From memory; check a catalogue. Porphyry's Life of Plotinus is printed with most editions.

Background: Plotinus has three principles: the One (beyond being and thought, the Good), Intellect (the realm of Forms, the One's first product) and Soul (which makes and governs the visible world). Production is overflow, often called emanation. The One loses nothing and does not choose, and its goodness spills over by necessity. Each product is incomplete until it turns back and looks at its source, which makes it what it is. So remaining, procession and return is the pattern of Plotinus. The three Greek words (mone, proodos, epistrophe) were formalised by Proclus two centuries later (research notes). Why does Soul descend? Plotinus gives more than one reason in different treatises: boldness or self-will (V.1.1), necessity, and the task of ordering the lower world (IV.8). He insists the cosmos is good, and he argues against the Gnostics (II.9), who held the world was made by a flawed power. For him separation is a loosening of attention and not a crime, which is close to the axiom. But the One does not experience separation or want to know itself. Knowing begins at the level of Intellect. Plotinus accepts rebirth. On religion: a pagan philosopher with no church, in whose circle there were Christians and Gnostics. Porphyry reports that Plotinus reached union four times while Porphyry was with him (Life of Plotinus 23). Neoplatonism shaped Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Eckhart and Islamic and Jewish thought.

If someone pushes back:
- For Plotinus the One does not experience separation or want anything, so it is not your axiom. Answer: correct. The fit is the shape, one source, a going out, a return. The essay offers the four stories as answers to why, and says the traditions are modest about it.
- Emanation is not a decision, so why quote it? Answer: that is the point of the contrast with lila and tzimtzum. The essay's own reading, that knowing needs a gap, is Alex's, and it says so.

## Zhuangzi, chapter 2 (Qiwulun), the butterfly dream (Zhuang Zhou, traditionally c. 369 to 286 BCE. The Inner Chapters (1 to 7) are usually attributed to him and the rest to later followers.)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Zhuangzi is one of the two founding books of philosophical Daoism, a collection of parables, dialogues and arguments named for its main figure. Chapter 2 is titled Qiwulun, rendered 'Discussion on Making All Things Equal' by Burton Watson.

In the essay: In 'Why separation feels so real': 'Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, woke up, and couldn't tell whether he was a man who had dreamed of a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming it was a man, though the text adds that there must be some distinction, which it calls the transformation of things.' It is Daoism's only appearance as a witness, as another picture of the veil.

Where it sits: Zhuangzi, chapter 2, the last paragraph. The closing Chinese line is 周與胡蝶，則必有分矣。此之謂物化, 'between Zhou and the butterfly there must be a distinction; this is called the transformation of things', as recorded in the CHECKLIST evidence (Watson's translation in a University of Hawaii reader and on China Heritage).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase, confirmed to the gist by the CHECKLIST and by searches this session: Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, happy and unaware of being Zhou; he wakes and is plainly Zhou; he cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamed a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming Zhou. The text says there must be a distinction between them, and calls this the transformation of things. I did not see Watson's English verbatim this session. From memory (research notes), earlier in the chapter: 'heaven and earth were born at the same time I was, and the ten thousand things are one with me', followed at once by the doubt that calling it one already makes two.

- Burton Watson's translation of chapter 2 (University of Hawaii course reader, PDF): https://www2.hawaii.edu/~freeman/courses/phil394/Zhuangzi.pdf (seen in a search result)
- China Heritage, 'Am I a Butterfly?', with Watson's translation: https://chinaheritage.net/journal/am-i-a-butterfly-zhuangzi/ (seen in a search result)
- D. K. Jordan, the butterfly dream with Chinese and notes (UC San Diego): https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/LaoJuang/ButterflyDream-e.html (seen in a search result)
- Chinese Text Project, Zhuangzi (address came from a search summary, not a result; chapter 2 is called 'Adjustment of Controversies' in Legge): https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/zhs (not checked)

Read it in a book: Burton Watson, The Complete Works of Zhuangzi (Columbia University Press, 2013). A. C. Graham, Chuang-tzu: The Inner Chapters (Hackett, 2001; first published 1981). Brook Ziporyn, Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings (Hackett, 2009). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Chapter 2 is the hardest in the book. It argues that our verdicts (this is right, that is wrong, this is me, that is not) depend on where we stand, and that the sage sees from the 'axis' of the Dao, where this and that stop opposing each other. The butterfly is the closing image. Note what it does not say. It does not say the self is an illusion in the strong sense. It says there is a distinction between the man and the butterfly, and then names the passing of one into the other the transformation of things (wuhua). Earlier in the chapter, as I remember it, there is also a passage on a great awakening after which we will know this life was a great dream. Scholars differ on what the chapter teaches. A. C. Graham and Chad Hansen read it as perspectival skepticism and not as asserting an ontological One, while other readers, including Ziporyn in places, find a monism of the Dao (research notes, memory). On religion: philosophical Daoism (daojia) is separated from the later religious Daoism (daojiao), which adopted Zhuangzi as a patron. Zhuangzi himself was no founder of a creed. The essay's introduction promises Daoist witnesses, but this is the only one used.

If someone pushes back:
- The butterfly is about dreams and perspective, not oneness. Answer: yes. The essay uses it as a picture of not being able to tell which state is the real one, and quotes the text's own line that there must be a distinction.
- Zhuangzi would reject your axiom as one more claim. Answer: Graham and Hansen read him that way. The essay uses him as a witness to one sentence, and the whole of Part two says it proves nothing.

## Confucius, Analects 15.24 (reciprocity, shu) (Confucius 551 to 479 BCE; the Analects was put together by disciples and later hands over generations)

Kind: person. Status: checked.

Who: Kong Fuzi, teacher and sometime official in the state of Lu. The Analects is a collection of his sayings and conversations with students, compiled after his death.

In the essay: In the golden rule paragraph of 'Karma, or the fingers': 'Confucius names reciprocity as the one word for conduct in Analects 15.24, numbered 15.23 in older translations.' The essay adds that most of these golden-rule sayings rest on reciprocity or empathy, and that a few, like the Isha Upanishad, go further.

Where it sits: Analects 15.24 (chapter Wei Ling Gong). Legge numbers it 15.23. Related passages: 5.12, 12.2 and 4.15 (seen in a search this session).

The passage: Exact, D. C. Lau's translation (Penguin), as quoted in a search summary this session: 'It is perhaps the word shu. Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire.' The question, in Legge's public domain English: 'Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?' Paraphrase: Zigong asks for a single word to guide a person's conduct through life, and the Master answers shu and then states the negative rule. Watson renders the answer: 'What you do not want others to do to you, do not do to others.'

Burton Watson, The Analects of Confucius (Columbia University Press, 2007). Annping Chin, The Analects (Penguin Classics, 2014). From memory; check a catalogue.

Background: Zigong was one of the best-known disciples, a merchant and diplomat. Shu is hard to translate: reciprocity, consideration, 'like-heartedness' (Jennings), or the habit of taking your own feelings as a measure of another's. At 4.15 the disciple Zengzi sums up the Master's way as doing one's best (zhong) and shu. The rule is negative, which some call the silver rule, against the positive form in Matthew 7:12. At 5.12 Zigong says he does not want to impose on others what he does not want imposed on him, and the Master replies that this is beyond him, a useful warning that the rule is harder than it sounds (seen in a search this session). Some scholars ask whether 15.24 is a rule at all or an expression of the Confucian Way, and one essay found in the search reads shu as a virtue and not as a formula. The key point for Alex is that shu rests on analogy between self and other, not on identity. That is reciprocity, which the essay says outright. On religion: Confucius spoke of Heaven (Tian) with respect and kept the rites, but was reserved about spirits. I recall 'respect the spirits but keep them at a distance' (6.22) and 'not yet understanding life, how can you understand death?' (11.12), both from memory. Whether Confucianism is a religion is debated.

If someone pushes back:
- It is just the golden rule, which every culture has. What does it prove about oneness? Answer: nothing, and the essay says so. It calls the golden rule the same logic read forwards and says most versions rest on reciprocity, with only a few going further.
- Confucius's version is negative. Answer: yes. Hillel's is negative too, and Jesus's is positive. The essay does not claim they are identical, only that they are close.
