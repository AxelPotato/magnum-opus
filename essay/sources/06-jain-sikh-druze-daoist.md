## Acharanga Sutra (Ayaro), in Hermann Jacobi's 1884 translation (Book I probably 5th to 4th century BCE; written down in the Svetambara canon at Valabhi (traditionally 454 CE); Jacobi's translation 1884)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A Prakrit text, the first of the twelve Angas of the Svetambara Jain canon, on monastic conduct and non-violence. Its first book is the oldest layer and is generally dated to about the fifth or fourth century BCE. Hermann Jacobi's English version (Sacred Books of the East vol. 22, 1884) was the first complete one and is in the public domain.

In the essay: In 'Karma, or the fingers': 'Even its oldest text on conduct, the Acharanga, tells the reader that the person you intend to hurt is as you are'. The essay sets one modern commentator's reading (oneness of the killer and the killed) against Jacobi's more careful wording and adds that Jain doctrine holds souls to be many. The table row 'What you do to another' (Jainism: non-harm grounded in the equality of souls) rests on the same ground. Also listed in the Sources section.

Where it sits: Jacobi, SBE 22: Book I, Lecture 5 (Essence of the World), Lesson 5, about p. 50 (sacred-texts page sbe2228). The general rule of non-harm is Book I, Lecture 4, Lesson 1. In Mahaprajna's numbering the same sutra is 5.101 (Sutras 99 to 103, 'Non-Violence').

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: in the fifth lesson of the fifth lecture the monk is led through killing, tyrannising over, tormenting and driving away, and each time is told that it is with the other as it is with himself; whoever lives by this neither kills nor has others kill. The earlier check (CHECKLIST C5-22) read Jacobi's sentence as 'As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill', repeated for 'tyrannise over' and 'torment'. My own search confirmed that 'whom thou intendest to kill' and 'intendest to tyrannise over' are on that page, but I did not see the whole sentence there, so read it on the page before you quote it.

- Jainpedia, reading page on the Akaranga Sutra: https://jainpedia.org/reading/akaranga-sutra (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Hermann Jacobi (trans.), Jaina Sutras, Part I (Akaranga Sutra and Kalpa Sutra), Sacred Books of the East 22, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1884 (reprinted by Motilal Banarsidass; the 1884 title page spells it 'Gaina Sutras'). For a modern translation with a Jain commentary see Mahaprajna, Acharanga Bhasyam (Jain Vishva Bharati, Ladnun, 2001). For the background: Paul Dundas, The Jains (Routledge, 2nd ed. 2002), and Walther Schubring, The Doctrine of the Jainas (Motilal Banarsidass, 1962).

Background: The Acharanga (Prakrit Ayaro) is the first of the twelve Angas, the core of the Svetambara canon. Its first book is a short, compressed discourse on living without harming any living thing. Jacobi and later scholars date that layer to about the fifth or fourth century BCE, which makes it one of the oldest surviving Jain texts. The canon was written down at Valabhi in the fifth century CE (traditionally 454), and the Digambara branch does not accept it, holding that the original Angas were lost. So 'Jainism's oldest text' is a Svetambara claim. The passage the essay uses sits in a lesson that goes through killing, ordering about, tormenting and driving away, and each time tells the monk that the other is as he is. As I remember the Prakrit, it is blunter than Jacobi's English, roughly 'you are the very one you think of killing'. Jacobi worked with the ninth century commentary of Silanka (from memory), and Mahaprajna's modern commentary reads the line toward non-duality. Jain philosophy as a whole treats the equality of souls as sameness of nature, since every soul has the same capacities, and not as one soul in many bodies. The safe sentence for conversation: the Acharanga grounds non-harm in the likeness of all living beings, and one modern Jain commentator reads it as closer to oneness.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Jainism says souls are many, so this can't support oneness.' Agreed on the doctrine, and the essay says so in the same sentence. The text is a witness for the ethic of likeness, and the metaphysics differ.
- 'Is it really the oldest Jain text?' It is the oldest on linguistic grounds in the Svetambara canon (Jacobi's judgment), and the Digambaras build on other texts.

Translation check (2026-10-08): Jacobi (SBE 22, 1884, Book I, Lecture 5, Lesson 5) renders a parallel formula as 'As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill', then the same for tyrannise over, torment, punish and drive away. The Prakrit is stronger (tumamsi nama sacceva jam hamtavvam ti mannasi, 'you are indeed the very one you think to kill'), which Mahaprajna's commentary reads as the non-duality of killer and killed. The essay now follows Jacobi: 'whoever you intend to kill or torment feels as you would'.

## Hermann Jacobi (1850 to 1937 (from memory))

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: German Indologist who taught at Münster, Kiel and Bonn. He translated the Akaranga and Kalpa Sutras (1884) and the Uttaradhyayana and Sutrakritanga (1895) for Max Müller's Sacred Books of the East, and did more than anyone to put Jainism on the Western academic map.

In the essay: The essay says 'Hermann Jacobi's older translation puts it more carefully' than the modern commentator who reads the Acharanga line as the oneness of killer and killed, and lists Jacobi (1884, SBE 22) in the Sources section.

Where it sits: Jacobi, Jaina Sutras Part I, SBE 22 (1884), introduction and the translation of Book I, Lecture 5, Lesson 5; Part II, SBE 45 (1895), for the Sutrakritanga.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: Jacobi's rendering keeps the sense of likeness between the one who would harm and the one who would be harmed (the other is as you are), and does not say in so many words that the two are one. Check the sentence on the sacred-texts page before quoting.

Read it free:
- SBE 22 index on sacred-texts (Jacobi's translation and introduction): https://sacred-texts.com/jai/sbe22/index.htm (seen in a search result)
- SBE 45 Sutrakritanga page, Jacobi 1895: https://sacred-texts.com/jai/sbe45/sbe4539.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Jacobi, Jaina Sutras, Part I (SBE 22, Clarendon Press, 1884) and Part II (SBE 45, Clarendon Press, 1895); Motilal Banarsidass reprints. For his argument that Jainism is independent of Buddhism, read the introduction to SBE 22.

Background: Jacobi (1850 to 1937, from memory) was a philologist of the nineteenth century German school. In the 1880s he argued that Jainism is an independent and old tradition and not a Buddhist offshoot, as some earlier European scholars had thought, and that Mahavira was an older contemporary of the Buddha. His translations appeared in the Sacred Books of the East series edited by Max Müller. His English is Victorian ('thou intendest'), he leaned on the medieval Sanskrit commentary of Silanka, and some of his choices have since been revised, so scholars treat him as a careful, early, public-domain starting point and compare newer work such as the 2001 English edition of Mahaprajna's commentary. He matters for the essay because his wording is cautious: it keeps the likeness of the other to oneself, which is the fairer thing to put next to a modern gloss of oneness. If you quote him, say 'Jacobi, 1884', say that it is a Victorian translation, and read the sentence on the page, because I have not seen the whole of it in this session. One small trap: the sacred-texts page header may show 'Part II' while SBE 22 is Part I of Jacobi's Jaina Sutras.

If someone pushes back:
- 'A translation from 1884 is out of date.' Partly true. Use it as a free, readable base and compare it with Tatia and Mahaprajna (2001) when the exact sense matters.
- 'Who was he to judge Jain doctrine?' He was a philologist and did not speak for the Jains; the essay uses him for wording and not for doctrine.

## Acharya Mahaprajna and his Acharanga Bhasyam (1920 to 2010 (from memory); English Acharanga Bhasyam published 2001)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Tenth head of the Svetambara Terapanth, a reform branch of Jainism, a prolific writer and the shaper of Preksha Meditation. The Acharanga Bhasyam is his Sanskrit commentary on the Acharanga; the English edition (Jain Vishva Bharati, Ladnun, 2001) was translated by Nathmal Tatia, Muni Dulaharaj and Muni Mahendra Kumar and prints the Prakrit in Roman letters.

In the essay: He is 'one modern commentator' in 'Karma, or the fingers': the essay says he reads the Acharanga line as the oneness of the killer and the killed, and sets this against Jacobi's more careful wording. Listed in the Sources section as 'Mahaprajna's Acharanga Bhasyam'.

Where it sits: Acharanga Bhasyam, commentary on Book I, Lecture 5, Lesson 5; in his numbering Sutras 99 to 103 ('Non-Violence'), the line being sutra 5.101. English edition: Jain Vishva Bharati, Ladnun, 2001, lxix + 546 pp.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: the English rendering of the sutra is in the second person, 'You are indeed he whom you intend to hurt ... you intend to kill' (the earlier check saw this in a mirror of the Bhasyam, CHECKLIST C5-23), and a summary of his comment says that the harmer and the harmed are not truly separate, that a person who thinks he kills another is in effect killing himself, and that once this non-duality is grasped, non-violence becomes part of one's own nature. I have not read the full comment myself.

Read it in a book: Acharya Mahaprajna, Acharanga Bhasyam, English translation by Nathmal Tatia, Muni Dulaharaj and Muni Mahendra Kumar (Jain Vishva Bharati, Ladnun, 2001). Hard to find outside India; a university library or the publisher is the likely route.

Background: Mahaprajna (from memory, born 1920, died 2010) led the Svetambara Terapanth, an eighteenth century reform movement that rejects temple image worship. With his teacher Acharya Tulsi he worked on a critical edition of the Svetambara canon and wrote Sanskrit commentaries on several Angas. He is best known outside the sect for Preksha Meditation, whose first step is a form of kayotsarga. The check found his English rendering of the sutra and a mirror of the commentary on Sutras 99 to 103. A summary of that commentary says the harmer and harmed are not truly separate and that non-violence follows once this is seen. What I cannot confirm is the word 'oneness'. Mahaprajna remains a Jain, and Jain doctrine holds that souls are many, so he may mean identity of feeling and nature, which Jain texts call likeness, and not numerical identity. Jainism is also not a school of non-dualism in the Vedanta sense, so 'non-duality' in a Jain commentary needs careful reading. Before you say in a conversation that a Jain commentator teaches oneness, open Sutras 99 to 103 and read his own sentences. Until then the safe wording is that he reads the line as showing that the harmer and the harmed are not truly separate.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Did he really say oneness?' I have seen his translation and a summary of his comment, not the full comment. I would say he reads the harmer and the harmed as not truly separate, and leave the word oneness out until you have read it.
- 'Isn't that against Jain doctrine of many souls?' It would be if he meant one soul. Read in a Jain way it is the equality and likeness of souls, and the essay already notes that Jain doctrine holds the souls to be many.

## Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (Umasvati): souls render service to one another (Umasvati (also Umasvami), roughly 2nd to 5th century CE, date disputed)

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The Tattvartha Sutra is a Sanskrit work in ten short chapters that sets out the whole Jain system, and it is the one Jain text accepted by both the Svetambara and the Digambara branches. It is attributed to Umasvati. Sutra 5.21 is the line 'parasparopagraho jivanam' (standard spelling jivanam with long vowels), which is now the motto of Jainism.

In the essay: The table (Jainism column, row 'The one whole') says 'Countless souls, alike in nature. Souls render service to one another.' It is cited in the Sources section as 'Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (souls render service to one another)'. The essay is honest here: the cell shows plurality and interdependence, not one whole.

Where it sits: Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, in the list of the functions of the substances (5.17 to 5.22). Numbering is the same in both sects at this point as far as I know; check.

The passage: 'Souls render service to one another' (Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is, HarperCollins 1994, p. 131, as quoted on the Wikipedia page for the phrase and recorded in CHECKLIST C10-04). Another common rendering is 'the function of souls is mutual support' or 'reciprocal dependence of living beings'.

- Jainpedia, Tattvartha Sutra: https://jainpedia.org/themes/principles/sacred-writings/tattvartha-sutra (seen in a search result)
- Vijay K. Jain, Acarya Umasvami's Tattvarthasutra with explanation in English from Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (free to read, PhilArchive): https://philarchive.org/rec/JAICUT-2 (seen in a search result)
- Wisdom Library, Tattvartha Sutra (with commentary): https://de.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Tattvartha Sutra: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is: Tattvartha Sutra of Umasvati/Umasvami (HarperCollins / Institute of Jainology, 1994), the Svetambara recension with Umasvati's own commentary. For the Digambara side: S. A. Jain, Reality: English translation of Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (Vira Sasana Sangha, Calcutta, 1960), or Vijay K. Jain's edition (free on PhilArchive). Also Manu Doshi, Tattvarthasutra (Shrut Ratnakar, Ahmedabad, 2007).

Background: Umasvati wrote in Sanskrit, probably between the second and fifth centuries CE, which was unusual for a Jain author of the time, since the canon is in Prakrit. In ten short chapters he covers knowledge, the soul, the worlds, the non-living substances, the inflow and bondage of karma, its stopping and shedding, and liberation. Svetambaras read it with Umasvati's own commentary, Digambaras with Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (about the sixth century, from memory), and the two recensions number some sutras differently. Sutra 5.21 sits in a list of the work each substance does for the world: matter gives bodies, speech, mind and breath, and pleasure, pain, life and death, and the function of souls is mutual help. Today the phrase is the Jain motto, printed on the Jain emblem (adopted in the 1970s, from memory) and read as 'live and help live'. In its place in the text it is a statement about how souls function in the cosmos, and the ethical reading is a later emphasis. Classical commentators illustrate it with relations such as master and servant or teacher and pupil (from memory). The essay is right to file it as plurality: many distinct souls who support one another, with no whole behind them. It supports interdependence and the ethic of care, and not the oneness of souls.

If someone pushes back:
- 'That is about interdependence, not oneness.' Yes, and the cell says 'countless souls'. It is offered as an example of an ethic of mutual dependence.
- 'Isn't it just a modern slogan?' The sutra is old. The slogan reading is modern, and in context it states a function of souls, which commentators then use for ethics.

## Tattvartha Sutra chapter 8: karma as a veil on the soul (Umasvati, roughly 2nd to 5th century CE)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Chapter 8 of the Tattvartha Sutra is the chapter on bondage (bandha): how karma, which Jainism understands as fine matter, attaches to the soul and which kinds there are. Its first type is knowledge-covering karma (jnanavaraniya), the Sanskrit word 'avarana' meaning covering or veil.

In the essay: 'Why separation feels so real': 'Jain teaching describes karma as fine matter that veils the soul's own natural knowledge, as clouds veil the sun, so that the perfection was there all along.' Also the table (Jainism column, row 'The veil'): 'Karma veils the soul's own knowledge, like clouds over the sun.' The Sources section lists chapter 10 and 5.21 but not chapter 8, which this sentence rests on.

Where it sits: Tattvartha Sutra 8.1 to 8.7 in the Digambara numbering (8.1 causes of bondage, 8.2 to 8.3 bondage defined, 8.4 four aspects, 8.5 the eight kinds of karma, 8.7 the five kinds of knowledge-covering karma; the check saw the last as 8.07). Svetambara numbering differs by a sutra or two; sutra numbers here are from memory except 8.07.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: because of the passions the soul takes in matter fit to become karma, and this is bondage; the first two of the eight kinds of karma cover knowledge and perception, and these, with delusion and obstruction, are the four that damage the soul's own qualities. The cloud-and-sun picture is not in the sutra itself; the check traced it to later Jain accounts and the Nandi Sutra, and a Jain text summarised in the earlier research says this karma does not reduce the soul's capacity but restricts its exercise.

Read it free:
- Tattvartha Sutra chapter 8 (herenow4u, sutra 8.07 on knowledge-covering karma): https://herenow4u.net/?id=132640 (seen in a search result)
- Pluralism Project, 'Karma: Clouding of the Soul' (a Jain account with the cloud and sun picture): https://pluralism.org/karma-clouding-of-the-soul (seen in a search result)
- Jainpedia glossary, jnana-avaraniya karma: https://jainpedia.org/glossary/jnana-avaraṇiya-karma (seen in a search result)
- Vijay K. Jain, Tattvarthasutra with Sarvarthasiddhi in English (free, PhilArchive): https://philarchive.org/rec/JAICUT-2 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is (HarperCollins, 1994), or S. A. Jain, Reality (Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi in English, Calcutta, 1960). For the doctrine in context: Padmanabh S. Jaini, The Jaina Path of Purification (University of California Press, 1979), and Paul Dundas, The Jains (Routledge, 2nd ed. 2002).

Background: In Jain teaching karma is not a moral ledger. It is fine matter (pudgala) that fills the world. The soul takes it in through its activity, and it clings because of the passions: anger, pride, deceit and greed. Chapter 8 sorts it into eight kinds. Four, the ghatiya karmas, damage the soul's own qualities: knowledge, perception, bliss and energy. The other four decide things like length of life, body and status. The word for the first kind is jnanavarana, 'knowledge-covering', so the essay's 'veil' is close to a literal translation. The soul's nature is infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and energy, and when the four damaging karmas are destroyed the soul becomes omniscient (kevali). That fits the essay's 'the perfection was there all along'. Differences to be fair about: the Jain veil is material, the soul stays a separate substance, there is no creator and no whole, and the cure is shedding karma by restraint and austerity. The earlier research notes that the first of the fourteen stages is mithyatva, wrong belief, a form of delusion karma, which comes closer to 'ignorance' than anything else in the system. The match with the axiom is the structure: a capacity that was intact all along and is covered, and a way of removing the cover.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Karma as dust is not a veil of ignorance.' Fair. The Jain veil is literal matter. What matches is the structure of a capacity that is intact and covered, and the essay says Jainism describes it 'in other words'.
- 'The cloud and sun picture is not in the sutra.' Right. It comes from later Jain teaching and the commentary tradition, and the sutra supplies the covering karma itself.

## Tattvartha Sutra chapter 10: the liberated soul rising (Umasvati, roughly 2nd to 5th century CE; Pujyapada's commentary about the 6th century)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: Chapter 10 is the chapter on liberation (moksha) in the Tattvartha Sutra. It says that when all karma is gone the soul rises to the top of the universe and stays there.

In the essay: 'The arrow': 'Jain teaching has a liberated soul rise by its own nature, like a gourd released from its clay, to the summit of the universe.' Also the table row 'The return' (Jainism: 'Moksha: the soul freed from karma, knowing all things'). Sources section: 'chapter 10 (the liberated soul)'.

Where it sits: Tattvartha Sutra 10.1 to 10.2 (omniscience, liberation as the destruction of all karma); 10.5 to 10.7 in the Digambara numbering (the upward motion, the four reasons, the four images); 10.8 (why it stops). The Svetambara text numbers these differently, so say which version you use.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase: after liberation the soul goes straight up to the end of the universe, for four reasons (the push of earlier practice, loss of attachment, severing of bondage, and its own natural upward motion), illustrated in Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi by the potter's wheel, the gourd freed of its clay coating that rises through water, the castor seed that springs from its pod, and the flame. It stops at the edge because there is no medium of motion beyond.

- Vijay K. Jain, Tattvarthasutra with Sarvarthasiddhi in English (free, PhilArchive): https://philarchive.org/rec/JAICUT-2 (seen in a search result)

- Jainworld, Tattvartha Sutra Sanskrit text (needs a Devanagari font; it surfaced for the chapter 10 search, open it to confirm the chapter): https://jainworld.jainworld.com/phil/tatsut13.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: S. A. Jain, Reality: English translation of Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (Vira Sasana Sangha, Calcutta, 1960) for the Digambara reading with the gourd image; Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is (HarperCollins, 1994) for the Svetambara text. Jaini, The Jaina Path of Purification (University of California Press, 1979), for the doctrine of the siddhas.

Background: Chapter 10 is the shortest chapter of the Tattvartha Sutra. It starts with omniscience, which comes when delusion and the knowledge-, perception- and obstruction-covering karmas are destroyed, and defines liberation as the complete destruction of karma. The liberated soul then goes straight up to the end of the universe (loka). The sutra gives four reasons: the impetus of earlier practice (a potter's wheel keeps turning after the potter lets go), loss of attachment, the severing of bondage (a castor seed pops from its pod), and the soul's natural upward motion (a flame). In the Digambara commentary of Pujyapada the standard images are the wheel, the gourd freed of its clay coating, the seed and the flame, in sutras 10.5 to 10.7 (a search summary gives the gourd as 10.7). Pujyapada's point is that karma weighs the soul down and does not cause its rise. The soul stops at the top of the cosmos, in the abode of the liberated (siddhashila), because beyond it there is no medium of motion. Siddhas keep their individuality, so Jain liberation is not absorption. The picture suits the essay's image of rising when weight falls away. It does not suit the idea of going beyond the universe or merging, and the essay does not claim it does.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Jain liberation does not go anywhere beyond the cosmos.' True. The soul stops at the top of the universe. The essay uses the image only for rising once the weight is gone.
- 'Which numbering are you quoting?' The Digambara one for 10.5 to 10.7; the Svetambara version numbers the sutras differently. Name the edition when you quote.

## The Jain verse of forgiveness (Khamemi savve jive) (Liturgical verse in Prakrit, recited in the annual Pratikramana; date of composition not established here)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A four line Prakrit verse recited at the end of the Samvatsari Pratikramana, the confession and repentance rite that closes the Jain festival of Paryushana (Svetambara). Digambaras keep the same practice as Kshamavani, the day after their ten-day festival.

In the essay: 'Karma, or the fingers': 'The Jain verse of forgiveness says "I forgive all beings, may all beings forgive me", and Jainism builds its ethic of non-harm on the equality of souls.' Also the table (Jainism, 'What you do to another': 'I forgive all beings'). The Sources section has no entry for the verse itself.

Where it sits: Pratikramana (Avashyaka) liturgy; recited at Samvatsari (Svetambara) and as Kshamavani (Digambara). I have not located its earliest canonical source line.

The passage: Prakrit as shown on Wikipedia's Kshamavani page and in a search summary: 'Khamemi savve jive, savve jiva khamantu me, mitti me savva bhuesu, veram majjha na kenai.' English, in the common form (translations differ slightly): I forgive all living beings; may all beings forgive me; I am in friendship with all; I hold enmity toward none. The festival greeting 'Micchami Dukkadam' means 'may the wrong I have done be fruitless'.

Read it free:
- Wikipedia, Kshamavani: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kshamavani (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Samvatsari: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samvatsari (seen in a search result)
- The Paryushana Parva (Colorado State University, Jain festival page): https://www.cs.colostate.edu/%7emalaiya/paryushan.html (seen in a search result)
- JAINA, Paryushana page: https://www.jaina.org/page/09_02_18_ParyushanD8 (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: For the practice in context: Paul Dundas, The Jains (Routledge, 2nd ed. 2002), and John E. Cort, Jains in the World (Oxford University Press, 2001). For the Pratikramana text in translation, a published Jain Vishva Bharati or Jain Study Centre edition; I did not identify one in this session.

Background: Paryushana is an eight-day (Svetambara) festival of fasting and repentance in late summer, and it ends on Samvatsari with the Pratikramana and this verse. People then ask each other for forgiveness in person and by message, with the greeting Micchami Dukkadam. Digambaras celebrate Kshamavani on the day after their ten-day festival (Das Lakshana). The verse is built so that the speaker moves first: the second line asks all beings to forgive me, which is the Jain version of the inner agency Part one calls saying sorry first. The Jain reason is also karmic. Unrepented harm is believed to make the bondage of karma harder to shed, so repair is a practical act and not a courtesy (a Jain account summarised in the search). It is meant for all beings, down to the one-sensed beings in earth and water, since every soul counts and is equal in nature. I have not found the verse's earliest source line, and I would say 'a verse from the Pratikramana liturgy' and not 'a canonical verse' until you have checked. A related Jain idea sometimes quoted with it is that one should forgive one's own soul first, but I did not find a source for that and would not use it.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Is this canonical or just liturgy?' It is liturgy, recited every year. I have not traced the first line to a canonical text, so I call it a verse from the Pratikramana.
- 'Does it show Jains think everyone is one?' It shows they think everyone counts and is alike in nature. Their doctrine keeps the souls many.

## Anekantavada and the Jain use of the elephant story (Vidyananda, Mallisena) (Doctrine old and developed over centuries; Vidyananda about 9th century; Mallisena 13th century on the usual dating (one source gives the 12th))

Background: Anekantavada goes with two tools. Nayas are standpoints, each of which sees one aspect. The classical thinkers include Siddhasena Divakara, Samantabhadra, Haribhadra, Akalanka and Hemachandra. The blind men and the elephant (andha-gaja-nyaya) is the stock illustration. The check found it credited to Vidyananda (9th century) and Mallisena (13th century), and I saw Mallisena's use only second hand and could not confirm the verse in Vidyananda's text. Mallisena says whoever takes a partial view and denies the other aspects falls under the maxim. The Jain use is not 'all views are equally true'. Each view is partial and becomes wrong when it denies the others, and Jain authors used it to place rival schools, among them Buddhist and Advaita positions, as one-sided. Shankara attacks anekanta in his Brahma Sutra commentary (2.2.33 and after, from memory). The essay is right to say it is borrowing a picture.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Doesn't anekantavada mean every religion is true?' No. The classical use says each view is partial and errs when it denies the rest, and Jain authors still ranked other schools. The essay borrows the picture and says so.
- 'Isn't the elephant story Jain?' The earliest known version is Buddhist (Udana 6.4). Jain thinkers adopted it later, as the essay says. A claim that it appears in older Jain scripture did not hold up in what I found.

Background: Jainism is a deliberately pluralist system. Souls are many, eternal and uncreated. For the essay: Jainism is not a witness for the first clause, and the table says as much. It is a witness for the veil, the staged return, rebirth until freed, and an ethic of non-harm that rests on likeness.

- 'Why quote them at all, then?' Because the claim is that the same few ideas turn up in different vocabularies, and the essay says where a tradition differs.

Background: Jain cosmology is a vast, beginningless wheel. Background: The Druze faith began in Cairo around 1017 to 1021 around the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim and the missionary Hamza ibn Ali. It grew out of Ismaili Islam and became a separate religion that closed its doors to converts. The Druze call themselves Unitarians (muwahhidun). The full doctrine is guarded: the initiated (uqqal) know more than the uninitiated (juhhal). Reincarnation (taqammus) is the part they openly profess. Hitti noted that it is very popular and held in the open, unlike other tenets. The modern view, from fieldwork and press reports, is that the soul moves at the moment of death into a newborn, that the body is like a shirt (qamis) the soul changes, and that children's talk of past lives (nutq) is taken seriously. Hitti's catechism also shows an older strand in which unbelievers return as animals, but current belief is human to human. Hitti argued that taken to its end the doctrine makes paradise, hell and a last judgment unnecessary. I recall that many of Stevenson's Lebanese cases came from Druze families (from memory), and that is where the essay's later point about cultural expectation applies. The Druze are a clear case of a rebirth doctrine that grew up inside an Islamic world that mostly rejects it. Because the inner teaching is secret, most public information comes from outsiders and from popular belief.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Is that really Islam?' The Druze are a separate religion with Ismaili Islamic roots, and many Muslims do not count them as Muslims. The essay lists them with the minority groups that accepted rebirth.
- 'How can you know a secret religion?' The belief in immediate rebirth is professed openly and has been reported since Hitti. The deeper teaching is guarded, and the essay claims only the open belief.

## Zhuangzi, chapter 2: the butterfly dream (Zhuang Zhou, traditionally about 369 to 286 BCE; the 33-chapter text was edited by Guo Xiang around 300 CE (from memory))

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: The Zhuangzi is, with the Daodejing, one of the two founding texts of philosophical Daoism. The first seven 'Inner Chapters' are generally thought to be the core and the closest to Zhuang Zhou himself. Chapter 2 (Qiwulun) is about the relativity of viewpoints and ends with the butterfly dream.

In the essay: 'Why separation feels so real': 'Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, woke up, and couldn't tell whether he was a man who had dreamed of a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming it was a man, though the text adds that there must be some distinction, which it calls the transformation of things.' Sources: 'Zhuangzi, chapter 2.' The essay's intro also names Daoist among the traditions quoted.

Where it sits: Zhuangzi chapter 2 (Qiwulun, 'Discussion on Making All Things Equal' in Watson), the last paragraph. The Chinese line: 周與胡蝶，則必有分矣。此之謂物化。 (seen in the check).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of the passage: Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, happy and not knowing he is Zhou; he wakes, solidly Zhou; he cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamed of a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming it is Zhou. 'Between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called the transformation of things.' The Chinese line quoted above is exact (seen in the check and a search). The English wording is Watson's as I remember it, and I did not see it in full in this session.

Read it in a book: Burton Watson, The Complete Works of Zhuangzi (Columbia University Press, 2013; the earlier Complete Works of Chuang Tzu, 1968). A. C. Graham, Chuang-tzu: The Inner Chapters (Allen and Unwin, 1981; Hackett, 2001) is the best for scholarly depth. Brook Ziporyn, Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings (Hackett, 2009), is the most readable. Victor Mair, Wandering on the Way (Bantam, 1994). James Legge, The Texts of Taoism, SBE 39 to 40 (Oxford, 1891), is public domain.

Background: Chapter 2 is about how every standpoint feels like the truth from within it. Its last paragraph is the butterfly dream, and it is less a metaphysical argument than a demonstration. Zhuang Zhou cannot settle which state is the real one, and the text ends by saying there must be a distinction, which it calls the transformation of things (wuhua). Specialists point out that the passage does not claim that the waking world is false, and it does not name a true self that sits behind the dream. It is about moving between perspectives, each fully real while one is in it, and the essay's careful wording ('though the text adds that there must be some distinction') follows that. Earlier in the same chapter there are lines closer to the axiom. The Chinese 天地與我並生，而萬物與我為一 is usually rendered 'heaven and earth were born together with me, and the ten thousand things are one with me', and there is a passage about a 'great awakening' after which life is seen as a great dream (from memory). Use the butterfly for the dream quality of the felt self, and those lines for oneness. Daoist thought has no doctrine of karma or rebirth in this text, so Zhuangzi supports the veil and the oneness, and not the lives.

If someone pushes back:
- 'Isn't this just a dream argument?' Partly. The passage keeps a distinction between Zhou and the butterfly and is about shifting perspectives, not about the world being unreal. The essay says as much.
- 'Is Zhuangzi a witness for oneness?' Better than the butterfly passage is the line 'heaven and earth were born together with me, and the ten thousand things are one with me' in the same chapter. Check a translation before you quote it.

## Daodejing 42 (the Way gives birth to one) (Attributed to Laozi; the text took shape in the fourth to third century BCE)

Kind: text. Status: unchecked.

Who: The Daodejing is the foundational text of Daoism, a short collection of verses in 81 chapters. The traditional author is Laozi; scholars date the text to the fourth or third century BCE on the evidence of the Guodian and Mawangdui manuscripts.

In the essay: In 'Why this idea is worth a closer look', Daoism bullet: in the Daodejing the Way gives birth to one, one gives birth to two, and the sequence runs on to the ten thousand things.

Where it sits: Daodejing 42 in the standard (Wang Bi) numbering.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase of a common rendering: the Way gave birth to one, one gave birth to two, two gave birth to three, and three gave birth to the ten thousand things. The wording differs between translators, and some say produces or generates.

Read it free:
- Chinese Text Project, Dao De Jing, with English translation: https://ctext.org/dao-de-jing (not checked)

Read it in a book: Robert G. Henricks, Lao-Tzu: Te-Tao Ching (Ballantine, 1989); Philip J. Ivanhoe, The Daodejing of Laozi (Hackett, 2003); Roger Ames and David Hall, Dao De Jing (Ballantine, 2003).

Background: The chapter is a short cosmogony: the many come from the one, and the one from the Way, which can't be named. Commentators read the one as primal unity or original breath, the two as yin and yang, and the three as their harmonized union, though the text does not say so. It does not say that we are one. The nearest Daoist text that says something like it is the Zhuangzi, in the Zhuangzi entry above. The essay uses chapter 42 only for the direction of travel, many from one.

If someone pushes back:
- Does the Daodejing say all is one? It says all things come from one, and later chapters speak of returning to the root. The essay claims no more than that.
- Is Daoism a religion? Both, in different periods: a philosophy of the early texts and an organized religion with priests and temples from the second century CE. The essay quotes the texts.
