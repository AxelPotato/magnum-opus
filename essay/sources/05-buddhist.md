## Udana 6.4, the Tittha Sutta (the blind men and the elephant) (Pali canon, Khuddaka Nikaya, in the Udana; written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE; the Udana is usually counted among the older books of the Khuddaka Nikaya)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Udana is a collection of short episodes in the Pali canon, each ending with a verse the Buddha speaks on the spot (udana means inspired utterance). Ud 6.4, the Tittha Sutta or Sectarians (1), carries one of the earliest known versions of the story of the blind men and the elephant.

In the essay: Part two, 'The same things in different words': the Buddha tells of blind men who feel an elephant and quarrel, as an image of sectarians who each hold one side. The essay adds that Jain thinkers and Rumi reuse the story and that it was not told to say all religions are one. Sources list: Udana 6.4.

Where it sits: Udana 6.4 (Tittha Sutta, 'Sectarians (1)'); chapter 6 of the Udana, which I recall is called the Jaccandha-vagga (those born blind). The parable and the closing verse come after the monks report the quarrel.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Monks return from the alms round in Savatthi and report that wanderers of other sects are quarrelling over views (for example whether the self and the world are eternal or not, finite or not). The Buddha tells of a king who has men blind from birth gathered and shown an elephant. One is shown the head, one the ear, one the tusk, the trunk, the body, the foot, and each says an elephant is like only that. They fight with their fists and the king is delighted. The closing verse says that some recluses and brahmans, tightly attached to their own views, each see only one side and quarrel.

Background: In the sutta, monks come back from the alms round in Savatthi and report that wanderers of other sects are quarrelling over views, such as whether the self and the world are eternal or not, finite or not. The Buddha answers with the parable of the blind men and the elephant. Note who is who. The king is not the Buddha, and the elephant is not named as the truth. The blind men are the sectarians, and the closing verse faults them for holding to a view and taking it for the whole. So the original point is against clinging to views. It does not say that every view is partly right, and it does not offer a way to join religions. Most Buddhists would add that the Buddha's own teaching is meant as the way out of view-clinging, which is different from being one view among several. The pluralist use ('each religion touches one part') is later and mostly modern. Jain thinkers used the story for their doctrine of many-sidedness (Vidyananda around the ninth century and Mallisena in the thirteenth, as Wikipedia lists them). Rumi has an elephant in a dark house in Masnavi III, and Sana'i told a version before him. Anandajoti notes that the Pali verse plays on the word for seeing one limb or part (ekangadassino). The essay already says it is borrowing the picture and not the meaning. Keep that stance when you talk about it.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't the story say the Buddha thinks everyone has only part of the truth? Not in the sutta. It says these particular sectarians are quarrelling over views they cling to. Whether the Buddha's own teaching is the whole elephant is left open by the text, and Buddhists answer it differently. The essay uses it as a picture and says so.
- Is this really a Buddhist story, or Indian folklore? It is in the Pali canon, which makes it Buddhist in the plainest sense, and versions turn up in Jain and Persian writing. Where it began, I have not checked.

## Dependent arising, ignorance (avidya) and emptiness in the early discourses (Pali discourses (the Buddha, roughly fifth century BCE); Nagarjuna's equation of emptiness with dependent arising, around the second century CE)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: Dependent arising (Pali paticcasamuppada, Sanskrit pratityasamutpada) is the Buddha's account of how suffering arises and ends, as a chain of conditions that starts with ignorance. Emptiness (sunnata, sunyata) is the word for what the chain shows: no thing or self stands on its own.

In the essay: Part two, 'The same things in different words' and 'Why separation feels so real': 'the Buddha with emptiness and dependent arising', 'Ignorance (avidya)' as the first link in the chain that keeps suffering going, and the table cell 'Dependent arising: nothing stands alone.' The essay also notes that Advaita's avidya is 'a word Buddhism uses too'.

Where it sits: SN 12.1 (Paticcasamuppada) and SN 12.2 (the analysis of the twelve links); Ud 1.1 to 1.3 (the Buddha reflecting on dependent arising after awakening); MN 38 for 'when this exists, that comes to be'; SN 35.85 (Suñña Sutta, 'Empty is the world'). Later: Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The twelve links run from ignorance to formations, consciousness, name and form, the six senses, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing and death. The formula is 'when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises'. In SN 35.85 the world is called empty because it is empty of a self or of anything belonging to a self. Nagarjuna says whatever is dependently arisen is explained as emptiness.

- SuttaCentral, SN 12.1 (standard address, not seen in a search): https://suttacentral.net/sn12.1 (not checked)
- SuttaCentral, SN 12.2 (standard address, not seen in a search): https://suttacentral.net/sn12.2 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Connected Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 2000), the Nidana-vagga for SN 12 and the Salayatana-vagga for SN 35. Jay L. Garfield, The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way (Oxford University Press, 1995) for Nagarjuna. Mark Siderits, Buddhism as Philosophy (Hackett, 2007).

Background: Dependent arising is the Buddha's account of how suffering comes about and how it stops. The twelve links run from ignorance through formations, consciousness, name and form, the six senses, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming and birth, to ageing and death. The core formula is 'when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises' (MN 38 and elsewhere). In Udana 1.1 to 1.3 the Buddha, just awakened, goes through it forwards and backwards. Ignorance (Pali avijja, Sanskrit avidya) here means not seeing the four noble truths and how conditioning works. In Advaita the same word means not knowing that you are Brahman. The word is shared and the meaning is not. Emptiness in the early texts shows up in SN 35.85: the world is called empty because it is empty of a self or of anything belonging to a self. Nagarjuna later equated emptiness with dependent arising (MMK 24.18), meaning that things lack intrinsic nature. 'Nothing stands alone' is a fair gloss of dependent arising. But specialists such as Jay Garfield and Mark Siderits separate interdependence from a One or a Whole. Dependence says things exist through causes, parts and labelling. It does not say there is one reality underneath. 'Interbeing' is Thich Nhat Hanh's coinage. So in this row the traditions agree on structure (a built self, a chain, an exit) and differ on whether anything unifies the many.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't avidya an Advaita word? Both traditions use it. In the Pali it is avijja, the first link of the chain, ignorance of the four noble truths. In Advaita it is not knowing your identity with Brahman. The essay only says Buddhism uses the word too, which is true. Do not let it suggest the same meaning.
- Doesn't the Buddha reject 'everything is one'? I recall SN 12.48 (the Lokayatika Sutta) listing 'everything is a unity' and 'everything is a plurality' as two extremes, with dependent arising as the middle. I have not seen the text this session, so read it before you discuss this.

## The Heart Sutra (Prajnaparamita Hridaya) (Mahayana; the earliest dated text is Xuanzang's Chinese of 649 CE, and the earliest dated copy is a stele of 661; the origin of the Sanskrit is disputed)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A very short Mahayana text, recited daily in many East Asian and Tibetan traditions. The bodhisattva Avalokitesvara, practising deep wisdom, sees that the five aggregates are empty and tells Sariputra so. It ends in a mantra, 'gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha' (gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond, awakening).

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'The Heart Sutra says that form is emptiness and emptiness is form, which Buddhists read as saying that things have no fixed independent nature of their own: the Buddhist way of saying that the walls between us are not made of anything solid.' Sources list: the Heart Sutra, with no translation named.

Where it sits: The central lines near the start: Sanskrit rupam sunyata, sunyataiva rupam ('form is emptiness, emptiness is form') and the line that emptiness is not other than form. The Sanskrit spelling is as recalled in the CHECKLIST, not seen on a page this session.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Form is emptiness, and emptiness is form; emptiness is not other than form, form is not other than emptiness; the same is true of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Later the text says there is no ignorance and no ending of ignorance, and so on through the twelve links.

Read it free:
- Wikipedia, Heart Sutra (translations, history, the Nattier debate): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_Sutra (seen in a search result)
- Jan Nattier, 'The Heart Sutra: A Chinese Apocryphal Text?' (1992), PDF copy: https://www.silkqin.com/02qnpu/40tyxs/ty28skj/ty28nattier.pdf (seen in a search result)
- Jayarava Attwood, 'The Chinese Origins of the Heart Sutra Revisited' (record): https://philarchive.org/rec/ATTTCO-4 (seen in a search result)
- Thich Nhat Hanh, commentary on his new Heart Sutra translation (Plum Village PDF): https://dev.plumvillage.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Thich-Nhat-Hanh-Commentary-on-new-Heart-Sutra-translation.pdf (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Edward Conze, Buddhist Wisdom Books (Allen and Unwin, 1958; the Heart Sutra with the Diamond Sutra and commentary). Red Pine, The Heart Sutra (Counterpoint, 2004). Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (Parallax, 1988). Donald S. Lopez, The Heart Sutra Explained (SUNY Press, 1988). Garfield's Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way for the philosophy behind it.

Background: The Heart Sutra is a few hundred characters long and is chanted daily in many temples. Emptiness (sunyata) in the Prajnaparamita and Madhyamaka tradition means lack of intrinsic nature (svabhava): things exist, but only in dependence on causes, parts and the mind's labelling. So 'form is emptiness' does not mean the world is unreal or that it is one. It means nothing stands on its own. The essay's gloss, no fixed independent nature, is the classical one. The sutra then goes through the Buddhist lists (the aggregates, the twelve links, the four truths) and says there is none of them, including no ignorance and no ending of ignorance. That matters here: the same text that gives the veil its place also says the veil lacks a fixed nature. A caution on the text itself. Jan Nattier argued in 1992 (Journal of the International Association of Buddhist Studies 15.2) that the Sanskrit is a back-translation from Xuanzang's Chinese, which was assembled from a passage in a larger Prajnaparamita text. Ishii Kosei and others dispute parts of this, and a 2021 study applied her method to the whole text. The matter is unsettled, so say 'the Heart Sutra' and name your translation, and avoid saying 'the Sanskrit original' unless you want that debate. Thich Nhat Hanh reads emptiness as 'interbeing' (empty of a separate self, full of everything). That is a teacher's reading, and classical Madhyamaka would not equate emptiness with fullness or a whole.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't emptiness just oneness in other words? No. Madhyamaka calls even a One empty, and Buddhists more often say 'not two' than 'one'. The essay's gloss, no fixed independent nature, is the careful one.
- Did Xuanzang write it, or is it Indian? Nattier thinks the Sanskrit derives from his Chinese. Others dispute that. It is unsettled, and nothing in the essay depends on it.

## Huayan Buddhism and the jewel net of Indra (Fazang; Francis Cook) (Huayan school in China, seventh and eighth centuries; Fazang 643 to 712; Cook's book 1977)

Kind: tradition. Status: partly checked.

Who: Huayan (Japanese Kegon) is a Chinese Buddhist school built on the Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra. Fazang, its third patriarch, lectured at the court of Empress Wu and made the school's teaching vivid with images such as a golden lion statue and Indra's net. Francis Cook's Hua-yen Buddhism: The Jewel Net of Indra (1977) is the standard English introduction.

In the essay: Part two, 'The axiom and the machine': 'The Huayan school of Buddhism pictured the universe as a net of jewels in which each jewel reflects all the others.' Also the table row 'The one whole' and the Sources list (Cook, 1977).

Where it sits: The jewel net is credited to the first patriarch Dushun and developed by Fazang, notably in his Treatise on the Golden Lion (Taisho 1881) and in his commentaries on the Avatamsaka Sutra. In Cook, look at the chapters on Indra's net and on the mutual identity and interpenetration of all things (check the contents page).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Picture a net over the palace of the god Indra with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects all the others, and each reflection reflects all the others in turn, without end. Fazang's golden lion makes the same point: the gold is wholly present in every hair of the lion, and each hair contains the whole lion. Summaries give the formula 'one is all, and all is one'.

Read it free:
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Huayan Buddhism: https://plato.stanford.edu/Entries/buddhism-huayan/ (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Indra's net: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra%27s_net (seen in a search result)
- Penn State University Press page for Cook's book (out of print; use a library): https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/0-271-02190-X.html (seen in a search result)
- CBETA (Chinese Buddhist Electronic Texts), Taisho 1881, Fazang's Golden Lion essay in Chinese (site and text number only; I did not confirm it hosts a translation):  (not checked)

Read it in a book: Francis H. Cook, Hua-yen Buddhism: The Jewel Net of Indra (Penn State University Press, 1977; 164 pages). Fazang's Golden Lion essay is, I believe, translated in Wing-tsit Chan, A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy (Princeton, 1963), which I have not checked. Thomas Cleary, Entry into the Inconceivable (University of Hawaii Press, 1983), and his Flower Ornament Scripture (Shambhala, 1984 to 1987) for the sutra.

Background: Huayan is the nearest Buddhist school to the axiom, and a specialist would say so and add a warning. Its core teaching is that everything contains everything. Fazang's golden lion makes the point with a statue: the gold is wholly present in every hair, and each hair contains the whole lion. The jewel net makes it with reflections. The doctrine is often called the non-obstruction of phenomena with phenomena. It rests on dependent arising and emptiness, so each jewel is what it is only through the others. The warning: the picture is of many jewels reflecting one another with no source jewel. It is mutual interpenetration of many things, which is a different structure from one whole that looks at itself through parts. Strands of Chinese Buddhism that speak of One Mind come through the Awakening of Faith, and Japanese Critical Buddhism attacks that line of thought as importing a substance. Cook himself calls Huayan a cosmic ecology and an organic unity (the publisher's wording). As for origin, the net image is attributed to Dushun and taken up by Fazang. I have not checked whether the Avatamsaka Sutra itself has this exact net, so say 'Huayan teachers' and not 'the sutra says'. I also recall that Fazang demonstrated the idea to the empress with mirrors set around a Buddha image and a lamp, but I have not seen that in this session.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't this just ecology in religious clothing? Cook himself uses the ecology comparison. The school goes further and says each part contains the whole.
- Is Huayan oneness or plurality? Both are said, held in mutual containment. It is fair to call it the closest Buddhist school to the axiom, and not the same as it, because there is no single source behind the jewels.

## Buddhist views of self and no-self (anatta) (Pali discourses (roughly fifth century BCE); the modern scholarly debate runs from the 1950s on)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: A cluster of teachings, not a single text. The Buddha analyses a person into five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness), says none of them is a permanent self, and treats the belief in such a self as a root of clinging. The argument since is over what follows: that there is no self at all, that the question is set aside as unhelpful, or (in some Mahayana texts) that a true self exists as buddha-nature.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'In Buddhism the belief in a permanent personal self is the first fetter a stream enterer drops, and the self is treated as something assembled from parts, which is why it can be let go of.' And in 'Not enough time in one life': Buddhism 'holds that there is no fixed soul, and yet that something continues'.

Where it sits: SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhana Sutta, the not-self discourse); SN 5.10 (Vajira Sutta, the chariot simile, where 'a being' is a label for assembled parts); SN 44.10 (Ananda Sutta, the Buddha's silence when Vacchagotta asks about a self); AN 10.13 for the fetter sakkaya-ditthi. SN 22.59 and SN 5.10 are from memory; SN 44.10 and AN 10.13 were seen in search results.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. In SN 44.10 the wanderer Vacchagotta asks whether there is a self and then whether there is no self, and the Buddha is silent both times. He tells Ananda that answering yes would side with those who hold the self eternal, and answering no would side with the annihilationists, and would further confuse an already bewildered questioner.

Read it free:
- Access to Insight, SN 44.10 (Thanissaro), 'On Self, No Self, and Not-self': https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn44/sn44.010.than.html (seen in a search result)
- Dhamma.gift, SN 44.10 in Bodhi's translation: https://dhamma.gift/bw/sn/sn44.10.html (seen in a search result)
- Thanissaro, 'The Not-Self Strategy' (Access to Insight): https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/notselfstrategy.html (seen in a search result)
- Tricycle, 'Is there no self?' (on the silence): https://tricycle.org/magazine/there-no-self/ (seen in a search result)
- SuttaCentral, SN 22.59 (standard address, not seen in a search): https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59 (not checked)
- SuttaCentral, SN 5.10 (standard address, not seen in a search): https://suttacentral.net/sn5.10 (not checked)

Read it in a book: Walpola Rahula, What the Buddha Taught (1959). Steven Collins, Selfless Persons (Cambridge University Press, 1982). Rupert Gethin, The Foundations of Buddhism (Oxford University Press, 1998). Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Connected Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 2000). Thanissaro's Selves and Not-self is free at dhammatalks.org.

Background: The mainstream scholarly reading, held by Walpola Rahula, Steven Collins and Richard Gombrich, is that early Buddhism denies any abiding self. Two other readings matter for a conversation. Thanissaro Bhikkhu argues that not-self is a strategy: you use it to let go of identification with every phenomenon until clinging stops, and the Buddha declined to say flatly that there is no self (his essay The Not-Self Strategy). The key text is SN 44.10, the silence. Bhikkhu Bodhi accepts the silence but reads it as avoiding annihilationism, not as hinting at a hidden self; a SuttaCentral thread is titled as a Bodhi versus Thanissaro debate. A third reading is Mahayana: the Mahaparinirvana Sutra calls the buddha-nature a true self, and Critical Buddhism rejects that as not Buddhist. What matters for the axiom is that none of these Buddhist readings says there is one Self shared by all. 'No separate self' is safe wording. 'One cosmic Self' is not, and the Buddhist side would say the Upanishadic atman is what the Buddha was arguing against, though some modern comparers (Coomaraswamy, for example) read him as denying only the small self. The essay's wording, a self assembled from parts that can be let go, matches the aggregates and the chariot simile. The Milindapanha (see its entry) develops the chariot. The essay claims the shared move, a built separate self that can end, and not a shared doctrine.

If someone pushes back:
- So Buddhism denies the self and the Upanishads affirm it. Doesn't that contradict the essay? On the doctrine, yes. The essay says the traditions use different words and sometimes gods. What it claims in common is the move: a separate self that is constructed and can be let go. It does not claim the doctrines are identical.
- Does the Buddha say 'there is no self'? He says the aggregates are not self. Whether he ever says flatly that no self exists is argued; SN 44.10 is the passage everyone goes back to.

Translation check (2026-10-08): the five aggregates are rupa (form, body), vedana (feeling), sanna (perception), sankhara (volitional formations: intentions and the habits they set), vinnana (consciousness). The essay now says 'body, feelings, perceptions, intentions and habits, and awareness'.

## The four stages of awakening and the ten fetters (AN 10.13; MN 118) (Pali discourses; systematized in the commentaries (Buddhaghosa, fifth century CE))

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: A map in the Pali discourses of four noble persons: the stream-enterer (sotapanna), once-returner (sakadagami), non-returner (anagami) and arahant. Each is defined by which of the ten fetters (samyojana) has been cut.

In the essay: Part two, 'The arrow': 'Early Buddhism has four stages from stream entry to the arahant.' And in 'Why separation feels so real': the belief in a permanent personal self is 'the first fetter a stream enterer drops'. Table: 'The belief in a permanent self is the first fetter to fall.'

Where it sits: AN 10.13 (the ten fetters in two groups of five; the first lower fetter is sakkaya-ditthi); MN 118 (the Buddha names arahants, non-returners, once-returners and stream-enterers among the assembly); Bodhi, In the Buddha's Words, the chapter on the planes of realization.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Five lower fetters: identity view (sakkaya-ditthi), doubt, attachment to rites and observances, sensual desire, ill will. Five higher: desire for form, desire for the formless, conceit, restlessness, ignorance. The stream-enterer has cut the first three and has at most seven more lives. The once-returner has cut the first three and thinned desire and ill will. The non-returner has cut all five lower fetters. The arahant has cut all ten and is not reborn.

Read it free:
- Dhammatalks.org, AN 10.13 (Thanissaro): https://dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN10_13.html (seen in a search result)
- Dhamma.gift, AN 10.13 in Bodhi's translation: https://dhamma.gift/bw/an/an10.13.html (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Fetter (Buddhism): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetter_(Buddhism) (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Fruits of the noble path: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruits_of_the_noble_path (seen in a search result)
- Spirit Rock, the four stages of enlightenment: https://www.spiritrock.org/practice-guides/four-stages-of-enlightenment (seen in a search result)
- Tricycle, 'What is stream entry?': https://tricycle.org/article/what-is-stream-entry/ (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Bhikkhu Bodhi, In the Buddha's Words (Wisdom, 2005). Nanamoli and Bodhi, The Middle Length Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 1995; MN 118). Bodhi, The Numerical Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 2012; AN 10.13).

Background: This is the clearest staged map in the early texts, and the essay's use of it is accurate. Three cautions from the specialist side. First, the stages describe what has been cut, and the texts tie stream entry to seeing the Dhamma and to unshakable confidence in the Buddha, the teaching and the community. It is not a feeling of progress. Second, the first fetter is not simply 'belief in a soul'. Sakkaya-ditthi is translated 'personality view' (Nanamoli), 'personal-existence view' (Bodhi) or 'self-identity view' (Thanissaro). It is the view that one of the five aggregates is the self, or that the self owns or sits in them. Third, the sense of self does not end at stream entry. The conceit 'I am' is a higher fetter and goes only with arahantship. I recall that SN 22.89 (the Khemaka Sutta) says even a non-returner keeps a residue of 'I am', like the scent left in a washed cloth. I have not seen that text this session. So 'the first fetter to fall' is true, and 'the sense of separation ends at stream entry' is not what the texts say. The commentaries also distribute the fetters among the stages differently from the suttas, as Wikipedia notes. The comparison with Gennady's arrow is Alex's own. The Buddhist ladder is a path of insight and release, and it has no stage 'beyond' in the cosmic sense the arrow has. Mahayana adds its own ten bodhisattva stages.

If someone pushes back:
- Is the arrow the same as these four stages? No. They are different maps with different purposes, and the essay says the arrow is the school's own scale and that other traditions draw staged maps too.
- Can anyone verify a stage? The texts list marks of each, but there is no outside test. The essay does not claim one.

## Samyutta Nikaya 36.6, the Sallatha Sutta (the dart, 'the second arrow') (Pali canon, Samyutta Nikaya (Connected Discourses on Feeling))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A short discourse in which the Buddha contrasts how an untrained ordinary person and a trained noble disciple respond to physical pain.

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real', in the paragraph on the method of letting go: 'in the Sallatha Sutta he says that an untrained person feels two pains, the physical one and the mental one added to it, while a trained disciple feels only the first.' Table: 'The second pain you add (SN 36.6).'

Where it sits: SN 36.6 (Sallatha Sutta). Thanissaro and Nyanaponika title it 'The Dart'.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. An untaught ordinary person, touched by bodily pain, sorrows, grieves and laments, and so feels two feelings, bodily and mental, like a man shot with one dart and then with a second. A trained disciple, touched by the same pain, does not sorrow or lament and feels one feeling, the bodily.

Background: This sutta is the source of the popular 'second arrow' teaching. The Pali word is salla, a dart or arrow, and translators differ (Thanissaro and Nyanaponika say dart). The saying 'pain is inevitable, suffering is optional' is modern and is not in the sutta. Two more points from the text, as I recall it and unchecked this session. The sutta goes on to say the untrained person, hit by pain, also looks for relief in sensual pleasure because he knows no other escape, so the second dart sits inside a loop. And it says the trained disciple is free of that loop. The disciple still feels the bodily pain, so nothing is numbed. That is 'released, not relaxed' in early Buddhist terms. Which kind of noble disciple is meant is a question for the commentary. I recall that it reads the passage as someone at the non-returner level or above, since ill will is cut there, but I have not seen that note, so check Bodhi's footnotes. For the axiom: the sutta has no whole and no wall. The match is with the method only, namely that a second pain is added by resistance. That is the 'non-acceptance' of Part two. If you use this with a non-Buddhist audience, say that the sutta presents it as a stage on the path to release from birth and death, and that the therapy reading (do not pile worry onto pain) is a modern and smaller use of the same line.

If someone pushes back:
- Is this just the Buddha saying 'don't catastrophize'? A therapy reading is possible. The sutta frames it as part of the path to release, so the tradition means more than coping. Say both.
- Is the phrase 'two arrows' in the sutta? It says two darts. The arrow wording is a popular rendering.

## Majjhima Nikaya 118, the Anapanasati Sutta (mindfulness of breathing, relinquishment) (Pali canon, Majjhima Nikaya)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A long discourse in which the Buddha teaches sixteen steps of breath-mindfulness, in four groups of four, to a large gathering of monks at Savatthi. (I recall it as a full-moon night at the end of the rains retreat; check.)

In the essay: Part two, 'Why separation feels so real': 'In the Buddha's instructions on breathing, one step is to breathe in and out contemplating relinquishment.' Table: 'Relinquishing, in the breath practice (MN 118).'

Where it sits: MN 118, step sixteen: Pali patinissagganupassi assasissami / passasissami. Nanamoli and Bodhi: 'contemplating relinquishment'. Thanissaro: 'focusing on relinquishment'.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. The fourth group of four steps is contemplating impermanence, fading away, cessation and relinquishment (my recollection of the order; the sixteenth step being relinquishment was seen on Access to Insight and Wikipedia). The sutta says that breath-mindfulness developed this way fulfils the four foundations of mindfulness, which fulfil the seven factors of awakening, which fulfil knowledge and release.

Read it free:
- Access to Insight, MN 118 (Thanissaro): https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.118.than (seen in a search result)
- Wikipedia, Anapanasati Sutta: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80n%C4%81p%C4%81nasati_Sutta (seen in a search result)
- SuttaCentral forum, how to translate patinissaga in MN 118: https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/patinissaga-in-anapanasati-mn-118/20013 (seen in a search result)

Background: The sixteen steps run roughly as follows. First come long and short breaths, experiencing the whole body and calming the bodily formation. Then rapture, pleasure, the mental formation and calming it. Then experiencing the mind, gladdening it, concentrating it and freeing it. That fits Alex's 'released, not relaxed'. I recall that the Pali commentaries read patinissagga as two things at once, giving up the defilements and leaping into nibbana, but I have not checked it. Translators differ ('relinquishment', 'letting go', 'giving back'), and a SuttaCentral thread discusses the choice. The modern 'body scan' is not found as such in this sutta. Breath-mindfulness is used across Theravada and other Buddhist traditions, and I have not checked the Chinese parallels. And the use of breath practice as stress reduction is a later and different purpose from the one the sutta states, which is release.

If someone pushes back:
- Is 'letting go' really what the Buddha means? The English is a loose gloss of patinissagga. The idea is giving up clinging after seeing impermanence, and Thanissaro's 'relinquishment' is closer.
- Isn't this just mindfulness? In the sutta it is a path that ends in release. Mindfulness for stress reduction is a later use.

## Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, the Nibbedhika Sutta (intention is kamma) (Pali canon, Anguttara Nikaya (the Book of the Sixes))

Kind: text. Status: checked.

Who: A discourse on 'penetrative' knowledge. Six things are each to be understood by their origin, their diversity, their result, their cessation and the path to their cessation: sensuality, feeling, perception, the effluents, kamma and stress.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers': 'In the Buddhist account nobody does [administer karma], and the Buddha said that it is intention he calls karma.' Sources list: Anguttara Nikaya 6.63.

Where it sits: AN 6.63, in the section on kamma. Pali: cetanaham bhikkhave kammam vadami.

The passage: Intention, I tell you, is kamma. Intending, one does kamma by way of body, speech, & intellect. (Thanissaro's translation, as shown on Access to Insight's kamma page and in a search result this session. Sujato renders cetana as 'choice' and another translator as 'volition'.)

Read it free:
- Access to Insight, AN 6.63 (Thanissaro): https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.063.than (seen in a search result)
- Access to Insight, the kamma page that quotes AN 6.63: https://accesstoinsight.org/ptf/dhamma/sacca/sacca4/samma-ditthi/kamma.html (seen in a search result)

Background: The line was a deliberate position in its time. Brahmanical ritual religion treated karma largely as correct ritual action, and I recall from MN 56 (the Upali Sutta) that the Jain leader Nigantha Nataputta treated bodily action as the weightiest kind. The Buddha defines kamma by the mental event behind the act, cetana: intention, volition or choice depending on the translator. Do not read it as 'good intentions are enough'. The same sutta goes on to say that kamma has results in this life, the next or later, that results differ by realm, and that kamma ends where contact ends, by the noble eightfold path. A SuttaCentral thread notes that other suttas also treat kamma by body, speech and mind and that MN 56 gives a fuller account, so a one-line summary can mislead. Who administers it? For the axiom: the essay's reading of karma, 'what I do to another part of the whole, I do to the whole', is its own consequence of the axiom. In the sutta, results come back to the doer through his own stream of intentions, and no whole is mentioned. The essay is right to say the traditions differ here, and you should say so too.

If someone pushes back:
- Does the Buddha say only intention matters, even for accidents? The texts and commentaries distinguish intended from unintended acts and give them different weight, but I have not checked the specifics. MN 56 is the place to read.
- Is karma a punishment? In the sutta there is no judge. Results arise from the action itself, according to conditions.

## The Milindapanha (Questions of King Milinda): Nagasena and the lamp (King Milinda is Menander I, an Indo-Greek ruler of the second century BCE; the dialogue was composed in north-west India, probably in the last centuries BCE or first centuries CE)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A dialogue between the Indo-Greek king Milinda and the Buddhist monk Nagasena. It is outside the Pali canon of the Sri Lankan and Thai Theravada tradition, but it is included in the Burmese canon and Theravada commentators respect it.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': Buddhism 'holds that there is no fixed soul, and yet that something continues, and in the Milindapanha the monk Nagasena compares it to a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same flame nor a different one.' Table: 'Rebirth without a fixed soul, like a flame passed from lamp to lamp.'

Where it sits: Rhys Davids, SBE 35, Book II, the questions on individuality and rebirth (the sacred-texts page sbe3505 contains the lamp burning through the night; the phrase 'neither the same nor another' was seen on the dhammatalks.net copy of the same translation). A second simile, lighting one lamp from another, is described in a journal summary as belonging to the Book III discussion of rebirth without a subject. Check which page carries which image.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Nagasena asks the king whether a lamp lit at dusk can burn all night. It can. Is the flame in the first watch the same as the flame in the middle watch? No. A different one? No. The light came from the same lamp all night. So, he says, one person's moments pass into the next, one arising as another ceases. Asked whether the one who is reborn is the same person or another, he answers 'neither the same nor another' (that phrase seen in a search summary of Rhys Davids's text). The separate image of one lamp lit from another is the one where nothing 'passes over'.

Background: Its most famous move is the chariot: 'Nagasena' is only a name for a collection of parts, as a chariot is a name for its pole, axle and wheels. On rebirth it answers the question 'what passes over?' with 'nothing passes over, yet it is not nothing'. The new life depends on the old as a flame depends on the flame it was lit from, or as curds depend on milk, butter on curds and ghee on butter. Another image is the man who steals mangoes from a tree he did not plant. It is a continuity of cause and effect and not a transmigrating entity. The essay's sentence, 'a flame passed from one lamp to another, neither the same flame nor a different one', blends two images: the lamp that burns through the night and the lamp lit from another lamp. The CHECKLIST's easy fix is to say 'a lamp flame that burns through the night, each moment passing on to the next: neither the same nor another'. I recall that the Chinese Nagasena Bhikshu Sutra is a shorter and perhaps earlier version, and that the Pudgalavada school held a 'person' neither the same as nor different from the aggregates, which other Buddhists attacked as a disguised self. I have not checked either point. Within Theravada, Nagasena's answer is read as pure process.

If someone pushes back:
- If there is no self, who is reborn? Nagasena's answer is that it is neither the same nor another. The next life depends on this one by causation, the way one flame depends on the one it was lit from. Whether that fully answers the question is argued among Buddhists too.
- Is the Milindapanha even scripture? Not in the Sri Lankan and Thai canon. The Burmese canon includes it, and it is quoted with respect. Say 'the Milindapanha' and not 'the Buddha said'.

Translation check (2026-10-08): two lamp images were fused in the old sentence. 'Neither the same nor another' is Nagasena's answer about the reborn person, and the lamp that goes with it burns through the night watches (the flame of one watch is not the flame of the next, yet it is one lamp). The lamp lit from another lamp is a separate simile for rebirth without transmigration: nothing passes across, and the second lamp burns because of the first (Rhys Davids, SBE 35). The essay now gives both.

## Samyutta Nikaya 15, the Anamatagga Samyutta (no beginning; everyone has been your mother) (Pali canon, Samyutta Nikaya (Connected Discourses on the Without Discoverable Beginning))

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: A set of short suttas (SN 15.1 to 15.20, I believe) in which the Buddha says that samsara has no discoverable beginning and illustrates it with very large images.

In the essay: Part two, 'Not enough time in one life': 'In the Samyutta Nikaya the Buddha says that samsara has no discoverable beginning and that it is not easy to find a being who has not at some time been your mother.' Sources list: Samyutta Nikaya 15.14 to 19.

Where it sits: SN 15.1 (no discoverable beginning, seen in Bodhi's translation); SN 15.14 (Mata Sutta, the mother) and SN 15.15 to 15.19 (father, brother, sister, son, daughter, identical except for the relative).

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. 'This samsara is without discoverable beginning' (Bodhi's wording as seen in the CHECKLIST). A first point is not discerned of beings hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving, running on and wandering on. It is not easy to find a being who has not at some time in this long course been your mother, and likewise your father, brother, sister, son or daughter.

Read it free:
- Access to Insight, SN 15.14 Mata Sutta (Thanissaro): https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn15/sn15.014.than.html (seen in a search result)
- Buddhadust, SN 15.1 in Bodhi's translation: https://buddhadust.net/dhamma-vinaya/wp/sn/02_nv/sn02.15.001.bodh.wp.htm (seen in a search result)

Read it in a book: Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Connected Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 2000), the Nidana-vagga, SN 15.

Background: The point of the series in the text is disenchantment, and it is not comfort. The Buddha piles up images. As I recall them, and unchecked: the tears you have shed in this long course are more than the water of the four oceans; the bones left from one being's wandering would make a heap as large as a mountain; the grass and sticks of the land would run out before you ran out of mothers to count. The conclusion in these suttas is that it is enough to become disenchanted with conditioned things, dispassionate toward them, and free of them. The kinship line is a tool for that. It is not at first a call to love. Later Tibetan practice (lojong and lamrim) reuses 'all beings have been my mother' as a base for compassion, which the earlier research notes marked as plausible and not checked. There is a tension with the axiom. The Pali Buddha refuses to speak of a first beginning, while the axiom's second clause, a whole that experiences separation, implies a starting point or at least a ground, and the early texts supply neither. The essay's use (something continues across many lives, and all beings are kin) is faithful. The 'beginning' side is a place where Buddhism does not fit the axiom, and you should say so if asked.

If someone pushes back:
- Doesn't this show that beings are one? It shows that beings are related through endless rounds of life. The text draws the conclusion that one should be disenchanted with samsara. It does not say that there is one whole.
- Does the Buddha say separation has a beginning? He says no beginning is discoverable. That is a direct difference from a story of a whole that begins to experience separation.

Background: Chapter 3 is a liturgy. The practitioner makes offerings, takes refuge, confesses, rejoices, and then takes the vow to awaken for the sake of all beings. After the vow comes this verse, in which he celebrates that his life has been fruitful and that he now belongs to the Buddhas' family. The notion of being born into a spiritual family (gotra, lineage) is common in Mahayana writing. I recall that the Pali texts also call noble disciples 'born of the Dhamma' (dhammaja, for example in DN 27), but I have not checked that. The essay's description, a threshold birth, is accurate. Two cautions. First, this is a rebirth by vow into a lineage, which is a different thing from the Christian 'born again' or the Hindu twice-born, and from the ripening of insight at stream entry in the early texts. Second, in the bodhisattva path this moment comes at the beginning, as the first step of awakening mind. It does not come after the top of a scale, so it cannot be mapped onto Gennady's 'birth' after plus four. The essay places it among 'other traditions', as an image of a decisive change, and that is the right level.

If someone pushes back:
- Is this the same as being born again? It uses a similar image for a different thing: joining a lineage by vow. Each tradition uses the image for something of its own, as the essay says.
- Does the verse say the practitioner has reached a high stage? No. It marks the start of the bodhisattva path.

## Udanavarga 5.18 (the Buddhist golden-rule verse) (Sanskrit Udanavarga, traditionally credited to Dharmatrata; the Tibetan version was translated into English by W. Woodville Rockhill in 1883)

Kind: text. Status: partly checked.

Who: The Udanavarga is the northern Buddhist counterpart of the Pali Dhammapada, a collection of verses arranged by theme. Verse 5.18 is the one cited in golden-rule compilations.

In the essay: Part two, 'Karma, or the fingers': 'Confucius names reciprocity ... and the Mahabharata and the Buddhist Udanavarga have it as well.' Sources list: Udanavarga 5.18.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. Do not hurt others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful. The wording 'Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful' circulates in golden-rule compilations attributed to Udanavarga 5:18, but I could not identify the translator, so I do not mark it exact.

Background: The Udanavarga is not part of the Pali canon but shares many verses with the Dhammapada. The Dhammapada parallel (129 and 130) says that all tremble at the rod, all fear death, life is dear to all, and 'making oneself the example' (attanam upamam katva) one should not kill or cause to kill. Both texts work by comparison: I am like you, so I should not harm you. This is empathy and reciprocity, and it is not an identity claim. A golden-rule survey by Douglas Jacoby classes the Udanavarga line as a 'silver rule', the negative form. The Dhammapada verses are about violence in particular, while the Udanavarga line as quoted is broader. The essay already says that most of these texts 'rest on reciprocity or empathy, and a few go further', and it gives the Isha Upanishad as the stronger case. So this entry supports the essay's narrower claim and does not support oneness. Before you cite the verse in a talk, open Rockhill or Anandajoti and read the actual line. The verse number and the translator's wording are the two things I could not confirm this session.

If someone pushes back:
- Is this the Buddhist golden rule? It is a Buddhist negative golden rule, built on comparison with oneself. It does not say the other is you.
- The verse number differs on other sites. That is why the CHECKLIST flags it. Check the edition before you cite a number.

## Dogen (1200 to 1253) and 'dropping off body and mind' (1200 to 1253; Genjokoan written 1233)

Kind: person. Status: partly checked.

Who: Eihei Dogen was the founder of the Soto school of Zen in Japan. He studied in China from 1223 to 1227 under Tiantong Rujing. His Genjokoan is the best-known short statement of his teaching.

In the essay: The table row 'Letting go' for Buddhism: 'Relinquishing, in the breath practice (MN 118). The second pain you add (SN 36.6). Dropping off body and mind (Dogen).' Dogen appears in the table only. The prose has no paragraph on him and the Sources list has no entry.

Where it sits: Genjokoan, the opening lines; the phrase shinjin datsuraku ('dropping off body and mind') in Dogen's writings (Eihei Koroku, Hokyoki) and in the biographical story of his awakening under Rujing.

The passage: To study the Buddha Way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be enlightened by the ten thousand dharmas. (As printed in a Tricycle article on the Genjokoan; I did not see the translator named. Another translation, in Meeting the Myriad Things by Roberts, Okumura and Redding, has 'actualized by myriad things' for the last verb.)

Read it free:
- Tricycle, Dogen's Genjokoan: https://tricycle.org/magazine/dogen-genjokoan/ (seen in a search result)
- Tricycle, 'The Five Ranks and Dogen's Genjokoan': https://tricycle.org/article/the-five-ranks-and-dogens-genjokoan/ (seen in a search result)
- Taigen Leighton, 'Dropping off body and mind and the pregnant pillars': https://www.ancientdragon.org/dropping-off-body-mind-and-the-pregnant-pillars/ (seen in a search result)
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, entry on Dogen (site and entry name only; no URL seen):  (not checked)

Read it in a book: Kazuaki Tanahashi (ed.), Moon in a Dewdrop: Writings of Zen Master Dogen (North Point Press, 1985; includes Genjokoan). Shohaku Okumura, Realizing Genjokoan (Wisdom, 2010). Shinshu Roberts, Shohaku Okumura and Zuiko Redding, Meeting the Myriad Things (a recent commentary).

Background: The phrase shinjin datsuraku can be translated 'dropping off', 'casting off' or 'shedding' body and mind. Taigen Leighton describes it as a technical term that Dogen uses both for zazen and for his awakening. The traditional story is that Rujing scolded a dozing neighbour by saying that zazen is the dropping off of body and mind, and Dogen awakened on hearing it. The historical status of that story is debated, and one teacher in the sources I saw notes that Dogen himself does not tell it in that form, so treat the details as tradition. Genjokoan puts the idea in a short chain: studying the self leads to forgetting the self, and forgetting the self is being actualized by everything else. 'Body and mind' stands for the whole constructed self, which makes it a good match for the essay's talk of the border of the self. Three cautions. Dogen is thirteenth-century Japanese Zen, a Mahayana lineage far from the Pali texts in the same table cell, and the single 'Buddhism' column blends them, so say 'in Soto Zen'. Dogen insisted that practice and realization are one (I recall the phrase shusho ittō), so 'letting go as a method toward a goal' is not quite how he put it. And translations of the key verb differ (enlightened, actualized, verified), so name the translator.

If someone pushes back:
- Is this the same as MN 118 relinquishment? They are cousins. Both give up clinging to the constructed self. They sit in different traditions, centuries and vocabularies, which the essay accepts.
- Is the Rujing story true? It is the standard biography. Its historical details are debated. Say 'the tradition tells it this way'.

## Nirvana, and the bodhisattva who returns to the marketplace (Ten Ox-Herding Pictures) (Pali texts (nibbana, early); the Ten Ox-Herding Pictures by Kakuan, twelfth-century China, as I recall; Shantideva eighth century)

Kind: concept. Status: partly checked.

Who: Nirvana (Pali nibbana, 'blowing out') is the goal in all Buddhist traditions. The Mahayana bodhisattva ideal adds the figure who postpones final nirvana to help others. The Ten Ox-Herding Pictures are a Chan and Zen series of images ending with a figure who returns to ordinary life to help others.

In the essay: The table row 'The return' for Buddhism: 'Nirvana: the ending of ignorance and craving. In Mahayana, the bodhisattva comes back to the marketplace.' In the prose: 'Buddhism uses other words than the Upanishads for what is left when the ignorance ends, and the move is the same one, the veil coming off.'

Where it sits: SN 38.1 (nibbana as the end of greed, hatred and delusion); SN 56.11 (Dhammacakkappavattana, the four noble truths, cessation of craving); Ud 8.3 ('an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unfabricated', Thanissaro); Kakuan's tenth picture, called 'Entering the marketplace with bliss-bestowing hands' in the CHECKLIST (Reps) and 'Entering the City with Bliss-bestowing Hands' in the Suzuki version seen on a PDF.

The passage (paraphrase, not cleared for quoting): Paraphrase. In the Pali texts nibbana is the ending of greed, hatred and delusion, and is described by negations as unborn and unmade. In the tenth ox-herding picture the one who has found the ox comes back into the town with open hands, and the point is that the awakened person lives among ordinary people and helps them.

Background: In the Pali texts nibbana is the ending of greed, hatred and delusion (SN 38.1). Ud 8.3 describes an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unfabricated. Bhikkhu Bodhi stresses that nibbana is not described as a consciousness or a ground. Some Theravada forest teachers disagree and read it as an unconditioned awareness. Both camps are Theravada, so the question of what is left when ignorance ends is open inside the tradition. This is a place for care. The essay's table cell, 'the ending of ignorance and craving', is safe. The prose sentence about 'what is left' is looser. Advaita says what is left is Brahman, Theravada does not say, and 'a return to knowing itself as one' fits only some Mahayana language (Huayan, buddha-nature, Dzogchen). Nibbana is not a return to anything. The Mahayana bodhisattva ideal is a separate development. Non-abiding nirvana means that wisdom keeps one out of samsara and compassion keeps one out of nirvana (my recollection of the earlier research). Shantideva's vow to stay 'as long as space endures' is at BCA 10.55 in the numbering I recall. The Ox-Herding Pictures are Chan and Zen. Sites disagree on who wrote the text (Kakuan Shien or an unknown author called Puming), and the translations are in copyright, so read them on the hosting pages. The essay already says 'In Mahayana', which is the right frame. Theravada also has a bodhisatta path.

If someone pushes back:
- Isn't nirvana just annihilation? The Pali texts deny that reading and describe it by negations of birth and making. What is left is left unsaid, which is why the essay should not name it.
- Is the marketplace image Buddhist in general? It is Chan and Zen. The essay says Mahayana, and 'Mahayana and Zen' would be more exact.

Translation check (2026-10-08): nibbana/nirvana literally means 'blowing out' or 'quenching', the image of a flame going out (etymology is not settled). The Buddha's standard definition is the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion (SN 45.7, AN 3.55, SN 38.1). The essay now says 'the ending of greed, hatred and delusion nirvana, a word that means a flame going out'.
