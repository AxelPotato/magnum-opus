Part three

Practical

How this is done today, by people with jobs, families and not much spare time.

About 25 minutes of reading

If your life is going well and feels well, you have no need of a method. Part two drew the road back to remembering that we are part of something bigger, and life moves all of us along it anyway, slowly, through the mirror and the turn. Nobody has to finish it in one life. A method is a way to do on purpose, and faster, what life does slowly.

This part is about walking the whole road in one life, past the small square and into the large one, until we are free of our own limitations. As far as I can see, that comes down to conditions, and I count eight. When all eight are in a person’s life, finishing the road is a realistic plan. When one of them is missing, the road gets much longer.

The old traditions built every one of these conditions long before us, and they can be found or built in many places. Read them as a list to hold your own life against. The order goes from what only you can supply, through the daily work, to what comes from other people.

1. Your wish, and your yes

Think of a time you really decided something: a move, a new job, a person. Before it, every day was a small debate. After it, the days started to line up, and things you had been putting off began to happen almost on their own.

That yes stands on a wish, and the wish is the one condition nobody else can supply. Part two said the whole handed the steering to each point of view, so nobody can start the return for you. A mentor can point and friends can lift you, and none of them can want it for you.

People come for all kinds of reasons, and the reasons change along the way. Some come with questions about why we exist. Some want to stay calm when the inbox explodes. Some simply can’t sleep and want to feel better, which is reason enough to start. Which one is yours matters less than that it is really yours.

  • From the outside:
  • For driven people it is usually a crash: burnout, a body that stops cooperating, or a depression that lands right after the goal is reached.
  • Something happens to someone close, a relative in the hospital or a partner’s betrayal, and values shift.
  • The founder in Part one kept building up tension until the old way stopped working, and out of options went on a retreat.
  • For one friend of mine it was a single talk. After years of trying everything spiritual, my friend decided it was all nonsense, went into business, and within months felt empty. One talk by my teacher was enough to sense that the point was the middle of Part one’s chart, letting go and action together.
  • From the inside:
  • Some feel an emptiness that results can’t reach. For me it showed up whenever I had time to myself, and it made the weekends the worst part of the week.
  • Others have nothing to complain about, and a quiet question shows up anyway: is this all there is?
  • Some reach a plateau, years of calm and comfort, until the calm itself starts to feel pointless.
  • And some only feel that something is off, with no event to point at.

How you come to this says nothing about how ready you are for it. One of the students whose example I learn from most started because of trouble sleeping, and the idea itself met years of resistance. Today that student is one of the people I look up to most.

A wish is the wanting, and a decision is the yes that goes with it, the moment the wanting becomes a direction. It can be quiet, a sentence you say to yourself on an ordinary evening. After it you no longer bargain with yourself every morning about whether today counts, and the energy the bargaining took goes into living. Many traditions call this the greatest victory there is, the one over yourself.

From the traditions · 5 passagesThe greatest victoryIn the Dhammapada the supreme conqueror is the one who conquers a single person, himself, and the verse sets that above…
  • In the Dhammapada the supreme conqueror is the one who conquers a single person, himself, and the verse sets that above winning a battle against a million. Another verse says no god, and not even Mara, the tempter of the Buddhist stories, can undo the victory of someone who has tamed themselves (103 to 105).
  • In Pirkei Avot Ben Zoma asks who is mighty, and answers: the one who subdues his inclination. He backs it with Proverbs, which says it is better to have self-control than to conquer a city (4:1, quoting Proverbs 16:32).
  • The Jain Uttaradhyayana has almost the same verse as the Dhammapada. King Nami, who has given up his throne, answers Indra, king of the gods, that a man may conquer thousands and thousands of valiant foes, and greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself (chapter 9).
  • The Daodejing says that whoever overcomes others is strong, and whoever overcomes himself is mighty (33).
  • A hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari reports the Prophet saying that the strong one is the one who controls himself while in anger, which he sets against overcoming people by strength (6114).

The wish has its days, a lift on some and a slump on others, and a low day ranks nobody. It’s a day. The wish also grows. A goal you love feeds it, and a bigger wish crowds out smaller ones, so you don’t have to fight the small ones one at a time. What drains it fastest is feeling sorry for yourself, and a half-wish that changes its mind every Tuesday about how much it’s willing to give.

You can see from the outside how strong a wish is. A project that never gets built, a plan that stays a plan: the wish behind it was probably weaker than it felt. I still have a few of those myself. So ask yourself how much you want this, and take the answer as information. Then do something concrete, and say yes again tomorrow.

Try itYour sentence, and the yes you already knowWrite one sentence that starts “I want this because”, in your own words. Most people can’t finish it on the first try,…
  • Write one sentence that starts “I want this because”, in your own words. Most people can’t finish it on the first try, and that’s fine: keep the half sentence somewhere you’ll see it and let it fill in over the coming weeks, because finding it is part of the work. In a month, read it again and ask whether it still sounds like you or like something you read somewhere.
  • Then remember a time you really decided something. Write down what changed in the days after, and ask what the same yes would sound like here.

2. A goal, and a reason

Anything that moves needs two things, energy and direction. A car with plenty of fuel and no steering is a bomb with a radio. A car with a perfect map and no fuel is a very well informed parked car. The wish is the fuel, and a goal is the steering.

Inner growth is easy to fake, even to yourself. A calm sit feels like progress whether or not anything has changed. So you set outer goals and judge the inner work by them. A good goal takes weeks or months, ends in a clear yes or no, and lives in the world where other people can see it. Plan the family trip for the summer. Finish a draft of an essay. A goal you care about also pulls effort out of you that plain willpower never would.

Behind each goal sits a reason, and the reason is what keeps you going after the first excitement wears off. The goal can be small as long as the reason is bigger than you, which is the pull from Part one. You plan the family trip because you want your kids to grow up feeling that home is a warm place. You finish the draft because someone out there may need what is in it.

Results are how you check the work, and they are never the aim. If you start chasing them for their own sake, you’ve rebuilt the Driven room with better vocabulary. Without any goal, the inner work has nothing to be checked against, and it can feel like growth for years while nothing moves.

Try itWhat is behind the goalTake a goal you have now, or something you’d like to be different in a month. Under it write “because”, and finish the…
  • Take a goal you have now, or something you’d like to be different in a month. Under it write “because”, and finish the sentence. Then ask “and why do I want that?” a few times over, until you reach something that feels true and a little surprising. Sometimes that is the reason to keep in front of you, and sometimes it shows the goal was standing in for something else.

3. Discipline

Part two’s step 2 ended on a pull that only goes one way: do nothing in particular and you end up a little more separate every year. A pull that never takes a day off can only be matched by something that doesn’t take one either, and that is discipline, doing what you decided to do after the mood for it has passed.

Keep it light, and keep it yours: a demand you make on yourself, in a friendly tone. Gritting your teeth through practice adds tension, which is the very thing practice is there to remove. Discipline that runs on pride runs down like a battery, and what keeps it going for years is that you start to enjoy it. Five minutes every day is a frame you can keep, and a frame you keep teaches your mind that you can trust your own word. When a day slips, pick it up the next day without a speech about it.

It grows from the outside in. A fixed time and a meeting on the calendar are the outside, and you can keep them even on a day when you don’t feel like it. After a while you see what they give you, and the keeping starts to come from you.

For me it looks like this. I’ve dedicated my life to the spiritual path, and it’s my focus every day. That’s why I do the three meditations a day, every day, and why I can’t just stop taking part in something because I don’t feel like it that day. Another thing I’m disciplined about is alcohol, cigarettes and drugs: a hundred percent no to all of them. It’s a way of life, and discipline is a big part of it. That is my line, though, the line of the life I chose. What the method asks changes with the stage you’re at, and at the start it asks much less: five minutes a day that you keep, and a decision you keep after the mood for it has passed.

Without discipline, direction follows mood, and mood brings back the swing from Part one.

Try itOne promise, and the bargainEach morning for ten days, promise yourself one small thing, then do it and mark it done. If the ten days hold, go for…
  • Each morning for ten days, promise yourself one small thing, then do it and mark it done. If the ten days hold, go for a month. Keep it small enough to feel almost silly. What you’re building is a word to yourself that holds.
  • When you catch yourself negotiating, “I’ll start tomorrow” or “today doesn’t really count”, write down the exact sentence your mind used. After two weeks, read the list. Most minds turn out to have three or four favorite lines, and mine are not very original.

4. Work with attention

Part two said the belief that I’m separate is held in the body as tension, and that suffering is the grip we add around the pain. That puts the most direct work in the body, with attention, and it has three jobs.

The first is to be here, in the body and in this room, with no detour through later. This is the plainest meaning of BE from Part one: how much of you is actually present for what is happening right now. It sounds trivial until you try to keep it for a minute.

The second job is letting go of the tension you’ve already built up. Tension sits in the body as clamps, and you can find them: a jaw, a shoulder, the muscles around the eyes. Attention is the tool. You put it on the clamp and keep it there, like a flashlight held on one spot, and you don’t push, because pushing is more tension. Eventually the clamp goes.

Stillness is the simplest form of this, and you can start tonight, alone. Close your eyes and feel the life in your body. Put your attention on that sensation, and learn to gain pleasure from it. That’s the whole instruction. Start with five minutes, and sit longer or more often whenever you like. It’s also where to look for the border of you that Part two asked about. You don’t need to agree with anything in this book to try it, and five minutes will tell you more about it than I can.

The third job is not to build new tension, and it’s the one people forget. If you clear your shoulders in the morning and spend the day clenching, you’ve bailed a boat with the hole still open. It mostly comes down to small choices, like saying the sentence now instead of carrying it, and not rehearsing the argument in the shower. Each of them is a clamp that never forms.

Underneath all three there’s a plain skill: seeing your own mind at work. Most of what you do in a day gets triggered. Someone says a thing, something fires, and you’ve answered before you’ve decided to. One tool I use: when something fires, notice where it lands in the body, the jaw or the stomach, and look at it there for a moment before you answer. Over time, things that usually arrive glued together start to come apart: what happened and the story I tell myself about it, a real danger and an old fear, what I actually want and what someone else expects of me.

The daily sit is the smallest part of the practice. What it changes is the rest of the day, at the checkout and in the argument. Your family, your work, your health and the people you live with are where the practice really happens, and where you find out whether anything has changed. Some weeks the five minutes feel flat and nothing seems to happen. Those dry stretches end, and they end for the people who kept sitting through them. Without the practice, you clear the clamps in the morning and build new ones all day, and the drift does the rest.

Try itFive minutes, then two columnsOnce a day, do the five minutes of stillness from this section: close your eyes, feel the life in your body, put your…
  • Once a day, do the five minutes of stillness from this section: close your eyes, feel the life in your body, put your attention on that sensation, and learn to enjoy it. If that’s all you do, it’s plenty.
  • If you’d like to take it further, open your eyes afterwards and think of one hard moment from the day. Write two columns: what happened, and the story you told yourself about it. After a few minutes of stillness the two are much easier to tell apart.

Prayer is the same attention pointed somewhere else. In stillness it rests on the body, and in prayer it turns toward the whole, which here means learning to talk with life itself. One way in is a question: how would you talk if you met God, or whatever you call the source? You would take off the masks and speak as you are. Asking is part of it, for help and for other people, and the asking tends to turn toward giving. You can tell the conversation is real the way you can tell a person is really listening to you. And prayers alone don’t bring rain. Someone has to dig the ditches for it.

5. Giving

Giving here means putting effort into the good of other people, or of something bigger than you, without counting what comes back. An hour of real attention counts. So does using your skills on someone else’s problem. This is where the reason bigger than you from section 2 turns into something you do.

It starts with the people closest to you, which is also where the cloth from Part two rises first. By the logic of the fingers, helping you is helping me, as a matter of plumbing, with niceness left out of it. The circle widens from there, one ring at a time, toward the people who are hard for you.

Give without conditions. Don’t demand thanks, recognition or a result in return, and don’t hold the person hostage to the goal behind it, however big that goal is. While you’re busy with something larger than yourself, there is less of you to guard, and that speeds up the one thing almost nothing else touches, the slow change in the ego. My one-line summary of the whole road, for anyone in a hurry: get over yourself. What wears away is the part of us that keeps defending, and what is left is what we bring into the world. If you’re the person who already gives until nothing is left, it works the other way round for you: rest, a no and a boundary are your hard step, because an empty tank helps nobody.

Without giving, inner work can go in a circle, polishing the same stone. Giving sends the energy somewhere, the way the mill in Part two lets the river through, and other people can tell you whether it helped. Part one’s two questions work here too: would you still do it if nobody ever found out, and does it make you gentler with the person in front of you?

Try itOne ring further outDraw rings on a page for the people around you, from the closest out to the people who are hard for you, and remember…
  • Draw rings on a page for the people around you, from the closest out to the people who are hard for you, and remember that the hardest one may be at home. This week, do one small thing for someone one ring further out than you usually go. When that starts to feel easy, go one more ring.

6. Mirror work and ego work

Part two said that what happens to you works like a mirror. A colleague’s tone, a partner’s silence, a stranger in traffic: whatever hooks me points at something in me that still needs work. Here that becomes a working rule: I take responsibility for what happens around me. That never means letting people hurt you.

It sounds like an insult and it’s closer to a lever. I can’t control the world, and I can control myself, so the only place I can ever change a result is on my side of it. I don’t need to work out whose fault it was. I go first, I say sorry first, and I check what shifted.

When someone harms you, you react, and sometimes the right reaction is to walk away or even fight back. The mirror asks about the state the reaction comes from: do I do it because I see it as the best course, or because hate or fear is deciding for me? The same step away can be a clear choice or a flinch, and from outside they look identical. There’s no beating yourself up in it either, because guilt is another clamp. The mirror is something you hold up to yourself, and holding it up to someone else is a way of skipping the work.

The clearest case I have is someone on the path I worked with very closely. This person was very stubborn and very emotional, and once the emotion rose, nothing I said landed. In that period I was learning that if we make the internal decision to change our relationship to another person or to a situation, we can change it almost at once, and that this internal click is one of the biggest keys to growing. I decided to take it seriously.

Then we had an enormous fight. I got crazy angry because once again the other person would not understand what I was trying to say, and I said we should stop and go our separate ways for a moment. I sat in another room, livid, asking how I could change my relationship to this and how I could let it go. It was close to prayer. It felt like the lever on a railway track in the cartoons, the one that is supposed to switch the rails and is so rusty it barely moves, except that this lever was inside me. Moving it was one of the hardest things I have done. After about ten minutes it moved.

I went to find the other person to apologize. Calming down quickly after a fight had never been this person’s way, and the anger could last for days. But by the time I had said my first word, I could see this person was already in a completely different state. So I learned that the internal click is possible and that it works. I did it a few more times over the years, it got easier each time, and it was never that hard again. I haven’t been that angry in many years.

That is what repair looks like: quick and light. A person who says “I’m sorry, that was me” the same afternoon, without an essay, has removed a clamp, and one who leaves it for a week has added one. The usual suspects behind the hook are pride, offense and the habit of judging, and the work on them has a name, ego work, which is a grand title for noticing them every time they show up. You can’t see which hooks you have until someone leans on them, and the people already in your life do that every day.

  • Pick one conflict that is still open, big or small. This week, take the first step. Say sorry for your part in one sentence, without an essay and without a “but” at the end, and then watch what shifts.
  • The hardest part is to really mean it, and meaning it takes the lever moving inside you. If it won’t move yet, ask yourself how you could change your relationship to this, and go when you can say it and mean it. Give it time, and don’t let the time turn into years.

7. Friends on the path

Part two said the world is built to be convincing, and that a cell stays well only while it keeps listening to its neighbors. So what you are surrounded by matters as much as what you do on your own, and the strongest help you can have is a few people in your life who are working on the same thing. On your own the wish dies down faster.

This matters more now than it used to. Most of us grew up in a world that is always offering something new and telling us that a good life means getting what you want, and even a cave doesn’t quite undo that, because you carry it in with you. A state can change in a day. A way of living changes when the people around you keep pointing the same way, week after week, which is what the Buddhist sangha, the Hindu ashram and the Christian monastery were all built for.

From the traditions · 4 passagesBuilt long before usThe Buddhist refuges are three: I take refuge in the Buddha, I take refuge in the Teaching, I take refuge in the…
  • The Buddhist refuges are three: I take refuge in the Buddha, I take refuge in the Teaching, I take refuge in the Sangha, the community (Khuddakapatha 1).
  • In Pirkei Avot Joshua ben Perahiah says: appoint for yourself a teacher, and acquire for yourself a companion (1:6).
  • The Vivekachudamani, traditionally attributed to Shankara, names three things that are rare and come by the grace of God: a human birth, the longing for liberation, and the shelter of a great soul (3).
  • The Rule of Benedict says a hermit goes out alone only after long probation in a monastery, having learned with the help of many brethren. Of the monks who live without a shepherd it says that their law is the desire for self-gratification: whatever enters their mind or appeals to them, that they call holy (chapter 1).

Two friends doing this together are the smallest community, and it grows from there. There are many communities on this road, and the one to look for is the one where you feel at home and find yourself growing. Among people who want the same thing, the other conditions in this part happen together and in company.

Friends on the path hold you up in the hard moments. When your mood falls through the floor, your friend’s hasn’t. I tend to be the one who holds others, but I have my moments too, most often when I’ve set myself a goal and haven’t reached it, or a deadline is close and the task looks unmanageable. I used to get very hard on myself then, and my close friends helped me come back to myself, calm down and let it go.

None of this replaces the people you already have. Most of us do it alongside family, old friends and a job, and those people tend to notice the change before we do.

It comes with conditions. The people have to be aimed at the same thing, whatever words each of them uses for it. The group has to settle its conflicts quickly, by talking them through without attacking anyone, and keep the agreements it makes. When two people disagree, the way out is often a third option: keep talking until you find one you both like better than either first idea. A quarrel reverses the effect: the same closeness that lifts a community weighs it down when there is a wall between two of its people. And the people have to want it. A community multiplies whatever is put into it, and anything times nothing is still nothing, a fact your math teacher would confirm.

When your own spark drops, lighting up a friend gets some of it back. Ananda, the Buddha’s attendant, once said that good friends are half of the spiritual life, and the Buddha answered that they are the whole of it.

  • Is there one person in your life who wants something like what you want? Suggest a weekly walk or call where each of you says what you’re working on and how the week went. If nobody in your life wants this yet, do it on your own for a while, and ask a friend who doesn’t share it to check in with you anyway.
  • If you both feel like it, make one small agreement together, for example five minutes of stillness every day for a month, and check in once a week on how you kept it.

8. Someone further along

Part two said that a limit you are inside of rarely shows up as a limit. That is true of every one of us, teachers included, the way everyone hears your accent except you. A person a few steps ahead can often name in one sentence a habit you would take years to find alone, and you can check it against your own life the same week.

A few steps is the useful distance, close enough that you can see how they got there and far enough that you want what they have. You have probably felt a small version of this already: an afternoon with the calmest person you know, and you leave a little calmer without having done anything.

It works the way a blacksmith learns. There is no syllabus. The apprentice stands next to the smith for years, watching how long the iron stays in the fire and what the smith does when a piece comes out wrong, and one day can do things nobody ever explained. Most of what you are learning here is like that: how to stay steady when a plan falls apart, how to say sorry first without making a ceremony of it. You pick it up the way you pick up an accent from a roommate, without trying, and it is a big part of why people mature much faster with a mentor than alone. Horse trainers know a version of this: a young, nervous horse runs steadier behind an older, calmer one.

Try itAsk for one sentenceAsk someone you trust: “What’s one habit of mine you think I don’t see?” Then listen and say thank you, without…
  • Ask someone you trust: “What’s one habit of mine you think I don’t see?” Then listen and say thank you, without defending it. That same week, check what they said against your own life and see how often it shows up.

A community has energy, and it can still be going in a circle. A teacher, someone who has walked much more of the road, can see where it is growing and where it is stuck. A teacher can’t walk the path for you or create your wish to walk it, which stays yours. What a teacher can do is point out where you have drifted, give you something to try on Monday, and on the hard days help you remember what you came for. A good teacher also has a steadiness that spreads, the way a calm guide on a mountain path steadies everyone walking behind.

A note on community and the teacher

The way to judge a teacher and a group is your own experience. Does this person know more than I do? Does this group actually live the values it talks about? Can I say I disagree and still be welcome, could I leave without a fight, and do I grow when I listen to this teacher? People do leave our community, too. Some go on along their path in new ways, and some come back after a while, and we stay friends whatever they chose.

There will be moments, plenty of them, when what you think is very different from what you’re offered to try. That discomfort comes to everyone who walks this road, whoever the teacher is, and with no teacher at all. For me there is one way to tell growth from something being wrong. The discomfort of growth comes together with an emotion in my body, so I decide nothing while the emotion is still there. I live through it first, and then I look at the disagreement again with a clear head. Usually most of it has gone with the emotion. When something was still there, I said so, and people took it up. Whether that’s a reason to stay or to leave is up to you.

The hardest thing to convey is the inner decision that this is a person whose view of me I trust, and that comes only through your own experience. And you don’t have to have a teacher at all. I studied on my own for years before I found mine, and it’s the slower road, but it is a road. If there’s nobody around you for this yet, that’s how most of us started, me included. It’s also what the form at the end of this book is for: a reader with nobody to compare notes with can at least write to me.

The cloth and the world

Start with something small. You want something, a new phone, a trip, a better job, and you get it. For a week it shines, then it’s just there, and the wanting has already moved on to the next thing. That’s the need setting from Part two, and the 21st century turned up everything that feeds it.

Now picture the same need with all of us behind it, a whole civilization reaching for the next thing. That reaching built a lot of what I’m grateful for, including the laptop I’m writing this on. It also runs into limits. The climate is changing, and the machines we build are getting more capable faster than we can agree on what to do with them. A lot of what makes these problems hard is that everyone guards their own corner, even when that leaves all of us worse off, which is the wall from Part two at a bigger scale.

This is usually the point where a book like this one asks you to save the world, and I’m going to do the opposite. What this work does for the world is what the cloth from Part two does. Lift one jewel and the ones next to it rise right after, and it starts with the people around you.

It’s the least glamorous way to help I know of. From the outside nothing was saved. Your partner had an easier evening. A colleague stopped bracing before meetings with you. A quarrel in your family ended a day sooner than it used to. The reason to do this work stays the wish from section 1, and what it does for the world comes by itself.

In practice, no magic happens. Part one called tension a tax on seeing solutions, and people who carry less of it see more of the shelf. They listen longer, say sorry first, and can stay at a table with someone they disagree with, which is what hard shared problems ask of us. None of it replaces the scientists, the engineers, or anyone who votes and negotiates. It changes the people who do those things. A few steady people in a family or a team change the weather in the room, and the first room is the one you live in.

What you can check

Start with a count: which of the eight conditions does your life have now, and which one is missing? After a few months of practice, ask: do you produce the thing you said you would? Does anything outside you change, and if it doesn’t, why not? How fast do you say sorry first now? Over the last year, are you more fulfilled, or more bleak? Put the answers on the chart from Part one, and ask again a month later. Look at the people a few steps ahead of you, too: do they live the way they talk? And do the people closest to you leave an evening with you a bit lighter or a bit flatter?

And one question for the road: who in your life could tell you honestly how you have changed?

That’s the whole road as I see it, from a chart about getting things done to the bigger map past it. I’m walking it in this life and I mean to finish it. How far you go is your own yes, and nobody can say it for you.
