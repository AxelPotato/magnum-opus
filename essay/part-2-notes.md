## Research behind this version

Same caveat as before: Crossref, doi.org and the publisher pages were blocked from this session, so the checks ran on search summaries that name their sources, plus background knowledge. No number in the essay is invented, and a few that I computed myself are marked below. One person with a normal connection should open the DOIs before this goes public.

Philosophy, physics and computing (checked, and the text changed accordingly):
- Chalmers 1995 is as cited. "Consciousness is a product of the mind" became "depends on that activity", because "produced by" is itself a theory (identity and filter views are rivals). "Materialism and determinism" are not a necessary pair, so the text says quantum mechanics is probabilistic in its standard form and that some interpretations (Bohmian mechanics, many worlds) keep the picture deterministic.
- Schrödinger. The wording "the over-all number of minds is just one" is verified through secondary sources (Mind and Matter, chapter 4, "The Arithmetical Paradox: The Oneness of Mind", the Tarner Lectures of 1956, published 1958), and the 1944 epilogue of What Is Life? says the multiplicity of minds is only apparent and calls this the doctrine of the Upanishads. He himself said that at that point he was taking religion and not science, so the essay says so. The widely circulated "the total number of minds in the universe is one" is a paraphrase and is not quoted. Check the Cambridge text before quoting anything.
- Spinoza and Einstein. One substance (Ethics, Part I). Einstein's 1929 telegram says "I believe in Spinoza's God", and he meant the lawful order of nature. "God or Nature" is from Part IV, so the essay does not give a part for it. Neither man held the view the essay uses, and the text says so.
- Hoffman. The citation is verified (Hoffman, Singh and Prakash 2015, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review). The mild form (perception is tuned for usefulness) is accepted; the strong form is a minority view. The text says "Hoffman argues" and nothing stronger. Kastrup is the nearest to your axiom (one mind, individual lives as dissociated parts) and so is in; Goff is out, because panpsychism says many small subjects and not one.
- Computing. Process isolation holds "by default" (the kernel and root can read anything, and Meltdown and Spectre, January 2018, leaked across the walls), which suits the metaphor. Virtualization, shared memory and sandbox escape are as described. I found no canonical source for "many selves, one machine", so the essay presents it as yours.
- Space. At the height of the space station (about 400 km), circular orbit speed is about 7.7 km/s and escape speed about 10.8, so escape is about 41 percent faster (the square root of two) at any height; from the surface it is 11.2. Gravity at that height is about 88 percent of the surface value. The essay no longer pairs 7.8 with 11.2, which gives 44 percent and was a mixed comparison. Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause on 25 August 2012 and is still inside the Sun's gravitational reach. The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across and the observable universe about 93 billion, which the essay rounds to "about 90 billion as far as we can see, and perhaps larger". The stage-by-stage escape speeds (about 17 km/s from Earth's surface to leave the Sun, something like 500 to leave the Milky Way from our position) are my own calculation plus Gaia-based estimates, and I rounded them. Kerbal Space Program (Squad, 2011) and The IT Crowd (Channel 4, 2006 to 2013) are as described.
- The several-lives step. The strongest honest objection is that the premise (the aim is unity) is unargued, so the essay says so, and also says that nothing in mainstream science supports survival after death. Stevenson's roughly 2,500 to 3,000 cases did not convince the scientific community. Buddhism teaches rebirth without a permanent soul, and the text keeps that distinction.

Carried over from variant A (checked then): Botvinick and Cohen 1998 (rubber hand; ten subjects), Kalckert and Ehrsson 2017 (onset typically within a minute), Lush and colleagues 2020 (the illusion tracks suggestibility), Lenggenhager and colleagues 2007, Metzinger 2003 and Blanke and Metzinger 2009, and Cialdini and colleagues 1997 (disputed by Batson and colleagues and by Maner and colleagues).

Religion and comparative religion: checked, and the text changed accordingly. Search summaries only, and several snippets came from Theosophical or devotional sites.
- Theosophy. Founded in New York on 17 November 1875 by Blavatsky, Olcott, Judge and others. Its 1875 statement had one aim, to gather knowledge of the laws governing the universe; the comparative study of religion is in the later wording, current since 1896. The motto "There is no religion higher than truth" was adopted around 1880. Hodgson 1885 and Harrison 1986 are as described; what the SPR itself said in 1986 comes from partisan-leaning sources, so the essay says only that the SPR's own journal published Harrison. Theosophy was one main route for karma and reincarnation to English readers, but Allan Kardec's Spiritism (1857) came earlier, so "did more than anyone" was dropped.
- Perennial philosophy. Steuco (1540) used the phrase before Leibniz (a letter of 26 August 1714); Huxley wrongly says Leibniz coined it. Huxley's three-part definition is verified only through secondary quotations; the essay paraphrases it. Check it against the printed Foreword before quoting. William James's "eternal unanimity" is paraphrased.
- The critics. Katz 1978 ("there are NO pure experiences" is his wording), Proudfoot 1985, Sharf, Zaehner; the replies from Forman and Hick. Fair summary in the essay: many scholars now reject strong perennialism, a modest family-resemblance version survives.
- Which traditions fit the axiom. The first round said "fits badly with Theravada, Jainism and the mainstream Western religions". That judged them by their public teaching, and you corrected it on 2026-10-07. It is withdrawn. The second round looked at inner layers and texts (below).
- Corrections made. Buddhist emptiness is not "the One" (Heart Sutra paragraph). Maya and mithya mean "dependent", and "illusion" translates them badly. Zhuangzi adds that there must be some distinction. Origen: the sixth century condemnations were about pre-existence and the final restoration of all, not reincarnation, so the popular claim that the 553 council banned reincarnation is not used. Tanasukh was rejected by mainstream Muslim thought, accepted by minority groups. Buddhist schools split on a state between lives, so "the soul rests between lives" is not said of Buddhism in general. Karma: nobody administers it in the Buddhist account, God does in theistic Hindu schools, and Advaita places it in the world of appearance. Golden rule texts mostly rest on reciprocity or empathy; only a few, such as the Isha Upanishad, rest on "the other is yourself". The Isha verb means "recoils from", so the essay says "recoils from nothing" and not "feels no hatred". Paul's "sow and reap" is accountability under God, not rebirth, and the essay says so. "Born again" and "born from above" are the two meanings of the Greek word, and the "twice-born" are those who received the initiation that counts as a second birth. Eckhart was investigated for heresy; I did not name a sermon because I could not confirm the number.
- Things I could not confirm, so the essay does not give them: the Mahabharata verse number (editions differ: 13.113.8 or 13.114.8, and the older "5.1517" belongs to another numbering), Ramakrishna's slogan "yato mat tato path" (the essay uses the lake parable instead), the 8 May 1986 SPR press release, Matthew 25:40's wording in your preferred translation, "measure for measure" (middah keneged middah), which is standard but was not part of this check, and Quran 17:7's last clause, whose English varies by translation ("it is to yourselves" is one common rendering).
- Sources I would open before this goes public: Huxley's Foreword, Schuon, the Hodgson Report and Harrison's paper (Journal of the SPR 53, pp. 286-310), Katz p. 26, Proudfoot, Forman, and the Upanishad translations (Olivelle is the usual one).


Second round, after your correction (2026-10-07): the inner layers of Judaism, Islam, Christianity, Buddhism and Jainism. Three research passes, search summaries only, and the shared search budget ran out in all three, so some items are marked as unconfirmed in the lists below.

What the research supports (all of it checked as far as the search summaries allow; 2.2.0 uses the positive parts as examples and leaves the objections out of the essay, see below):
- Judaism. Chabad Hasidism (acosmism: from God's side the world has no separate existence; "ein od milvado"), the Zohar line (Torah, the Holy One and Israel are one), Cordovero's panentheism, Luria's one soul of Adam that divides into sparks, tikkun, devekut, gilgul, bittul and hishtavut. Limits: the unity ethic is mostly about Jewish souls; separation is a real catastrophe or descent, not an illusion; Scholem held devekut is attachment and not union (Idel disagrees); the Mitnagdim and the Maimonidean line stress transcendence.
- Islam. Quran 2:115, 57:3-4, 2:156, 7:172, the nawafil hadith, Ibn Arabi's "He and not He", Ghazali's Mishkat, fana and baqa, Mulla Sadra's continued development after death. Limits: classical commentators read the nearness verses as nearness of knowledge and power; the Quran refuses a return to this world (23:99 to 100); Ibn Taymiyya and the Salafi line reject wahdat al-wujud; "hidden treasure" and "die before you die" are not accepted as hadith.
- Christianity. Strongest: the return (1 Cor 15:28, Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Eriugena, theosis), the one body and its ethic (Paul, Augustine, Acts 9:4, Anthony, Silouan), letting go (kenosis, Eckhart, John of the Cross, hesychasm). Weakest: "one whole that experiences separation as its own self-experience". Every inner voice keeps union by participation and not identity, and the Church policed the strongest language (Eriugena 1225, Porete 1310, Eckhart 1329).
- Buddhism. The same structure of problem and way out: the constructed self, ignorance, staged path, rebirth without a fixed soul, "everyone has been your mother", ethics from "I am like you", letting go (the strongest match with your Part one). Limits, kept here for you and not in the essay since 2.2.0: the Buddha lists "everything is a oneness" among four extremes in SN 12.48 and sets them aside; Theravada has no whole and no ground; interdependence is not a One; only some Mahayana strands (Huayan, buddha-nature, Dzogchen) come close, and other Buddhists dispute them.
- Jainism. Strong on the veil (karma over the soul's own knowledge), staged growth (fourteen gunasthanas, with falling back), ahimsa from the equality of souls, forgiveness, samayika and kayotsarga. Not on the one whole: the Sutrakritanga refutes the single soul, liberated souls stay many, and some souls are said never to be liberated. The Acharanga line about the one you intend to kill exists in two renderings (a modern commentary reads it as the oneness of killer and killed; Jacobi's older version only equates the experience), so the essay says it both ways.

What the research did not support, so the essay does not say it: that Jainism or Theravada say "one whole"; that the golden-rule texts mostly rest on "the other is you" (most rest on reciprocity or empathy); that Matthew 25:40 simply means all people (many scholars read "the least of these" as disciples); that Origen taught reincarnation; that the 553 council banned it; that Eckhart was condemned for the "eye" saying; that the Acharanga reads "none other than you" in Jacobi's translation.
